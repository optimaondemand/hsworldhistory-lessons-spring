🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

In 1870 Europeans held only the edges of Africa. By 1914 they ruled almost all of it, and Britain governed an Indian empire of some three hundred million people. Today you will see how this happened so fast, and what each side did to shape it.

THE NEW IMPERIALISM

Europe had built empires before, in the Americas and in Asia. The telegraph let a government in London give orders overseas within hours. And medicine and weapons changed the odds. Motive mattered as well. Merchants wanted markets and raw materials. Governments wanted naval bases and prestige, especially after Germany and Italy appeared as new powers. Missionaries and humanitarians wanted to spread the Gospel and end the slave trade that still operated in East Africa. In this week's discussion you will weigh which of these motives counted most. As you read, keep a list of the evidence for each.

THE SCRAMBLE FOR AFRICA

In 1870 most European posts in Africa were on the coast. The French held Algeria. Dutch-descended farmers, called Boers, and the British lived in the south. Then explorers such as David Livingstone and Henry Morton Stanley opened the interior to Europe's imagination. King Leopold II of Belgium sent Stanley back to the Congo basin, and in 1884 Leopold claimed it through a private association.

Fearing a quarrel, the great powers met in Berlin in 1884 and 1885 at the invitation of Bismarck. No Africans took part. The conference decided that a power could claim land if it truly occupied it, and it recognized Leopold's Congo Free State. After that, the rush was on. Within twenty years the powers, following lines drawn on maps in Europe, had divided the continent. These lines often cut across peoples and joined enemies in a single colony. Leopold's rule in the Congo was especially harsh. Agents forced villagers to collect rubber under threat of violence, and reports by missionaries and a British shipping clerk, Edmund Morel, produced a public outcry. In 1908 the Belgian government took the Congo from the king.

AFRICAN RESISTANCE AND BRITISH AMBITION

Africans did not simply give way. At Isandlwana in 1879 a Zulu army wiped out a British column, though the British won the war that year. In Sudan, a religious leader called the Mahdi overthrew Egyptian rule and killed General Charles Gordon at Khartoum in 1885. Britain avenged him in 1898, when Lord Kitchener's army, using Maxim guns, destroyed the Mahdist army at Omdurman while losing few men. Soon afterward, a French expedition reached the Nile at Fashoda, and Britain and France came close to war before France backed down.

Britain had occupied Egypt in 1882 to protect the Suez Canal, making Egypt a protectoratepruh-TEK-ter-itA land that keeps its own rulers on paper but is controlled in foreign affairs by a stronger nation. See the glossary box. in everything but name. In the south, Cecil Rhodes dreamed of a railway from the Cape to Cairo. His agents pushed north, and a quarrel over the gold of the Transvaal led to the Boer War of 1899 to 1902, which Britain won only at great cost. The most striking African success was Ethiopia. Its emperor, Menelik II, bought modern rifles and trained his army, and in 1896 he crushed an Italian invasion at Adwa. Ethiopia and Liberia were the only African lands to keep their independence.

Not every African ruler chose to fight. Some signed treaties, hoping to use European help against their rivals, and some, such as the kings of Buganda, played one European power against another. Many Africans served the new governments as soldiers, clerks, interpreters, and guides, and Africans did most of the actual work of building the roads and railways.

FROM COMPANY TO CROWN

India's story is older. The British East India Company, a trading firm with its own army, came to dominate Bengal after Robert Clive's victory at Plassey in 1757. Over the next century it took more territory, partly by war and partly by treaty with Indian princes who were often at odds with one another. Most of its soldiers were Indians, called sepoysSEE-poyA word from the Persian and Urdu languages for an Indian soldier serving in a European-led army. See the glossary box., commanded by British officers.

In 1857 many sepoys in northern India rose in revolt. A rumor spread that new rifle cartridges were greased with cow and pig fat, an insult to Hindus and Muslims alike. But the real causes ran deeper: resentment at the annexation of Indian states, fear for religion and custom, and anger at British officers. The rebels seized Delhi and Lucknow. After more than a year of bitter fighting, the British won, helped by Indian troops from the Punjab who stayed loyal. Parliament abolished the Company in 1858 and put India under the Crown. RULING INDIA

A viceroy in Calcutta, answering to London, led the government. A small Indian Civil Service of a few thousand Britons ran the districts, aided by a large Indian staff and by an army of Indians. About a third of India was ruled indirectly through hundreds of princes. The British built railways, irrigation canals, telegraph lines, schools, and courts. Thousands of Indians studied English and the law, and some of them began to ask why Britons spoke so well of liberty yet kept it from India. In 1885 educated Indians founded the Indian National Congress, which at first asked only for a greater share in the government.

Yet the Raj also faced hard limits. Famines in the 1870s and 1890s killed millions, and the government's relief was slow and arguable. Indian cotton makers suffered from cheap factory cloth shipped from Lancashire. Still, the Raj kept order in a land that had often known disorder, and it ruled with remarkably few British officials. By 1900 India had more than twenty-five thousand miles of railway, and Indian soldiers and Indian money also served Britain's wars in Asia and Africa. Whether that was a help or a harm to India, its people debated for generations.
