🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

China and Japan were the two great powers of East Asia. In the 1800s both were forced open by Western gunboats. Within fifty years, one was a struggling empire and the other was a rising power. Today you will see how that happened, and what made the difference.

CHINA AND THE CANTON TRADE

China was ruled by the Qing dynasty, a line of emperors from Manchuria who had governed since 1644. Europeans prized Chinese tea, silk, and porcelain. Since 1757 the Qing had allowed them to trade at only one port, Canton, through a few licensed Chinese merchants. China, in turn, wanted little that Europe made. In 1793 Lord Macartney came to Beijing to ask for freer trade. The Qianlong Emperor politely refused. In his letter to King George III he wrote, in the English translation of 1914, "we possess all things. I set no value on objects strange or ingenious, and have no use for your country's manufactures."

Trade at Canton was tightly controlled. Foreign merchants could live only in a strip of warehouses and offices on the river bank outside the city walls, and only during the trading season. They could not bring their wives, and they were forbidden to hire Chinese teachers to learn the language. Each foreign firm had to deal through a licensed Chinese merchant, who answered to the government for the foreigners' behavior. To Europeans this looked like a trap. To the Qing it looked like a sensible way to deal with strangers who had no place in the empire's order.

The result was that British silver flowed to China to pay for tea. The British traders looked for something the Chinese would buy, and they found it in opium, grown in British India and smuggled into China. The East India Company grew the opium in Bengal and sold it at auction in Calcutta to private traders, who carried it to the China coast and sold it to Chinese smugglers. That way the Company could say it never sold opium in China. By the 1830s tens of thousands of chests arrived each year. Addiction spread, and silver began to flow the other way, out of China. The Daoguang Emperor named an honest official, Lin Zexu, to stop the trade. In 1839 Lin seized and destroyed more than twenty thousand chests of opium at Canton, dissolving it in trenches with lime and salt and flushing it out to sea. He also wrote a letter to Queen Victoria, which you will read, asking her to stop it at its source.

The lesson shows that the emperor sent an honest official, Lin Zexu, to stop the trade, and that the dispute grew into the First Opium War, which China lost. Another path was to bargain over the trade rather than stamp it out. That might have avoided a war, but it would have left the addiction and the loss of silver in place. Each path had its own costs, and nobody could be sure how either would end.

Lin's seizure turned a quarrel between merchants and officials into a quarrel between governments. The British trade superintendent at Canton, Captain Charles Elliot, persuaded the merchants to hand over their opium by promising that the British government would pay for it. Once he had made that promise, the loss became a debt that only Parliament could settle, and the merchants had every reason to ask London to send a fleet.

THE OPIUM WARS

British merchants demanded payment for the lost opium and freedom of trade. Some members of Parliament thought the war was a disgrace. The young William Gladstone said he feared for Britain's good name. But the government sent a fleet, and in 1840 the First Opium War began. British steam-powered gunboats, such as the iron-hulled Nemesis, could go up rivers against wind and tide, and Chinese forts and sailing junks were no match for them. The British could strike anywhere along the coast and up the great rivers, while the Qing armies, spread across a huge empire, could not be in every place at once.

In the Second Opium War of 1856 to 1860, British and French troops marched on Beijing and burned the Summer Palace, in reprisal for the death of prisoners in Chinese hands. China opened more ports, admitted Christian missionaries, and allowed the opium trade. Russia, taking its chance, won lands north of the Amur River.

Hong Kong was a surprise even to the British government. The foreign secretary, Lord Palmerston, complained that Elliot had accepted a barren island with hardly a house upon it, and he dismissed him. But the island had a deep harbor, and it became the base from which British merchants and warships worked the China coast for more than a century.

The wars struck an empire already in trouble. The Taiping Rebellion, led by Hong Xiuquan, who believed himself the brother of Jesus Christ, raged from 1850 to 1864 and cost some twenty million lives. Hong had failed the civil service examinations, and he built his movement on a mixture of Christian tracts and visions. His rebels held Nanjing for eleven years. The Qing won in the end, with armies raised by regional governors and led in part by Chinese officials, and with the help of a foreign-led force under a British officer, Charles Gordon, the same Gordon who would die at Khartoum. A few officials began a program of reform, building arsenals and shipyards, but the court was divided and hesitant. The Empress Dowager Cixi ruled from behind the throne for decades. In 1898 a young emperor tried a bolder program of reform, and it lasted a hundred days before Cixi seized power and put him under guard. The dynasty stayed weak.

The Taipings were more than a religious sect. They promised to share land among families, forbade foot-binding and opium, and gave women places as soldiers and officials in their camps, and many peasants who hated the tax collectors flocked to them. But Hong's court at Nanjing grew luxurious and quarrelsome, and in 1856 its leaders turned on one another in a bloody purge that cost the rebellion much of its strength.

The shogun kept the lords in check by making them spend every other year in Edo and leave their families there when they went home. For more than two hundred years Japan kept to a policy of near isolation. Only Dutch and Chinese traders were allowed at Nagasaki, and through the Dutch a few Japanese scholars studied Western books on medicine and science. It was peaceful and orderly, and its cities were among the largest and most literate in the world. Edo had about a million people, and a large share of Japanese men, and many women, could read. The country had a strong merchant class, good roads, and a long habit of careful craftsmanship. After two centuries of peace, many samurai were officials and clerks living on fixed stipends of rice that bought less each year.

That ended in July 1853 when Commodore Matthew Perry of the United States Navy sailed into Edo Bay with four warships, two of them steam-driven and painted black. He carried a letter from President Millard Fillmore asking for trade and for the safe treatment of shipwrecked sailors. He promised to return the next year for an answer. The shogun's advisers were divided, and they knew that the Chinese had already lost to the British. In 1854 they signed a treaty opening two ports, and in 1858 they agreed to a treaty on terms much like China's. The American envoy, Townsend Harris, warned them that if they did not sign with him, Britain and France would soon arrive with warships and demand more. The shogun's chief minister signed without waiting for the emperor's approval, and angry samurai murdered him in 1860.

People in Edo had never seen steamships, and they called Perry's fleet the black ships, after the smoke and the tar on their hulls. A popular rhyme of the day played on the name of a famous brand of tea, Jokisen, four cups of which could keep a man awake all night, and on the four steamships that had kept the whole country awake. The joke covered real fear. The guns of the fleet could reach the shogun's capital, and the shogun's own forts could not reach the fleet.

THE MEIJI RESTORATION

The shogun looked weak, and angry samurai from the western domains of Satsuma and Choshu rallied to a cry of "Honor the emperor, expel the barbarians." But when British ships bombarded Kagoshima in 1863, after samurai of Satsuma had killed a British merchant on a road, and a fleet of Western ships shelled Choshu's forts in 1864, the samurai learned something. The foreigners could not be expelled by swords. They could be matched only by learning to do what the foreigners did. Satsuma and Choshu had been bitter rivals, but they formed an alliance in 1866. The last shogun gave up his office in 1867. In January 1868 these samurai seized the imperial palace in Kyoto and declared the emperor restored to power, and a short civil war ended with the defeat of the shogun's supporters. The new emperor, a boy of fifteen, took the name Meiji, meaning "enlightened rule," and the capital moved to Edo, which was renamed Tokyo. In a promise called the Charter Oath, the government pledged that knowledge would be sought throughout the world.

What followed was one of the fastest changes in history, led by a small group of capable men. The slogan of the new government was "rich country, strong army." The daimyo gave up their lands to the emperor in 1871. The samurai lost their special status and their pensions, and in 1873 Japan began drafting men from every class into a national army. Not everyone welcomed this, and peasants rioted against the draft and the new taxes. In 1877 an army of angry samurai under the famous Saigo Takamori rebelled, and the new conscript army defeated it. Japan sent a mission of its leaders, the Iwakura Mission, around the United States and Europe from 1871 to 1873 to study their governments, factories, and schools. Five girls went with it to be educated abroad, and the youngest was six. The government built railways, telegraphs, shipyards, and a system of public schools, and in 1889 it granted a constitution, modeled partly on Prussia's, with an elected Diet, although only a small share of men could vote.

    The government built model factories, such as the Tomioka silk mill, and hired hundreds of foreign advisers to teach engineering, law, and medicine. Later it sold many of the factories to private businessmen, and families like the Mitsui and the Iwasaki, who founded Mitsubishi, grew into great business houses. The foreign advisers were well paid but kept on short contracts, and when a Japanese student had learned the work, the foreigner went home. Japan was borrowing, but it was borrowing on its own terms.

    TWO PATHS

    The results soon showed. Japan won the right to rewrite its unequal treaties by 1911, and Britain agreed as early as 1894 to give up its special courts there. Japan defeated China in a war in 1894 and 1895 and took Taiwan, and in 1904 and 1905 it defeated Russia, a great European power, destroying a Russian fleet at the Battle of Tsushima. No one in Europe had expected an Asian nation to win such a war. Japan itself now became an imperial power, and in 1910 it annexed Korea.

    The peace of 1895 had a sting for Japan, though. The Treaty of Shimonoseki gave Japan a large payment from China and the island of Taiwan, and also the Liaodong Peninsula on the Chinese mainland. Within a week Russia, France, and Germany warned Japan to give the peninsula back, and Japan, not yet strong enough to refuse, did so. A few years later Russia was occupying that very territory. The episode showed Japan's leaders how the world of the great powers looked from the inside, and it sharpened their wish for a stronger navy.

    At Tsushima Admiral Togo Heihachiro destroyed a Russian fleet that had sailed halfway around the world from the Baltic Sea, a voyage of some eighteen thousand miles. Nearly all its ships were sunk or captured, and only a few reached port. The victory made Japan's name across Asia, where many people under European rule took heart from it, and it alarmed Europeans who had assumed that they would always win.

    Why did Japan change so quickly? Japan was a smaller country, with one language and a strong sense of itself. Its leaders had a clear choice: reform or be swallowed. China was vast, and its court was divided, still convinced of its own greatness, and struggling with rebellions. Judge both states by the oldest tests you know: keeping order at home and defending the country. None of this was certain beforehand. A few different decisions in Edo or Kyoto might have given Japan a fate like China's. Now think about the question of the week: was imperialism a matter of profit, power, or duty?
