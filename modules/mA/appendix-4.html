🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

In the late twentieth century, long after the world had said "never again" about the Holocaust, governments and armed groups carried out mass killings of their own people in several countries. This is a hard subject. Today you will study four cases with care and ask what made such crimes possible and how the world responded.

CAMBODIA, 1975-1979

Cambodia had been dragged into the Vietnam War, and a bitter civil war followed. In April 1975 a communist movement called the Khmer Rougekuh-MAIR ROOZHA French name meaning "Red Khmers." The Khmer are the main people of Cambodia, and red was the color of communism. See the glossary box., led by a man known as Pol Pot, captured the capital, Phnom Penh. Its leaders wanted to build a pure peasant society at once. They emptied the cities, abolished money, schools, markets, and religion, and forced millions to work in the fields. Anyone seen as an enemy, including teachers, officials, monks, and people with glasses or a foreign language, could be arrested and killed. Ethnic minorities such as the Cham Muslims and ethnic Vietnamese were targeted. Between 1.5 and 2 million people, perhaps a quarter of the country, died of execution, starvation, and disease.

The killing ended in January 1979 when neighboring Vietnam invaded and drove the Khmer Rouge from power. Vietnam acted for its own reasons, not as a rescuer, and many governments kept recognizing the Khmer Rouge at the United Nations for years. A joint court, set up with the UN decades later, finally convicted two surviving leaders in 2018 of genocide against the Vietnamese and the Cham. Most of the victims were fellow Khmer, which is one reason lawyers still debate how the term applies to the whole slaughter.

THE BALKANS, 1991-1999

Yugoslavia was a country of Serbs, Croats, Bosniaks (Bosnian Muslims), Albanians, and others, held together by a communist state and by the personal authority of its leader, Josip Tito, who died in 1980. When communism collapsed, politicians in several republics stirred up national feeling to win support. Slovenia and Croatia declared independence in 1991, and Bosnia followed in 1992. Serb forces, backed by the government in Belgrade, attacked Bosnian towns and drove out Muslims and Croats. The worst single atrocity came in July 1995 at Srebrenica, a town the United Nations had declared a safe area. Bosnian Serb forces took it, separated the men and boys from the women, and killed more than eight thousand of them. Dutch peacekeepers on the spot had few soldiers and orders that did not allow them to fight. NATO air strikes and an American-led peace conference at Dayton, Ohio, ended the Bosnian war in November 1995. RWANDA, 1994

Rwanda's people, the Hutu majority and the Tutsi minority, spoke one language and shared one culture and one land. Under earlier kings the Tutsi had held more power, and when Belgium ruled the country it made the difference official by giving everyone an identity card that stated his group. After independence the Hutu took over the government, and Tutsi were often persecuted. In the early 1990s a rebel army of Tutsi exiles invaded from Uganda, and a fragile peace followed. Extremists in the Hutu government feared it and prepared a plan.

On April 6, 1994, a plane carrying Rwanda's president was shot down, and within hours roadblocks went up and killing began. Radio broadcasts told listeners to kill their Tutsi neighbors, and militiasmuh-LISH-uhAn armed band of civilians, often organized or supported by a government, that fights outside the regular army. See the glossary box. and ordinary citizens took part, sometimes with machetes. In about a hundred days, perhaps 800,000 people died, most of them Tutsi, along with Hutu who tried to protect them. A small United Nations force under a Canadian general, Roméo Dallaire, begged for reinforcements, and the Security Council instead cut the force. The killing stopped when the Tutsi-led rebel army took the capital in July. Rwanda later tried huge numbers of suspects in local community courts, and a UN tribunal tried the top leaders.

DARFUR, 2003 TO THE PRESENT

Darfur is a dry region in western Sudan, where African farming peoples and Arab herding peoples had shared land and water for centuries. Drought, a growing desert, and fights over scarce land and wells raised tensions, and the Sudanese government in Khartoum had long neglected the region. In 2003 rebels from the farming groups attacked government posts. The government responded by arming Arab militiasmuh-LISH-uhAn armed band of civilians, often organized or supported by a government, that fights outside the regular army. See the glossary box., who burned villages, killed civilians, and drove millions from their homes. Estimates of the dead run to about 300,000, with some 2.5 million displaced.

The United States called these events genocide in 2004, but a United Nations commission the next year did not find proof of genocidal intent, though it described crimes against humanity. The International Criminal Court later charged Sudan's president, Omar al-Bashir, including with genocide. He was never tried by the court and was removed by the army in 2019. African Union and UN troops arrived, but they were too few and often blocked by the government. Violence returned to the region in later years.

WHAT MADE IT POSSIBLE, AND WHAT CAME AFTER

Look across the four cases. In each, a government or movement held a monopoly of armed force and claimed that a group was an enemy or a threat. History supplied old grievances, such as the Belgian identity cards or the memory of wartime Yugoslavia. Economic stress, whether hunger, drought, or the collapse of a state, made fear easier to stir up. Propaganda, whether on the radio or in political speeches, taught ordinary people to see neighbors as enemies. Courts, newspapers, churches, and other institutions that could have resisted were silenced or captured.

Responses varied. Governments sent peacekeepers, imposed sanctions, or fought, but were often slow or unwilling, and their forces were sometimes too weak. Private organizations such as the Red Cross, church groups, and human-rights monitors fed and sheltered survivors and gathered evidence for later trials. Tribunals have convicted many leaders, though years or decades late. None of the four was inevitable. Individuals made choices, some to kill, some to refuse, and some to hide a neighbor at great risk. Pick one case and write down one point at which a different decision might have saved lives, and who could have made it.
