🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

When the Bolsheviks took power in Russia in 1917, they were a small party. Yet they had inherited something from the century before: a tradition of secret circles, plots, and willingness to use violence for a cause. Today you will see where that tradition came from, and then you will see what the new rulers did to the Church and other faiths.

A SOCIETY WITHOUT A SAFETY VALVE

Imperial Russia had no parliament until 1906, and until 1861 most of its peasants were serfsSURFA peasant who was legally bound to the land and to its owner and could not leave. See the glossary box.. Tsar Alexander II freed them that year, and then granted local councils and modern courts. These were real reforms, but they did not satisfy the educated young people of the cities, who read of Western freedom and saw none at home. Under the tsars, a man who wanted to argue for change in public often found himself under arrest. Political talk went underground. Each time the government answered criticism with prison or exile, it pushed critics toward more extreme ideas.

The first revolt came in 1825, when a group of young officers, the Decembrists, tried to prevent Nicholas I from taking the throne and to force a constitution. It failed in a day. Five leaders were hanged, and many others were sent to Siberia. Their example, and their wives who followed them into exile, became a legend for later generations of radicalsRAD-ih-kulA person who wants to change society at its roots and is often willing to use extreme means. TO THE PEOPLE, THEN TO THE BOMB

In the 1860s and 1870s a movement known as the populists, or narodnikinuh-ROD-nee-keeA Russian word, based on narod, "the people." It names the young radicals of the 1870s who went to the villages to win the peasants over. See the glossary box., arose among students. Their name comes from the Russian word for "the people." Thousands of them left the universities and went into villages in 1874 to teach the peasants and to stir them to revolt. The peasants, who were pious and loyal to the tsar, often turned them in to the police. Many of the young people concluded that talk was useless and only force would work.

A group called the People's Will chose terror. After several failed attempts, its members killed Alexander II with a bomb in St. Petersburg in 1881. It was a terrible irony. The tsar they murdered was the reformer, and his son, Alexander III, answered with harsh repression and a larger secret police. The radicals did not win the people. They lost the moderate reforms they might have used. A student named Alexander Ulyanov joined a later plot against Alexander III and was hanged in 1887. His younger brother Vladimir, who watched his family shunned afterward, would one day be known as Lenin.

THE CULTURE OF REVOLUTION

By 1900 a revolutionary culture had taken shape. It had its own heroes, such as the Decembrists and the assassins of the People's Will. It had its own habits: underground presses, false passports, smuggled pamphlets, secret meetings in student rooms, and years in prison or Siberia that served as a school for revolutionaries. It had a creed that the existing order was so evil that any means could be justified against it. A novel of 1863, Nikolai Chernyshevsky's What Is to Be Done?, portrayed such people as noble. Lenin said it shaped him more than any other book.

Lenin took the tradition and added to it the ideas of Karl Marx. In 1902 he published his own pamphlet with the same title. He argued that workers by themselves would only ask for better wages and that a small, strict party of full-time revolutionaries, a vanguardVAN-gardThe leading group in an advance. In Lenin's thought, a small party of dedicated revolutionaries who would lead the working class. See the glossary box., had to lead them. In 1903 the Russian Marxists split over this. Lenin's followers, who won a narrow vote, called themselves Bolsheviks, from the Russian word for "majority," and their rivals the Mensheviks, "minority." The names stuck even though the Bolsheviks often were the smaller group.

1917

The Bolsheviks did not cause the revolution of February 1917. Hunger, defeat in the First World War, and the collapse of the tsar's authority did that. But when the Provisional Government that replaced him proved weak, a disciplined party with experience of underground work was ready. In October the Bolsheviks seized the capital almost without a fight. They then closed the elected Constituent Assembly, where they were a minority, built a secret police called the Cheka, and won a civil war that killed millions. Their habits of secrecy, command, and force did not fade when they gained power. They became the habits of the state.

A WAR ON FAITH

Marx had called religion the "opium of the people," and Lenin held that it served the old order. The new government treated atheismAY-thee-iz-umThe belief that there is no God. See the glossary box. as official doctrine. In January 1918 it separated the Church from the state and from the schools, and it took the Church's land and buildings. The Russian Orthodox Church was the largest institution outside the party's control, and the party meant to break it. In 1921 and 1922 a famine struck, and the government used it as a pretext to seize church valuables, including sacred vessels. Resistance brought trials and executions. The head of the Church, Patriarch Tikhon, was placed under house arrest.

The campaign went on. In 1925 the party founded the League of the Militant Godless, which printed magazines and staged mock religious processions. Teachers taught atheism in schools. Believers were refused jobs, housing, or university places. Under Stalin's collectivization in the early 1930s, villages lost their churches, and in the Great Terror of 1937 and 1938 tens of thousands of priests, monks, and active believers were shot or sent to the Gulag. Mosques, synagogues, and Buddhist temples were closed as well, and Muslims and Jews suffered alongside the Christians. In 1943, in the middle of the war with Germany, Stalin eased the pressure and allowed a patriarch to be chosen, because he needed every ally. Another harsh campaign closed thousands of churches under Khrushchev around 1960.

THE PATTERN SPREADS

Other communist states followed the Soviet example. In Albania, the government closed or destroyed most of the country's churches and mosques, and in 1967 declared the country the world's first officially atheist state. In China, the Communist Party allowed only approved religious bodies under party control, and during the Cultural Revolution of the late 1960s mobs and Red Guards wrecked temples, churches, and mosques. Cuba, North Korea, and other communist governments placed similar limits on worship. Priests were jailed and clergy had to register with the state.

Yet faith did not die. Believers met in homes, hid icons and Bibles, and passed prayers from grandmother to grandchild. When Soviet power collapsed in 1991, millions returned to the churches. Look back at the whole story. The government wanted total control, and a rival loyalty was a danger. A believer answers to something beyond the state. Write two sentences explaining why this made a communist government treat religion as a threat.
