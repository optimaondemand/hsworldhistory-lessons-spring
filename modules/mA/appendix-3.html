🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

After 1945 dozens of new nations appeared in Africa and Asia, and the republics of Latin America and the Caribbean struggled to hold on to self-government. Some of them built lasting democraciesdih-MOK-ruh-seeA system in which citizens choose their leaders in regular, fair elections and the leaders must obey the law. See the glossary box.. Others lost them, sometimes within a few years. Today you will compare several cases and ask what separated the successes from the failures.

WHAT A REFORM MOVEMENT NEEDS

A democratic reform movement is a public effort to replace rule by one man, one party, or one army with government that answers to the people. Winning a single election is the easy part. The hard part is keeping the rules afterward. A lasting democracydih-MOK-ruh-seeA system in which citizens choose their leaders in regular, fair elections and the leaders must obey the law. See the glossary box. needs offices whose authority people accept, courts that apply the same laws to rulers and ruled, and a loser who agrees to leave. It also needs habits among the people, such as tolerance for opponents, willingness to compromise, and the self-government to wait for the next election instead of reaching for a gun.

Keep that checklist in mind as you read. No single factor guarantees success, and in each case a few individual choices tipped the balance.

ASIA: INDIA AND THE PHILIPPINES

India became independent in 1947 and adopted a constitution in 1950. It was a poor, enormous, and divided country, with many languages and religions, and few scholars expected it to stay democratic. Yet it held national elections again and again, and power changed hands at the ballot box. The most serious test came in 1975, when Prime Minister Indira Gandhi declared a state of emergency, jailed opponents, and censored the press. In 1977 she called an election anyway and lost. She accepted the verdict and left office. India's experience owed much to its parties, an independent election commission, and a free press, but it also owed something to a leader's decision to obey the voters.

The Philippines shows a different path. President Ferdinand Marcos ruled under martial law from 1972. When he held a snap election in 1986 and claimed victory despite evidence of fraud, hundreds of thousands of Filipinos filled the main avenue of Manila in peaceful protest. Soldiers refused to fire on them, and Marcos fled the country. The movement was a success, and democracy was restored. Yet its habits were fragile, and the country has since faced corruption, political violence, and the return of Marcos's family to power by election.

Since then, power has passed peacefully between the major parties many times, and Ghana is now often held up as one of Africa's more stable democracies. The country took a long road to get there.

Mandela became president and served a single term, a decision that set a model for how power can be handed on. It could have ended in civil war. It did not, largely because leaders on both sides chose to bargain instead of to fight.

LATIN AMERICA AND THE CARIBBEAN

His government jailed, tortured, and killed thousands of opponents, though it also reformed the economy. See the glossary box. on whether he should rule for eight more years, expecting to win. The voters said no. Opposition parties had united, registered millions of voters, and watched the count. Pinochet accepted the result, and elections returned in 1989. Argentina's junta, which seized power in 1976 and killed thousands in a so-called dirty war, collapsed in 1983 after losing a war over the Falkland Islands. It was followed by the first elected government in years, and Argentina has kept elections ever since.

In the Caribbean, Jamaica, Barbados, and Trinidad and Tobago built parliaments on the British model, and their governments have changed hands by election for generations. Haiti, the first free black republic, tells a harder story. After the Duvalier family dictatorship fell in 1986, a priest named Jean-Bertrand Aristide won the presidency in 1990, was removed by a coup the next year, and returned only after foreign intervention. Governments have risen and fallen amid violence ever since, and Haiti remains among the poorest nations in the Western Hemisphere.

WHEN REFORM FAILS

Not every movement ends well. In June 1989 Chinese students and workers gathered in Beijing's Tiananmen Square to ask for a more open government. The Communist Party sent the army in, and soldiers killed hundreds, perhaps more. The party kept its monopoly on power. In Myanmar, then called Burma, the main opposition party led by Aung San Suu Kyi won a national election in 1990, but the generals refused to hand over power. A later opening in the 2010s was ended by another military coup in 2021. In the Arab uprisings of 2011, Tunisia seemed to give the best hope. It wrote a new constitution and held elections, but in 2021 its president suspended parliament and took sweeping powers. Egypt's elected president was removed by the army in 2013.

WHAT DO THE CASES SHOW?

Look at what the successes share. In India, Ghana's later years, South Africa, and Chile, citizens were organized into parties, unions, churches, and newspapers that could resist a ruler and also negotiate with him. Elites mostly kept to the rules after an election they lost. Armies stayed out of politics or stepped back. In the failures, one institution, usually the army or one party, held more power than any rival, and nothing else could check it. A democracy does not defend itself. It depends on the choices of ordinary people and leaders who prize law and self-restraint over the temptations of power.

Be careful not to treat any of this as inevitable. Indira Gandhi could have stayed in office. Pinochet could have ignored the vote. Soldiers in Manila could have fired. History moved the way it did because particular people made particular choices, and different choices were open to them. Choose two of the cases above. For each, name one decision by a leader and one by a group of citizens that helped decide the outcome.
