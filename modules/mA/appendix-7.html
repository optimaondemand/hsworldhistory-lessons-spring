🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

People have always moved. Some walk away from a famine. Some cross an ocean for land or work. Some run from a war with only what they can carry. Today you will learn how geographers explain why people move, and how a movement changes both the place left behind and the place where the migrants settle.

PUSHED AND PULLED

Geographers sort the reasons for migration into two kinds. A push factorPUSH FAK-terA reason that drives people to leave a place, such as famine, war, persecution, or lack of work. See the glossary box. makes staying hard. It may be hunger, war, persecution, or a lack of work. A pull factorPUL FAK-terA reason that draws migrants toward a place, such as jobs, land, safety, or freedom. See the glossary box. makes another place appealing, such as a job, cheap land, safety, or the freedom to worship. Most migrants feel both at once. They are pushed from one place and pulled to another, and they also weigh the cost of the journey and what they know about their destination. News from relatives who left earlier is often the strongest pull of all.

Keep one distinction in mind. Most migrants choose to go, even if their choices are hard. Some are forced, as were the millions of Africans carried across the Atlantic in slave ships. This lesson looks mainly at those who chose, or who fled.

IRELAND, 1845

Ireland in the 1840s was crowded and poor. Millions of tenant farmers lived on tiny plots, and much of their food was a single crop, the potato. In 1845 a blight arrived from America and rotted the potato fields. The harvests failed again in the next years. About a million people died of hunger and disease in the Great Famine, and more than a million others chose to emigrateEM-ih-graytTo leave one's country to live in another. See the glossary box. over the following decade. British policy did not help, as exports of grain and cattle continued and relief was slow and stingy. Hunger was the push.

The pull was across the Atlantic. The United States and Canada needed workers for canals, railroads, and factories, and wages were far better than at home. The Irish arrived in Boston, New York, and Liverpool in huge numbers. Look at the effects at each end. Ireland's population, which had been about eight million in 1841, kept falling for decades and never recovered its old size. Ireland lost many of its young and its strongest workers, though the migrants sent home remittancesrih-MIT-nsMoney that a migrant sends home to family in the country he or she left. See the glossary box. that kept families alive and helped others to follow. In America, the newcomers filled cities and built a strong Catholic church. They also met suspicion from Protestants who feared their religion, and for a time political leaders won votes by stirring up that fear.

SOUTH ASIA, 1947

When Britain left India in 1947, it divided the land into two states, a mainly Hindu India and a mainly Muslim Pakistan. The border ran through Punjab in the northwest and Bengal in the east, where Hindus, Muslims, and Sikhs lived side by side. Fear of violence drove people out. Within months, perhaps ten to fifteen million people crossed the new line in both directions, in one of the largest forced migrations in history. Hundreds of thousands, and by some counts as many as a million, died in riots, massacres, and the hardships of the road.

The effects are plain on any modern map. Punjab was cut in two, and the cities of Lahore and Amritsar, only about thirty miles apart, ended up on different sides of a hostile border. Villages that had been home to many faiths for centuries became nearly single-faith. Refugeesref-yoo-JEEA person who has fled his or her country to escape war, persecution, or violence. See the glossary box. filled Delhi, Karachi, and Calcutta and strained their resources. The border itself became one of the most heavily guarded in the world.

GUEST WORKERS IN EUROPE

After the Second World War, West Germany's factories were booming and the war had left too few young men to staff them. The government signed agreements with Italy, Spain, Greece, and in 1961 Turkey to bring in what it called GastarbeiterGAHST-ar-by-terA German word meaning "guest worker." It was the name for foreign workers invited to Germany in the 1950s and 1960s, who were expected to stay only for a time. See the glossary box.. The workers were supposed to come for a year or two and then go home. For the Turkish villagers, the pull was wages many times higher than at home. Many left farms in Anatolia. They sent remittancesrih-MIT-nsMoney that a migrant sends home to family in the country he or she left. See the glossary box. that bought homes and helped to pay for their children's schooling.

Germany stopped recruiting in 1973 when its economy slowed, but many of the workers stayed and brought their families. Millions of their descendants are now German citizens. The effects at the destination were deep and mixed. German industry gained workers and new neighborhoods, with their own shops, mosques, and food, spread through the cities. The arrival also stirred debates about language, schooling, and what it means to belong. At the origin, villages lost many of their young workers but gained money from abroad.

A BORDER IN MOTION

Migration also changes the border areas where movement is concentrated. Find El Paso, Texas, on your map, and across the Rio Grande the Mexican city of Ciudad Juarez. Once they were small towns on a river crossing. In 1965 Mexico began a program that let foreign companies build factories, called maquiladoras, just south of the border and ship goods back without heavy taxes. Workers from all over Mexico moved north to fill the jobs, and Ciudad Juarez grew into a city of more than a million. The cities on both sides depend on each other for trade, family ties, and shopping, and they also share problems of crime, traffic, and water.

Now compare a different kind of border. Since 2011 the war in Syria has driven more than six million people from their homes to other countries. The greatest number went to nearby Turkey, Lebanon, and Jordan, which strained their schools and housing. In 2015 many more traveled on to Europe, and Germany took in about a million. Border areas such as the Greek islands and the Balkan roads became crowded, and governments quarreled over who should bear the load. In each case, a border is not a simple line. It is a place where pushes and pulls meet.

WHAT TO NOTICE

Look at what the cases share. Migrants take with them their skills, their faith, their food, and their loyalties. Sending places lose workers but gain money and news, and receiving places gain labor and new ideas while they also face costs and tensions. How well newcomers fit in depends partly on choices by governments and partly on the habits of the people already there, such as whether they welcome strangers, expect them to learn the language, or give them a path to citizenship.

Choose one of the four cases above. List two push factors and two pull factors, then list one effect on the place the migrants left and one on the place they reached. Check your answers against your map, and note whether the migrants crossed a border and what that border changed.
