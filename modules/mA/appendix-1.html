🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

In 1400 no European ship had ever reached India by sea or crossed the Atlantic and returned. By 1522 one fleet had sailed around the whole earth. Today you will learn what drove that change, who paid for each voyage, and why a few small nations on the edge of Europe led the way.

WHY GO?

Europeans wanted the goods of Asia. Pepper, cinnamon, cloves, and silk reached Europe by long and costly routes, passing through many hands. Each merchant, port, and ruler along the way took a share, so a sack of pepper cost far more in Lisbon than in India. Anyone who could sail directly to the source could keep that difference. Africa mattered too. Gold from West Africa crossed the Sahara by camel, and a ship that reached the African coast might meet those traders on the shore.

Faith was part of the mix. Portugal and Spain had spent centuries fighting Muslim kingdoms on their own soil, and the habit of holy war did not end when the fighting did. Many Europeans hoped to find Christian allies beyond the Muslim world, and they hoped to spread their faith. Add rivalry. In 1453 the Ottoman Turks took Constantinople, and later Ottoman power over the eastern Mediterranean made the old trade routes more tense and more expensive. Historians argue about how much that mattered, since spices kept flowing east of Europe by several routes. It is safer to say that merchants and kings felt hemmed in and went looking for another way.

THE TOOLS OF THE SEA

Desire alone was not enough. Sailors also needed better tools. A ship that sailed south easily had to find a way home. The solution was to swing far out into the Atlantic and catch the westerly winds that carried ships back, a trick that took the Portuguese decades to master.

Navigators also learned to find their position far from land. With the compass for direction and the astrolabeAS-truh-laybA hand-held instrument that measures the height of the sun or a star above the horizon, which helps a sailor find his latitude. This was a slow, practical, and very careful kind of knowledge. It belonged to people who tried things, failed, wrote down what happened, and tried again.

PORTUGAL POINTS THE WAY

Portugal was small and poor, but its crown could focus its effort. Prince Henry, later called the Navigator, was a sponsorSPON-serA person, ruler, or company that pays for a voyage in the hope of a return. See the glossary box. of many voyages down the African coast from the 1420s. He rarely sailed himself. He paid for ships and crews, and each expedition pushed a little farther. After his death in 1460 the crown carried on. In 1488 Bartolomeu Dias rounded the southern tip of Africa, which later generations called the Cape of Good Hope, and found that the Indian Ocean lay open beyond it.

Ten years later King Manuel I sent Vasco da Gama to finish the job. Da Gama left Lisbon in 1497 with four ships, rounded the Cape, hired a pilot in East Africa who knew the monsoon winds, and reached Calicut in India in May 1498. The voyage cost many lives, and he lost a ship and a large part of his crew. Yet the cargo he brought home paid for the expedition many times over. Portugal had opened a sea road to Asia. In 1500 another Portuguese captain, Pedro Alvares Cabral, bound for India, swung so far west that he landed in Brazil, and Portugal claimed it.

A GENOESE CAPTAIN AND A SPANISH QUEEN

Christopher Columbus was an Italian sailor from Genoa who thought he could reach Asia by sailing west. His plan rested on a mistake. He believed the earth was much smaller than it is, and the experts who advised the Portuguese king knew he was wrong. They were right, and when Columbus asked Portugal for ships, the answer was no. He then spent years pleading with the rulers of Spain. Queen Isabella of Castile and King Ferdinand of Aragon first said no as well, since they were busy finishing their war against the last Muslim kingdom in Spain, Granada.

Granada fell in January 1492, and the Spanish crowns agreed to be his sponsorSPON-serA person, ruler, or company that pays for a voyage in the hope of a return. See the glossary box.. Columbus left in August with three ships and sighted land in October. He had found islands in the Caribbean, not Asia. Here is a puzzle to consider. If the Portuguese advisors had been wrong instead of right, Portugal might have claimed the Atlantic. If Granada had fallen a year later, Columbus might have gone to France or England. As it happened, Spain gained a huge new field of action, and soon men like Cortés and Pizarro were using it. To keep the peace between the two rivals, the Treaty of Tordesillas in 1494 drew a line down the Atlantic. Spain could claim lands to the west of it, and Portugal to the east.

ENGLAND, FRANCE, AND THE NORTHERN SEARCH

Other kingdoms wanted a share. In 1497 John Cabot, another Italian, sailed from Bristol for King Henry VII of England and reached the coast of North America, probably Newfoundland. England did little with the claim for decades. France sent Jacques Cartier, who explored the Gulf of St. Lawrence and the great river beyond it in the 1530s for King Francis I, hoping to find a passage to Asia. He did not find one.

In the next century the sponsors changed. Early voyages were paid for by kings, but the Dutch and English now chartered trading companies, which raised money from many investors. In 1609 the Dutch East India Company hired the Englishman Henry Hudson to look for a northern route to Asia, and he explored the river that now carries his name. The rise of the company as a sponsorSPON-serA person, ruler, or company that pays for a voyage in the hope of a return. See the glossary box. marks a shift. Private merchants, not only crowns, were now ready to gamble on distant ventures.

AROUND THE WORLD

The greatest voyage of the age was also the strangest. Ferdinand Magellan was a Portuguese captain who had quarreled with his own king. He offered his plan to the young king of Spain, Charles I, who is better known as the Holy Roman Emperor Charles V. Magellan proposed to reach the Spice Islands by sailing west around South America. Charles agreed to pay for five ships in 1519. The crews faced mutiny, storms, hunger, and scurvy. Magellan found the strait at the tip of South America that now bears his name and crossed the Pacific, which took more than three months and nearly destroyed the fleet.

Magellan did not live to see the end. He was killed in a fight in the Philippines in April 1521. A Spanish officer, Juan Sebastian Elcano, took command, and in September 1522 the last ship, the Victoria, reached Spain with eighteen survivors. They had managed to circumnavigatesur-kum-NAV-ih-gaytTo sail all the way around the world. See the glossary box. the globe. Only one ship and a handful of men came back from a fleet of five ships and around 240 men, but the cargo of cloves still earned a profit. The voyage proved that the earth could be circled by sea and that the Pacific was far larger than anyone in Europe had guessed.

WHAT TO REMEMBER

Look at the pattern. Each voyage needed a sponsorSPON-serA person, ruler, or company that pays for a voyage in the hope of a return. See the glossary box. with money and a captain with courage, and the rewards were uncertain. Prince Henry, Manuel, Isabella, and Charles each judged that the risk was worth it. Their captains faced the sea with real daring, and many paid with their lives. Some sailors were also ruthless toward the peoples they met, and later lessons show how the contacts that began here changed three continents.

Nothing about this was fixed in advance. Portugal's choice to keep exploring, Granada's fall in the year Columbus sailed, and a Portuguese captain's quarrel with his king all changed who went where. On your map, trace the routes of Dias, da Gama, Columbus, and Magellan, and note which crown paid for each. Then ask yourself why the nations on the Atlantic edge of Europe, and not the great powers in its center, took the lead.
