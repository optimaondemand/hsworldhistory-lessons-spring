🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

Every history book assumes that you know what year it is, how we know it, and why anyone should care about the past at all. Today you will look at those assumptions. You will see how different peoples counted time, how historians test what they are told, and why remembering matters for the kind of people we become.

WHAT YEAR IS IT?

Every calendarKAL-un-derA system for dividing time into days, months, and years and for numbering the years. See the glossary box. answers two questions. How do you divide time, and where do you start counting? The answers differ because peoples have looked at different heavens and held different beliefs. Most of what you read in this course dates events by the Gregorian calendar, which numbers years from the traditional birth of Jesus. Historians now write BCE and CE, for "before the common era" and "common era," but the starting point is the same.

The Gregorian calendar is a repair of an older one. Julius Caesar introduced a year of 365 days with a leap day every four years. The solar year is slightly shorter, and by the 1500s the calendar had drifted about ten days away from the seasons. Pope Gregory XIII fixed it in 1582 by dropping ten days and changing the leap-year rule. Catholic countries adopted the change at once. Protestant England and its colonies resisted for 170 years, until 1752, and Russia waited until after its revolution in 1918. For generations, a letter dated in one country could say a different day than the same moment in another.

OTHER WAYS TO COUNT

Muslims count years from the HijraHIJ-rahAn Arabic word meaning "migration." Their calendar is purely lunar, with twelve months of twenty-nine or thirty days. A lunar year is about eleven days shorter than a solar year, so the months slide steadily through the seasons. The month of fasting, Ramadan, therefore falls in summer in some years and in winter in others. For Muslims, that is part of the point. The calendar follows the moon, and worship does not depend on the farmer's seasons.

Chinese reckoning is lunisolarloo-nih-SOH-lerBased on both the moon and the sun. A lunisolar calendar follows the moon's phases for its months and adds extra months to stay in step with the sun's year. See the glossary box.. Its months follow the moon, and an extra month is added every few years to keep the New Year near the same season. Years are named in a repeating cycle of sixty, combining twelve animals with five elements. The Maya of Central America used a calendar of 260 days for ritual and a 365-day year for the seasons, and the two cycles meshed like gears. For long spans, they kept a Long Count, which numbered days from a starting point in what we would call 3114 BCE.

Sometimes a government tried to start time over. In 1793 the French revolutionaries declared that the founding of the republic in 1792 had begun a new age, Year One. They made weeks ten days long and gave the months new names such as Thermidor and Brumaire. They wanted to erase the Church's calendar of saints and Sundays. Few people liked it, since a ten-day week gave them fewer days of rest, and Napoleon abolished it in 1806. A calendar is hard to change because it is bound up with a people's habits and worship.

HOW DO WE KNOW?

Calendars tell us when people said events happened. They do not tell us whether the people were right. For that, historians ask questions, and the method is called historical inquiry. A historian begins with a puzzle, finds sources that bear on it, and asks who made each source, when, and why. A letter, a law, a tax record, and a chronicle written by a monk may all say different things. Where they disagree, the historian weighs which one was closest to the events and had the least reason to lie.

Written sources are only part of the story. archaeologyar-kee-OL-uh-jeeThe study of the past through the objects, buildings, and remains that people left behind. Other sciences help date and read these finds. Linguistics helped a French scholar, Jean-Francois Champollion, decipher Egyptian hieroglyphs in 1822 by comparing the Greek, demotic, and hieroglyphic texts on the Rosetta Stone.

A modern example shows the methods working together. English chronicles said that King Richard III was killed at Bosworth in 1485 and buried in a friary in Leicester, but the friary was long gone and its site forgotten. In 2012 archaeologists dug beneath a parking lot and found a skeleton with a curved spine and a battle wound to the skull. Radiocarbon dating placed the man in the right century, and DNA from a descendant of Richard's family matched. Documents pointed to the place, and science confirmed the identity. Neither could have done it alone.

WHY DOES THE PAST MATTER?

Now ask a different question. Why should anyone bother? One answer is that a people's memory of its past makes it who it is. At Passover, Jewish families retell the story of the flight from Egypt, and each person is taught to see himself as one who was set free. The Romans kept the busts of their ancestors in the hall of the house and carried them in funeral processions. A young Roman grew up under the eyes of men who had held office and led armies, and was expected to be worthy of them.

The Greek writer Plutarch took this idea to its logical end. In his Parallel Lives, he paired Greek and Roman statesmen and told their stories to show what courage, moderation, and ambition could do. He did not think history was a pile of dates. He thought it was a school of character, and that a reader who watched a great man succeed or fall would learn how to act. Many of the founders of republics in later centuries read Plutarch in just this spirit. This is what a classical education means by history. It is a long conversation with those who came before us.

Memory can also fail us. A people may remember only its triumphs, or it may invent a golden age that never was. Nations have been bound together by shared stories, and they have also been led astray by false ones. That is why the careful work of the first half of this lesson matters. The historian's tools protect the memory of a people from becoming a myth. A community that remembers truthfully, and honors what is worthy in its past without hiding what is not, has a firmer sense of who it is and what it owes to those who come after.

TRY IT

Choose one event from this course. Write down the year by the Gregorian calendar, then look up how a Muslim, a Chinese, or a Jewish calendar would date the same year. Next, name two kinds of evidence a historian could use to check what the event's chroniclers claimed. Finally, write two sentences on what a young person in the society that lived through it might have been taught to remember, and why.
