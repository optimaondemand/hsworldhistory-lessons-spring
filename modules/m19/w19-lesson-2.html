THE EMPIRE OF THE FOUR QUARTERS

The Inca ruled the largest empire in the Americas, stretching some 2,500 miles along the Andes mountains from what is now Ecuador to Chile. Their capital was Cusco, and their ruler was called the Sapa Inca. Perhaps ten million people lived under his rule. The Inca had no writing as we know it. Officials kept records on bundles of knotted cords called khipuKEE-pooA Quechua word for a set of knotted cords used by the Inca to record numbers and other information. See the glossary box.. They built thousands of miles of stone roads and stocked storehouses along them, so that armies and messengers could cross the mountains quickly.

The Inca had no money, and they collected no tributeTRIB-yootGoods, labor, or money that a conquered people must regularly pay to their conqueror. See the glossary box. in coins. Instead the state claimed labor. Every household owed a share of work to the empire, a duty called the mitaMEE-tahA Quechua word for the labor that each Inca household owed to the state. It was an orderly system, but it depended on one thing above all: a ruler everyone obeyed.

A CIVIL WAR AND A PLAGUE

Around 1527 the Sapa Inca, Huayna Capac, died suddenly. Many historians think a disease, perhaps smallpox, spread south from the Spanish settlements and killed him. His two sons, Huáscar and Atahualpa, then fought a long civil war over the throne. Atahualpa won in 1532. His armies were exhausted, and the empire's best generals had been killed or had made enemies. Into this broken moment walked Francisco Pizarro.

Pizarro was born in Spain around 1478, the illegitimate son of a soldier. He never learned to read. He had spent twenty years in the Americas, and he had heard rumors of a rich kingdom in the south. The Spanish king gave him permission to conquer it. In 1532 he marched into the Andes with 168 men. Notice the timing. A year earlier, and he would have met a united empire. By accident, he arrived as the war ended.

THE TRAP AT CAJAMARCA

On November 16, 1532, Atahualpa agreed to meet the Spaniards in the town of Cajamarca. He came with thousands of attendants, most of them lightly armed, because he did not think 168 strangers were a threat. Pizarro had hidden his men around the town square. A priest stepped forward and demanded that Atahualpa accept the Christian faith and the Spanish king. Atahualpa threw down the book the priest held out. Pizarro gave the signal. Cannon fired, horsemen charged, and soldiers cut through the crowd. Thousands of Inca died. Atahualpa was captured. Not one Spaniard was killed.

Atahualpa tried to buy his freedom. He offered to fill a large room once with gold and twice with silver. He kept his promise, and the Spaniards melted down tons of beautiful metalwork into bars of bullionBUL-yunGold or silver in bars or lumps, valued by weight rather than as coins. See the glossary box.. Then they put him on trial and executed him in July 1533. Some Spaniards protested that this was wrong. Pizarro went ahead, because he feared the Inca armies would rally around a living emperor.

WHY WAS IT SO EASY?

Pizarro's victory looks like Cortés's, but there are important differences. Pizarro had no large native army at his side at first. What he had was a plan that took advantage of how the Inca state worked. All authority in the empire flowed from one man. When the Spaniards seized Atahualpa, they took the head of the whole system. His generals hesitated, because they did not want to risk the emperor's life, and each day of hesitation helped the invaders.

Even so, the conquest was neither quick nor complete. Peoples who had been conquered by the Inca, such as the Cañari and the Chachapoya, resented their rulers and helped the Spaniards. But in 1536 Manco Inca, a brother of Atahualpa, whom the Spaniards had made their puppet, escaped and raised a huge army. He besieged Cusco for months, and the Spaniards barely held on. Manco's heirs kept a small Inca state alive in the mountains until 1572. Again we can see contingency at work. A united Inca army, a better-guarded square, or an emperor who stayed away from Cajamarca might have turned the whole story around.

THE MOUNTAIN OF SILVER

The Spaniards fought each other over the spoils, and Pizarro himself was murdered in 1541. Inca resistance went on for decades, until the last Inca leader was executed in 1572. By then something far larger than the gold of Cajamarca had been found. In 1545 a rich vein of silver was discovered in a mountain at Potosí, high in the Andes in what is now Bolivia. Within fifty years the barren town had grown to some 160,000 people, a city as large as London or Paris.

A RICH CROWN, A POOR KINGDOM

The silver traveled in fleets across the Atlantic to Spain, where the Crown took a fifth of it. Some of it crossed the Pacific on Spanish ships to buy Chinese silk and porcelain. Spain's kings, Charles V and then Philip II, spent it on armies. They fought the Protestants, the Ottoman Turks, and the rebels you will meet next week. The money never stayed. The Crown borrowed against future shipments and went bankrupt again and again, in 1557, 1575, and 1596 among other years. Prices rose across Europe, because there was suddenly more silver chasing the same goods.

This reveals a hard truth about the first-order problem of prosperity. A country gets rich by producing things, not by digging up money. Spain's nobles looked down on trade and craft work, and its kings spent bullionBUL-yunGold or silver in bars or lumps, valued by weight rather than as coins. See the glossary box. on wars instead of workshops and roads. Plutarch would have recognized the pattern. A people can be dazzled by treasure and neglect the habits that make a nation strong. Meanwhile, in the cold northern provinces of Philip's own empire, merchants and sailors were learning how to grow rich without a single mine. That is where we go next.
