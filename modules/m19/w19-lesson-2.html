THE EMPIRE OF THE FOUR QUARTERS

The Inca ruled the largest empire in the Americas, stretching some 2,500 miles along the Andes mountains from what is now Ecuador to Chile. Their capital was Cusco, and their ruler was called the Sapa Inca. Perhaps ten million people lived under his rule. The Inca had no writing as we know it. Officials kept records on bundles of knotted cords called khipuKEE-pooA Quechua word for a set of knotted cords used by the Inca to record numbers and other information. See the glossary box.. They built thousands of miles of stone roads and stocked storehouses along them, so that armies and messengers could cross the mountains quickly.

The Inca called their empire the Land of the Four Quarters, because four great regions met at Cusco like the arms of a cross. The Sapa Inca was believed to be a descendant of the sun, and the bodies of dead rulers were kept as mummies that were still treated as members of the court. Runners were posted at stations along the roads and passed messages from one to the next, so news traveled faster than any army could march. Storehouses along the roads held maize, cloth, and weapons. A governor who read the cords could see at a glance what a province could supply and what it still owed.

The Inca had no money, and they collected no tributeTRIB-yootGoods, labor, or money that a conquered people must regularly pay to their conqueror. See the glossary box. in coins. Instead the state claimed labor. Every household owed a share of work to the empire, a duty called the mitaMEE-tahA Quechua word for the labor that each Inca household owed to the state. The Spanish later turned it into a forced-labor draft. See the glossary box.. People farmed state lands, built roads, or served in the army. In return the state fed them in lean years. It was an orderly system, but it depended on one thing above all: a ruler everyone obeyed.

A CIVIL WAR AND A PLAGUE

Around 1527 the Sapa Inca, Huayna Capac, died suddenly. Many historians think a disease, perhaps smallpox, spread south from the Spanish settlements and killed him. His two sons, Huáscar and Atahualpa, then fought a long civil war over the throne. Atahualpa won in 1532. His armies were exhausted, and the empire's best generals had been killed or had made enemies. Into this broken moment walked Francisco Pizarro.

The two brothers had split the empire between them in everything but name. Huáscar held Cusco, the old capital. Atahualpa had been with the army in the far north, near Quito, and the veteran soldiers followed him. His generals beat Huáscar's forces and took Huáscar prisoner in 1532. Atahualpa was resting with his army near the hot springs outside Cajamarca, still celebrating, when messengers told him that strangers had come up from the coast.

Pizarro was born in Spain around 1478, the illegitimate son of a soldier. He never learned to read. He had spent twenty years in the Americas, and he had heard rumors of a rich kingdom in the south. The Spanish king gave him permission to conquer it. In 1532 he marched into the Andes with 168 men. Notice the timing. A year earlier, and he would have met a united empire. By accident, he arrived as the war ended.

His road to Cajamarca had been long. In 1528 he had reached the Inca coast and seen a trading raft loaded with fine cloth and metalwork. He went home to Spain to ask for a royal contract, and he sailed south again in 1531. In the autumn of 1532 he led his men up from the coast into the mountains, a hard climb of weeks through thin air and cold. Atahualpa's scouts reported on the strangers the whole way. He had just won a war, he commanded tens of thousands of soldiers, and the strangers were few. He chose to let them come and to meet them.

THE TRAP AT CAJAMARCA

On November 16, 1532, Atahualpa agreed to meet the Spaniards in the town of Cajamarca. He came with thousands of attendants, most of them lightly armed, because he did not think 168 strangers were a threat. Pizarro had hidden his men around the town square. A priest stepped forward and demanded that Atahualpa accept the Christian faith and the Spanish king. Atahualpa threw down the book the priest held out. Pizarro gave the signal. Cannon fired, horsemen charged, and soldiers cut through the crowd. Thousands of Inca died. Atahualpa was captured. Not one Spaniard was killed.

Look at how the square was built. It was large and ringed by long stone halls with wide doorways. Pizarro put his horsemen in two of the halls and his foot soldiers in a third, and he aimed two small cannon at the open space between them. Then his men waited through the whole day. Atahualpa arrived late in the afternoon, carried on a litter by nobles, and saw only a few Spaniards standing in an empty square. The priest, Vicente de Valverde, spoke through an interpreter. When the signal came, the crowd had almost no room to run, and Spanish accounts say a wall gave way under the press of bodies. Pizarro was wounded in the hand while keeping his own men from killing the emperor. The plan depended on taking Atahualpa alive.

Atahualpa tried to buy his freedom. He offered to fill a large room once with gold and twice with silver. He kept his promise, and the Spaniards melted down tons of beautiful metalwork into bars of bullionBUL-yunGold or silver in bars or lumps, valued by weight rather than as coins. See the glossary box.. Then they put him on trial and executed him in July 1533. Some Spaniards protested that this was wrong. Pizarro went ahead, because he feared the Inca armies would rally around a living emperor.

The ransom took months to gather. The room was about twenty-two feet long and seventeen feet wide, and the gold was to rise as high as a man could reach. Messengers carried treasure in from temples and palaces across the empire, and the Spaniards also stripped the golden plates from the great temple at Cusco. By the usual estimate they melted about thirteen thousand pounds of gold and twice as much silver. A fifth went to the king, and the rest was shared out among the men. Even a foot soldier came away with a fortune. While Atahualpa was still a prisoner, his brother Huáscar was put to death, and Spanish sources say he gave the order. At his trial the charges included that killing and plotting against the Spaniards. The sentence was to be burned alive. At the last moment Atahualpa accepted baptism, and he was strangled instead.

WHY WAS IT SO EASY?

Pizarro's victory looks like Cortés's, but there are important differences. Pizarro had no large native army at his side at first. What he had was a plan that took advantage of how the Inca state worked. All authority in the empire flowed from one man. When the Spaniards seized Atahualpa, they took the head of the whole system. His generals hesitated, because they did not want to risk the emperor's life, and each day of hesitation helped the invaders.

The Spaniards reached Cusco in November 1533 and took it without a major fight. Many nobles who had backed Huáscar saw the newcomers as allies against Atahualpa's faction, and for the moment they were. Pizarro placed Manco, another son of Huayna Capac, on the throne as a ruler who would do what he was told. Manco soon found out what that meant. The conquerors treated him as a captive, took what they wanted from his people, and ignored his complaints. An emperor who is a prisoner in his own palace has little to lose.

Even so, the conquest was neither quick nor complete. Peoples who had been conquered by the Inca, such as the Cañari and the Chachapoya, resented their rulers and helped the Spaniards. But in 1536 Manco Inca, a brother of Atahualpa, whom the Spaniards had made their puppet, escaped and raised a huge army. He besieged Cusco for months, and the Spaniards barely held on. Manco's heirs kept a small Inca state alive in the mountains until 1572. Again we can see contingency at work. A united Inca army, a better-guarded square, or an emperor who stayed away from Cajamarca might have turned the whole story around.

    THE MOUNTAIN OF SILVER

    The Spaniards fought each other over the spoils, and Pizarro himself was murdered in 1541. Inca resistance went on for decades, until the last Inca leader was executed in 1572. By then something far larger than the gold of Cajamarca had been found. In 1545 a rich vein of silver was discovered in a mountain at Potosí, high in the Andes in what is now Bolivia. Within fifty years the barren town had grown to some 160,000 people, a city as large as London or Paris.

    The leader executed in 1572 was Túpac Amaru, the last ruler of the Inca state in the mountains. The man who ordered it was the same official who reorganized the mines. Potosí sat more than thirteen thousand feet above the sea, where almost nothing grows. Grain, cloth, wine, and mules all had to be hauled up to it from far away, and the mountain fed a whole trading economy across the Andes.

    Here is how the mercury process worked. Water-driven mills crushed the ore to a fine powder. The powder was mixed with water, salt, and mercury and trampled by workers or animals for days. The silver clung to the mercury and formed a paste. When the paste was heated, the mercury boiled away and left the silver behind. The method had been developed in Mexico, and it let miners use ore that was too poor to smelt. It also needed a steady supply of mercury, which came mostly from a mine at Huancavelica in Peru, where the same kind of forced draft sent men underground. Some highland villages emptied as families moved away to escape the draft. The mine that fed Spain was fed in turn by the labor of people who had never agreed to it.

    A RICH CROWN, A POOR KINGDOM

    The silver traveled in fleets across the Atlantic to Spain, where the Crown took a fifth of it. Some of it crossed the Pacific on Spanish ships to buy Chinese silk and porcelain. Spain's kings, Charles V and then Philip II, spent it on armies. They fought the Protestants, the Ottoman Turks, and the rebels you will meet next week. The money never stayed. The Crown borrowed against future shipments and went bankrupt again and again, in 1557, 1575, and 1596 among other years. Prices rose across Europe, because there was suddenly more silver chasing the same goods.

    Spanish mints struck much of the silver into coins called pieces of eight, each worth eight small units called reales. Merchants accepted them from London to Manila. After 1571 Spanish ships sailed regularly from Mexico across the Pacific to Manila, where Chinese traders sold silk and porcelain and wanted silver in return. Bankers in Genoa and Augsburg lent the king money against the fleets. A fleet that was late, or lost to a storm or to pirates, could leave the king unable to pay what he owed. The bankruptcies came from that gap between promises and ships.

    This reveals a hard truth about the first-order problem of prosperity. A country gets rich by producing things, not by digging up money. Spain's nobles looked down on trade and craft work, and its kings spent bullionBUL-yunGold or silver in bars or lumps, valued by weight rather than as coins. See the glossary box. on wars instead of workshops and roads. Plutarch would have recognized the pattern. A people can be dazzled by treasure and neglect the habits that make a nation strong. Meanwhile, in the cold northern provinces of Philip's own empire, merchants and sailors were learning how to grow rich without a single mine. That is where we go next.
