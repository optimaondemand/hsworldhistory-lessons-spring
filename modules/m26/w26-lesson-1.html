🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

The National Assembly had a king who gave way and a country on the edge. In the next two years its deputies ended the privileges of the Old Regime, wrote a declaration of rights, and tried to build a constitution. Today you will see why a revolution that set out to limit a king did not stay limited.

PARIS ON EDGE

In early July 1789 Louis XVI moved thousands of soldiers, many of them foreign regiments, toward Paris. On July 11 he dismissed Necker, the minister whom the public trusted most. Parisians feared that the king meant to dissolve the Assembly by force. They were also hungry, since bread was still dear. On July 12 a young journalist named Camille Desmoulins leapt onto a table in a garden cafe and called the crowd to arms.

On the morning of July 14 Parisians took thousands of muskets from the Invalides, a soldiers' hospital. They still needed gunpowder, and some of it was stored in the Bastille. This old fortress, with eight towers, was used as a prison and stood as a symbol of the king's power.

THE BASTILLE, JULY 14

Later that morning a crowd of shopkeepers, craftsmen, and workers gathered at the fortress. Its governor, the Marquis de Launay, had only about a hundred defenders. Talks dragged on. Then shots were fired, and the crowd, joined by mutinous French Guards with cannon, forced the garrison to give up. About a hundred of the attackers died. De Launay was seized and killed, and his head was carried through the streets on a pike. Inside the prison the crowd found only seven prisoners.

The Bastille held little of military value, but it mattered as a symbol. When the king heard the news, a famous story says, he asked if it was a revolt. The answer was "No, Sire, it is a revolution." Whether or not that line is true, soldiers were wavering, Paris belonged to its own militia, and the king had lost control of his capital. Louis then returned Necker to office and accepted the new red, white, and blue cockade, the badge of the Revolution.

THE GREAT FEAR AND THE NIGHT OF AUGUST 4

News of Paris spread. In the countryside a wave of panic called the Great Fear swept the villages, as rumors said that hired bandits were on the way. Peasants armed themselves, and some attacked manor houses and burned records of feudal duesFYOO-dul DYOOZPayments and services that peasants owed to a landlord, often a noble, under old customs. See the glossary box.. Frightened by the unrest, the Assembly met on the night of August 4. Noble after noble stood up to give up privileges, and in a few hours the deputies abolished the tithe, many feudal dues, and the special rights of nobles, clergy, towns, and provinces. In law, the Old Regime was over.

Note how much this took. The nobles did not give up everything. Many dues were turned into payments that peasants had to buy out. Many peasants, who had expected much more, refused to pay. But the principle was now clear: all French people would stand equal before the law and share the burden of taxes.

THE RIGHTS OF MAN

On August 26 the Assembly approved the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. The Marquis de Lafayette, a hero of the American war, helped shape it, and he had shown his draft to his friend Thomas Jefferson. The Declaration said that men are born and remain free and equal in rights. It named liberty, property, safety, and resistance to oppression as natural rightsNACH-uh-rul RYTSRights that people are said to have by nature, such as life and liberty, rather than by gift of a government. See the glossary box.. It said that power belongs to the nation, and that the law should be the same for everyone. It guarded freedom of speech and religion, and it said that every man is innocent until proven guilty.

You can hear Locke and the American Declaration in these words, and also Rousseau, who held that law should express the will of the people. But the document contained tensions. It said "man" and meant men. Women could not vote, and the writer Olympe de Gouges published a Declaration of the Rights of Woman in 1791 to make the point. Slavery continued in France's colonies. Protestants won full rights in 1789 and Jews won them in 1791, but only after fierce debate.

THE MARCH OF THE WOMEN

Bread was still scarce, and the king still delayed signing the new laws. On October 5 thousands of Parisian women marched twelve miles in the rain to Versailles. They demanded bread and an answer from the king. A crowd forced its way into the palace the next morning, and several royal guards were killed. Lafayette arrived with the National Guard, and the royal family was escorted to Paris. The king had become a prisoner in all but name, and the Assembly followed him there.

BUILDING A NEW ORDER

Over the next two years the deputies remade France. They replaced the old provinces with 83 equal departments and swept away the guilds. They also took over Church lands to pay the government's debts. In 1790 the Civil Constitution of the Clergy made priests into paid officials, elected by voters, and required them to swear an oath to the nation. About half of the priests refused, and the pope condemned the measure. Faithful Catholics, including many peasants who had cheered the end of the tithe, began to turn against the Revolution.

The deputies disagreed about the king. The Constitution of 1791 made France a limited monarchy with a single elected Assembly. THE FLIGHT TO VARENNES

In June 1791 Louis, disguised as a servant, slipped out of Paris with his family and drove for the frontier. At the small town of Varennes a postmaster recognized him and raised the alarm, and the family was brought back. Whatever the king had intended, most French people now believed that he had tried to desert the Revolution. A constitution that depended on the king's honest cooperation had lost its foundation. Yet the Assembly pretended that he had been kidnapped, and in September Louis swore to the new constitution.

So the moderate settlement of 1791 had been made with a king who did not trust it and with revolutionaries who did not trust him. Could it have lasted? That question is for you to weigh this week.
