🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

The National Assembly had a king who gave way and a country on the edge. In the next two years its deputies ended the privileges of the Old Regime, wrote a declaration of rights, and tried to build a constitution. Today you will see why a revolution that set out to limit a king did not stay limited.

PARIS ON EDGE

In late June some of the French Guards, the city's own regiment, had refused to fire on crowds, and a crowd had freed several who were jailed for it. In early July 1789 Louis XVI moved thousands of soldiers, many of them foreign regiments, toward Paris. On July 11 he dismissed Necker, the minister whom the public trusted most. Parisians feared that the king meant to dissolve the Assembly by force. They were also hungry, since bread was still dear. Necker was loved because he was thought to be the man who might feed them. On July 12 a young journalist named Camille Desmoulins leapt onto a table in a garden cafe and called the crowd to arms. The garden belonged to the king's cousin, so the police could not enter, which made it a place to speak freely. People pinned green leaves to their hats as a badge. By the next day church bells were ringing across Paris, and the city's electors were forming a citizen militia.

Crowds spent the night of July 12 burning many of the customs posts that ringed the city, where a tax was collected on every cart of food and wine that came in. On July 13 they broke into the monastery of Saint-Lazare and carried off wagonloads of grain. The posts and the grain showed what the crowd was angry about, and it was not only the king's soldiers.

On the morning of July 14 Parisians took thousands of muskets from the Invalides, a soldiers' hospital. They still needed gunpowder, and some of it was stored in the Bastille. This old fortress, with eight towers, was used as a prison and stood as a symbol of the king's power. Its guns also pointed down the street toward a poor part of the city.

THE BASTILLE, JULY 14

Later that morning a crowd of shopkeepers, craftsmen, and workers gathered at the fortress. Its governor, the Marquis de Launay, had only about a hundred defenders, many of them old soldiers. Delegates went in to ask for the gunpowder, and talks dragged on through the afternoon. Then shots were fired, and the crowd, joined by mutinous French Guards with cannon, forced the garrison to give up. About a hundred of the attackers died. De Launay was seized and killed, and his head was carried through the streets on a pike. Inside the prison the crowd found only seven prisoners: four forgers, two men judged insane, and a young nobleman jailed at his family's request.

The end came quickly. Attackers who had pushed into the outer courtyard came under fire from the walls, and when the French Guards hauled up their cannon, de Launay saw that the fortress could not hold. Some accounts say he thought of blowing up the powder stored inside, which would have wrecked the whole neighborhood, and that his own officers talked him out of it. He surrendered on a promise that his men would not be harmed. The promise did not hold.

The Bastille held little of military value, but it mattered as a symbol. That day the king wrote one word in his hunting diary: "Nothing." He meant that he had killed no game. When he heard the news that night, a famous story says, he asked if it was a revolt. The answer, from a nobleman, was "No, Sire, it is a revolution." Whether or not that line is true, soldiers were wavering, Paris belonged to its own militia, and the king had lost control of his capital. Louis then returned Necker to office and accepted the new red, white, and blue cockade, the badge of the Revolution. On July 17 he drove into Paris, where he was handed the cockade at the city hall. His younger brother, the Count of Artois, and a number of other great nobles had already left France, the first of many. Within days workers began to pull the Bastille down, and the contractor sold its stones as souvenirs, some of them carved into small models of the fortress.

THE GREAT FEAR AND THE NIGHT OF AUGUST 4

News of Paris spread. In the countryside a wave of panic called the Great Fear swept the villages, as rumors said that hired bandits were on the way. Village bells rang, and men took up pitchforks and went out on the roads to meet bandits who were never found. Peasants armed themselves, and some attacked manor houses and burned records of feudal duesFYOO-dul DYOOZPayments and services that peasants owed to a landlord, often a noble, under old customs. See the glossary box.. The dues were many. A lord's pigeons could eat a peasant's seed, and the peasant was not allowed to kill them. Frightened by the unrest, the Assembly met on the night of August 4. It began when a liberal noble, the Viscount de Noailles, proposed giving up the old dues. Noble after noble stood up to give up privileges, and in a few hours the deputies abolished the tithe, many feudal dues, and the special rights of nobles, clergy, towns, and provinces. The deputies also ended the sale of government offices, the practice you read about in Week 23. In law, the Old Regime was over.

The scene was more planned than it looked. A small group of liberal deputies, some of them nobles, had prepared the proposal in advance, hoping to calm the countryside by giving up what they could no longer keep. Then the mood in the hall ran away with them. In the days that followed, the deputies argued over how to turn that night's enthusiasm into law, and the decrees of mid-August kept some dues alive and made peasants pay to be rid of others.

Note how much this took. The nobles did not give up everything. Many dues were turned into payments that peasants had to buy out. Many peasants, who had expected much more, refused to pay. But the principle was now clear: all French people would stand equal before the law and share the burden of taxes.

THE RIGHTS OF MAN

On August 26 the Assembly approved the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. The Marquis de Lafayette, a hero of the American war, helped shape it, and he had shown his draft to his friend Thomas Jefferson. The Declaration had seventeen short articles. It said that men are born and remain free and equal in rights. It named liberty, property, safety, and resistance to oppression as natural rightsNACH-uh-rul RYTSRights that people are said to have by nature, such as life and liberty, rather than by gift of a government. See the glossary box.. It said that power belongs to the nation, and that the law should be the same for everyone. Its sixth article called law the expression of the general will, which is Rousseau's phrase. It guarded freedom of speech and religion, and it said that every man is innocent until proven guilty.

Two of its articles show what the deputies were reaching for. Article 1 reads, "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights. Social distinctions may be founded only upon the general good." Article 16 reads, "Any society in which the guarantee of rights is not assured, or the separation of powers not determined, has no constitution at all." (Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, 1789, Articles 1 and 16, in a standard English translation.) Where have you met the second idea before?

You can hear Locke and the American Declaration in these words, and also Rousseau, who held that law should express the will of the people. But the document contained tensions. It said "man" and meant men. Women could not vote, and the writer Olympe de Gouges published a Declaration of the Rights of Woman in 1791 to make the point. Slavery continued in France's colonies. Protestants won full rights in 1789 and Jews won them in 1791, but only after fierce debate.

THE MARCH OF THE WOMEN

Bread was still scarce, and the king still delayed signing the new laws. On October 5 thousands of Parisian women, many of them market women, marched twelve miles in the rain to Versailles. They demanded bread and an answer from the king. They are said to have called for the baker, the baker's wife, and the baker's boy, meaning the king, the queen, and their son. That evening the king accepted the August decrees and the Declaration, but the crowd stayed. A crowd forced its way into the palace the next morning, and several royal guards were killed. Lafayette arrived with the National Guard, and the royal family was escorted to Paris. The queen stepped onto a balcony before the crowd, and Lafayette kissed her hand. The royal family was lodged at the Tuileries, an old and neglected palace in the city. The king had become a prisoner in all but name, and the Assembly followed him there.

The move to Paris had consequences. At Versailles the king and the deputies had been twelve miles from the city. Now they lived in the middle of it, within reach of its crowds, its clubs, and its newspapers. Once the Declaration protected the press, hundreds of new papers appeared in Paris, and writers such as Marat and Desmoulins used them to name enemies and rouse readers. Every vote the Assembly took could be read, argued over, and shouted about in the street by the next morning.

A YEAR AFTER THE BASTILLE

On July 14, 1790, a year after the Bastille, hundreds of thousands of people gathered in the rain on the Champ de Mars in Paris to celebrate. Lafayette led the National Guard in an oath of loyalty to the nation, the law, and the king, and Louis swore to keep the constitution that the Assembly was writing. Many people wept. For a day, it looked as though the Revolution might succeed without breaking the monarchy.

BUILDING A NEW ORDER

Over the next two years the deputies remade France. They replaced the old provinces with 83 equal departments, drawn so that every town lay within a day's ride of its center, and swept away the guilds. A law of 1791 also forbade workers' associations and strikes. They also took over Church lands to pay the government's debts, a step that a bishop, Talleyrand, had proposed. The state issued paper notes backed by the value of that land. At first the notes paid off creditors, and soon they circulated as money. In 1790 the Civil Constitution of the Clergy made priests into paid officials, elected by voters, and required them to swear an oath to the nation. About half of the priests refused, and the pope condemned the measure. Faithful Catholics, including many peasants who had cheered the end of the tithe, began to turn against the Revolution.

The deputies disagreed about the king. The Constitution of 1791 made France a limited monarchy with a single elected Assembly. The deputies had argued for days over whether he should have an absolute veto, and the delay was a compromise. Only men who paid a certain amount in taxes could vote. The Assembly called those who qualified active citizens and everyone else passive citizens, and roughly two men in three qualified. Outside the Assembly, political clubs grew, and the most famous was the JacobinJAK-uh-binA member of a political club that began in Paris in 1789 and later became the leading voice of the most radical revolutionaries. See the glossary box. club, which met in a former convent in Paris and soon had branches across France. The name came from the Dominican friars, called Jacobins in France, who had once lived there.

THE FLIGHT TO VARENNES

In June 1791 Louis, disguised as a servant, slipped out of Paris at midnight with his family and drove for the frontier. The queen traveled as a governess. They left behind a written protest against the Revolution's changes. The heavy carriage ran hours behind schedule, and the cavalry that had been posted along the road to protect them gave up waiting and withdrew. The family was held overnight in a grocer's house in Varennes until guards arrived from Paris. At the small town of Varennes the alarm had been raised by a postmaster who recognized him, and the family was brought back. Whatever the king had intended, most French people now believed that he had tried to desert the Revolution. A constitution that depended on the king's honest cooperation had lost its foundation. Yet the Assembly pretended that he had been kidnapped, and in September Louis swore to the new constitution. The Assembly then dissolved itself. Robespierre had persuaded the deputies to vote that none of them could sit in the next Assembly, so the new legislature met in October 1791 without any of the men who had learned the work of making laws. Mirabeau, the most gifted of them, had died in April. He had been secretly advising the king.

Varennes also split the revolutionaries. On July 17, 1791, a crowd gathered on the Champ de Mars to sign a petition calling for the king to be removed. The Assembly had declared martial law, and Lafayette's National Guard fired on the crowd and killed dozens of people. A Revolution that had begun with soldiers refusing to fire on Parisians had now fired on them. After that day moderates and radicals trusted each other less, and the Jacobin club, which had been united around the petition, broke apart over it.

So the moderate settlement of 1791 had been made with a king who did not trust it and with revolutionaries who did not trust him. Could it have lasted? That question is for you to weigh this week.
