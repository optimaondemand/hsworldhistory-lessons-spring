🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

By 1793 the Revolution that began with a declaration of rights was beheading its enemies by the hundreds. Today you will see how war, fear, and a belief in civic virtue led a republic to the Terror, and how it ended with the fall of the man who had embodied it.

WAR AND THE END OF THE MONARCHY

In April 1792 France declared war on Austria. Many revolutionaries hoped to spread liberty, and the king and queen quietly hoped that France would lose. France did lose at first, and when Prussia joined Austria, a Prussian commander, the Duke of Brunswick, threatened to destroy Paris if the royal family were harmed. Parisians were furious. On August 10, 1792, National Guards and a crowd of working people that people were beginning to call sans-culottessahn koo-LOTA French word meaning "without knee breeches." Volunteers from Marseille marched in singing a song that Paris named for them, the Marseillaise. The king's Swiss Guards, about six hundred men, defended the palace, and most of them died. Louis took refuge with the Assembly, which suspended him. In September, word came that the border fortress of Verdun had fallen, and Paris feared that enemies in its jails would rise as volunteers left for the front. A frightened Paris massacred more than a thousand prisoners.

The song had been written in April 1792, in Strasbourg, by an army captain named Claude Joseph Rouget de Lisle, as a marching song for the Army of the Rhine. Volunteers from Marseille sang it all the way to Paris, which is how it got its name. Its words call citizens to arms against tyrants and invaders, and it later became the national anthem of France.

On September 20 the French army held firm against the Prussians at Valmy. It was mostly an artillery duel, and the French shouted "Long live the nation" as the Prussians drew back. The German poet Goethe, who was with the Prussian army, later wrote that a new age in the history of the world began that day. The next day a new body, the National Convention, met and abolished the monarchy, and France became a republic.

THE KING ON TRIAL

The Convention put Louis on trial for treason. Papers found in a hidden safe in the Tuileries showed that he had been in secret contact with France's enemies. The safe had been made with the help of the locksmith who had taught the king his hobby, and the locksmith told the authorities where it was. Most deputies voted that he was guilty. The vote for death was close, and on January 21, 1793, Louis XVI went to the guillotine. His last words, a witness said, were a plea that his blood not be used against France. Drums drowned out the rest.

Louis was allowed lawyers. One of them was Malesherbes, the former royal censor who had warned Diderot before a police search, by then an old man. The Convention found the king guilty almost without dissent. The sentence split the deputies, and the margin for death was only a few dozen votes. Many who voted for death were frightened more than bloodthirsty, since a living king would be a rallying point for every enemy of the Republic. Malesherbes was himself guillotined in 1794.

The execution horrified the monarchs of Europe. Britain, Spain, and the Dutch Republic joined Austria and Prussia against France. At home, in the western region called the Vendée, peasants rose in revolt in March 1793. They resented the order to send young men into the army, a call for 300,000 men, and the attacks on their priests. The war that followed was savage on both sides. Historians put the dead at well over a hundred thousand.

It is early 1793. The king has been executed. Britain, Spain, and the Dutch Republic have joined Austria and Prussia against France, and peasants in the Vendée are in revolt.

A government fighting invasion and civil war at once needs to act fast, and a small group can act faster than a large assembly. But power given for an emergency is easy to hand over and hard to take back, and a small body with great power faces few checks. To make the center's orders count, the Convention sent its own members out to the provinces and the armies as representatives on mission. They carried broad powers and could remove officials, requisition grain, and order arrests. Some used those powers with restraint, and others with great harshness. Each town also had a surveillance committee of local citizens that drew up lists of suspects. A grocer who muttered against the Republic, or a neighbor who held a grudge, could find himself on a list.

Back in July, the radical journalist Marat was stabbed in his bath by Charlotte Corday, a young woman who sympathized with the Girondins, and she was guillotined four days later. Fear grew. Marie Antoinette was executed in October 1793, and so were many leaders of the moderate Girondins. The guillotine, which Dr. Guillotin had proposed in 1789 as a swift and equal way to carry out the death penalty, had first been used in 1792. A young member of the Committee, Saint-Just, aged twenty-six, also served with the armies. Courts in the provinces convicted thousands. In the Vendée and in rebel cities, thousands more were killed, many of them without a trial. In Nantes, a representative of the Convention drowned prisoners in the Loire.

ROBESPIERRE AND THE REPUBLIC OF VIRTUE

The most famous member of the Committee was Maximilien Robespierre, a lawyer from Arras. He lived simply in a rented room in a carpenter's house, refused bribes, and was called "the Incorruptible." He had read Rousseau and admired the citizens of early Rome. His dream was a Republic of Virtue, a nation of citizens who loved the public good more than themselves. He had been an opponent of the death penalty earlier in his career, and in 1791 he had stood in the Assembly to argue for abolishing it. Now he argued that the Republic could not survive unless its enemies were destroyed. Virtue, he said in a speech in February 1794, was the spirit of a republic, and terror was the tool to defend it. He called terror nothing but swift and severe justice.

⚡ WHY THE CHANGE?
Earlier in his career Robespierre was an opponent of the death penalty. By 1794 he defended terror. What best explains the change?

Here the old Classical Republican question comes alive. Polybius and Machiavelli had taught that republics survive on the habits of their citizens. But habits grow slowly, through families, churches, and schools, and they cannot be made by force. A government that tries to compel virtue with the guillotine may crush both virtue and liberty. Remember Rousseau's line that a citizen who refused the general will could be forced to be free. The revolutionaries also tried to remake life itself. They created a new calendar with ten-day weeks, which gave workers one day of rest in ten instead of one in seven, closed churches, turned Notre-Dame into a Temple of Reason, and held festivals to a Supreme Being. On June 8, 1794, Robespierre led one of them as president of the Convention, and some deputies resented the show. Many French people found this alarming.

Robespierre was not a cruel man by temper, and that is what makes his case so troubling. He believed he was defending a pure cause against corrupt enemies. Once a leader decides that disagreement is treason, every critic becomes a traitor, and there is no limit to the list. A free republic needs ways to settle quarrels peacefully, and the Terror had destroyed them.

THE GREAT TERROR

In the spring of 1794 the Terror turned on the revolutionaries themselves. Danton, a gifted speaker who had himself helped set up the Revolutionary Tribunal and who wanted the killing to slow down, was executed in April. Beside him went Camille Desmoulins, the journalist who had called Paris to arms in 1789. In June a new law allowed the Tribunal to convict without witnesses or a defense lawyer. It left only two possible verdicts, acquittal or death. In just six weeks the Paris courts sent about thirteen hundred people to the guillotine. In all, about seventeen thousand people were formally condemned, and many more died in prison or without any trial. Historians still argue about the exact number. Most of those executed were ordinary peasants, craftsmen, and workers, not nobles, and most died in the regions that had risen in revolt, not in Paris.

In Paris the carts carrying the condemned crossed the city every afternoon, and in June 1794 the guillotine was moved to the eastern edge of the city, partly because the neighbors of the old site complained of the smell of blood. Under the new law there were days when fifty people or more were condemned at a single sitting. By one common estimate, about three hundred thousand people were arrested as suspects during the Terror. For many Parisians the Terror was no longer a matter of politics. It was the sound of the carts.

Then the military crisis eased. In June French armies won a major victory at Fleurus in Belgium. The emergency that had justified the Terror seemed to be over, but the killing went on. Deputies began to ask whether they might be next.

Fleurus showed what the new army could do. The Committee had merged the old royal regiments with the volunteers, promoted officers for ability rather than birth, and fed its soldiers by requisition from the countryside. The commander at Fleurus, Jean-Baptiste Jourdan, had been a common soldier before the Revolution. The same government that kept the guillotine busy had also built an army that would soon carry the Revolution far beyond the borders of France.

THERMIDOR

In the weeks before, Robespierre had quarreled with some of his colleagues on the Committee and had stopped coming to its meetings. He was ill and worn out. His colleagues could suspect that he was preparing an attack on them, and he had given them no way to know whom he meant. Fear, in other words, now ran in both directions.

The day before, he had spoken of enemies inside the government and refused to name them, so every deputy wondered whether he was on the list. Some of those who feared that they were met that night. Fearful deputies shouted him down. One is said to have cried out that the blood of Danton choked him. He was arrested along with his closest supporters. The Paris city government tried to free him, but the Convention declared them outlaws. Thousands of the city's National Guards gathered outside the city hall to defend him, but they were given no clear orders. By one account, a summer rainstorm fell, and many drifted home. Soldiers retook them in the night. Robespierre's jaw was shattered by a pistol shot, and historians disagree about whether he shot himself or was shot. On July 28 Robespierre, who was thirty-six, and about twenty others went to the guillotine, to cheers.

The Terror ended, though not at once. Prisoners were freed, the Jacobin club was closed, and in 1795 France adopted a new constitution. It gave power to a five-man Directory. The Directory was weak and corrupt, and it came to rely on the army to hold on to power. The paper money issued in 1790 lost nearly all its value, and prices soared.

The end of the Terror brought its own violence. Bands of young men, known as the gilded youth, roamed Paris with clubs and hunted down former Jacobins. In the south, in 1795, gangs took revenge on supporters of the Revolution in what became known as the White Terror. A people who had been taught that every quarrel was a matter of life and death did not stop believing it when one man fell.

    WEIGHING THE CAUSES

    What caused the Terror? Ideas mattered. Revolutionaries believed that the people's will was sacred and that virtue could be taught. War and fear mattered, because France was invaded and in revolt. Personalities mattered as well. But none of it was fixed in advance. In 1790 the British statesman Edmund Burke warned that a revolution built on abstract principles would end in the rule of some popular general. Within a few years it began to look as if he was right. Next week you will meet the man who proved him right.
