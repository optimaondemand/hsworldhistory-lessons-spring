🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

By 1793 the Revolution that began with a declaration of rights was beheading its enemies by the hundreds. Today you will see how war, fear, and a belief in civic virtue led a republic to the Terror, and how it ended with the fall of the man who had embodied it.

WAR AND THE END OF THE MONARCHY

In April 1792 France declared war on Austria. Many revolutionaries hoped to spread liberty, and the king and queen quietly hoped that France would lose. France did lose at first, and when Prussia joined Austria, a Prussian commander threatened to destroy Paris if the royal family were harmed. Parisians were furious. THE KING ON TRIAL

The Convention put Louis on trial for treason. Papers found in a hidden safe in the Tuileries showed that he had been in secret contact with France's enemies. Most deputies voted that he was guilty. The vote for death was close, and on January 21, 1793, Louis XVI went to the guillotine. His last words, a witness said, were a plea that his blood not be used against France.

The execution horrified the monarchs of Europe. Britain, Spain, and the Dutch Republic joined Austria and Prussia against France. At home, in the western region called the Vendée, peasants rose in revolt in March 1793. They resented the order to send young men into the army and the attacks on their priests.

ROBESPIERRE AND THE REPUBLIC OF VIRTUE

The most famous member of the Committee was Maximilien Robespierre, a lawyer from Arras. He lived simply, refused bribes, and was called "the Incorruptible." He had read Rousseau and admired the citizens of early Rome. His dream was a Republic of Virtue, a nation of citizens who loved the public good more than themselves. He had been an opponent of the death penalty earlier in his career. Now he argued that the Republic could not survive unless its enemies were destroyed. Virtue, he said in a speech in February 1794, was the spirit of a republic, and terror was the tool to defend it.

Here the old Classical Republican question comes alive. Polybius and Machiavelli had taught that republics survive on the habits of their citizens. But habits grow slowly, through families, churches, and schools, and they cannot be made by force. A government that tries to compel virtue with the guillotine may crush both virtue and liberty. The revolutionaries also tried to remake life itself. They created a new calendar with ten-day weeks, closed churches, and held festivals to a Supreme Being. Many French people found this alarming.

Robespierre was not a cruel man by temper, and that is what makes his case so troubling. He believed he was defending a pure cause against corrupt enemies. Once a leader decides that disagreement is treason, every critic becomes a traitor, and there is no limit to the list. A free republic needs ways to settle quarrels peacefully, and the Terror had destroyed them.

THE GREAT TERROR

In the spring of 1794 the Terror turned on the revolutionaries themselves. Danton, a gifted speaker who wanted the killing to slow down, was executed in April. In June a new law allowed the Tribunal to convict without witnesses or a defense lawyer. In just six weeks the Paris courts sent about thirteen hundred people to the guillotine. In all, about seventeen thousand people were formally condemned, and many more died in prison or without any trial. Historians still argue about the exact number.

Then the military crisis eased. In June French armies won a major victory at Fleurus in Belgium. The emergency that had justified the Terror seemed to be over, but the killing went on. Deputies began to ask whether they might be next.

Fearful deputies shouted him down, and he was arrested along with his closest supporters. The Paris city government tried to free him, but the Convention declared them outlaws. Soldiers retook them in the night. On July 28 Robespierre and about twenty others went to the guillotine, to cheers.

The Terror ended, though not at once. Prisoners were freed, the Jacobin club was closed, and in 1795 France adopted a new constitution. It gave power to a five-man Directory. The Directory was weak and corrupt, and it came to rely on the army to hold on to power.

WEIGHING THE CAUSES

What caused the Terror? Ideas mattered. Revolutionaries believed that the people's will was sacred and that virtue could be taught. War and fear mattered, because France was invaded and in revolt. Personalities mattered as well. But none of it was fixed in advance. In 1790 the British statesman Edmund Burke warned that a revolution built on abstract principles would end in the rule of some popular general. Within a few years it began to look as if he was right. Next week you will meet the man who proved him right.
