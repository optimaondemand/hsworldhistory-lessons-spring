Today you will see how the victors redrew the map, why the settlement pleased almost no one, and how a radical party seized Russia while the world was looking elsewhere.

PARIS, 1919

In January 1919 delegates from some thirty countries gathered in Paris. Germany and Russia were not invited. The real decisions were made by four men: President Woodrow Wilson of the United States, Prime Minister David Lloyd George of Britain, Prime Minister Georges Clemenceau of France, and Vittorio Orlando of Italy, who walked out when Italy did not receive the lands it had been promised.

Each came with different aims. Wilson had laid out his hopes in the Fourteen Points of January 1918, which you will read. They called for open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, lower trade barriers, smaller armies, and the self-determinationself-dih-TER-mih-NAY-shunThe principle that each people has the right to choose its own government and its own nation. See the glossary box. of peoples. The last point proposed a League of Nations, to keep peace by collective action. Clemenceau, whose country had lost over a million men and had been invaded twice in fifty years, wanted security above all. Lloyd George had just won an election in which crowds shouted that Germany must pay, yet he also feared pushing Germany too hard.

THE TREATY OF VERSAILLES

Germany was handed the treaty and told to sign it. It did so on June 28, 1919, in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, five years to the day after Sarajevo. The terms were severe. Germany returned Alsace and Lorraine to France and gave land to Poland, which gave Poland a corridor to the sea. Germany lost all its colonies. The coal-rich Saar was placed under League administration for fifteen years, Danzig became a free city, and Austria was forbidden to unite with Germany. Its army was limited to 100,000 men, with no tanks, no military aircraft, and a small navy, and the Rhineland was to remain free of German troops. Article 231, the so-called war guilt clause, said Germany and its allies were responsible for the war and its damage. On that basis, Germany owed reparationsrep-uh-RAY-shunzPayments that a defeated nation is required to make to the winners for the damage caused by a war. See the glossary box., which a commission set in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks, a huge sum.

Germans called the treaty a Diktat, a dictated peace, and almost all of them resented it. The treaty seemed too harsh to Germans, yet the French thought it too weak. Marshal Foch is said to have remarked that this was no peace, but an armistice for twenty years. Think about the dilemma. Germany had not been invaded and its factories were whole, so it remained the strongest nation in Europe by population and industry. The treaty humiliated it without taking from it the power to try again.

A NEW MAP

Other treaties dealt with Germany's allies. The empire of Austria-Hungary fell apart, and in its place stood Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, and Yugoslavia, with parts going to Poland and Romania. Poland came back onto the map, and Finland, Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania were born from the old Russian lands. The principle of self-determination was applied unevenly. About three million Germans found themselves in Czechoslovakia, and Hungary lost two-thirds of its land. Millions of people were left as minorities in states ruled by a different nation. These grievances would return.

The Ottoman Empire was dismembered, too. A Turkish army under Mustafa Kemal, later called Atatürk, threw off the harsh peace imposed on Turkey and, in 1923, won a fairer treaty and a new republic. The Arab lands of the old empire were handled differently. The League of Nations gave them to Britain and France as mandatesMAN-daytAfter World War I, a territory taken from a defeated power and governed by another nation under the League of Nations, until it was ready to govern itself. See the glossary box., to be governed until the peoples were ready to rule themselves. Britain took Iraq, Transjordan, and Palestine, and France took Syria and Lebanon. Some Arab leaders, who had been promised more during the war, were bitterly disappointed.

A LEAGUE WITHOUT TEETH

Wilson held the League of Nations to be the heart of the treaty. But the United States Senate refused to approve it. Senator Henry Cabot Lodge and others feared the League might commit America to wars it had not chosen. Wilson toured the country to rally support and suffered a stroke in October 1919. The Senate rejected the treaty in 1920. Germany would join in 1926, and the Soviet Union in 1934, but Italy and Japan would later walk out. Without America, and with Germany and Russia outside it at the start, the League had no army and no power to force its decisions. Britain and France were weakened by the war and unwilling to enforce the peace alone.

LENIN'S RUSSIA

While Paris argued, Russia was in flames. Last week you saw the BolsheviksBOHL-shuh-vikFrom the Russian word for "majority." Vladimir Lenin was a patient, ruthless organizer who believed a small, disciplined party should lead the working class. He argued that workers left alone would only seek better wages, and that a party of dedicated, professional revolutionaries had to guide them. The party took its authority from the councils of workers and soldiers, known as sovietsSOH-vee-etA Russian word meaning "council." In 1917 it named the councils of workers and soldiers that arose across Russia, and later it named the whole Soviet state. See the glossary box.. When elections in January 1918 gave the Bolsheviks only about a quarter of the seats in a new assembly, Lenin dissolved it. A secret police, the Cheka, soon hunted opponents. In July 1918 Bolshevik guards shot the former tsar, Nicholas II, and his family.

A civil war between the Reds and the Whites, a mixed group of officers, liberals, and monarchists, lasted until 1921. The Whites were divided and had no common cause, while the Reds held the central cities and railways. Britain, France, the United States, and Japan sent troops at various times, but not enough to matter. Famine and the civil war killed millions. In 1921 sailors at Kronstadt, once heroes of the revolution, rose against the Bolsheviks, and the revolt was crushed. Lenin, shaken, retreated from the harshest policies and allowed small private trade under the New Economic Policy. In 1922 the Bolsheviks proclaimed the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics.

Recall that Marx expected revolution in the most industrial countries. It had come in a land of peasants, because war had broken the old government and a small, determined party was ready to seize the moment. Lenin died in January 1924. Who would succeed him, and what kind of state would he build? Those questions are the next lesson's story.
