🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

In 1920 Europe had more democracies than it ever had before. By 1939 many of them had given way to dictators. Today you will meet three of those men, see how they gained power, and ask how far their rise was a matter of conditions and how far a matter of choices.

WEIMAR GERMANY

After the Kaiser fell, Germany became a republic with a constitution written in the city of Weimar. It had a parliament, a president, and wide freedoms. But many Germans saw it as the government that had signed the hated treaty. In 1923 Germany fell behind on reparationsrep-uh-RAY-shunzPayments that a defeated nation is required to make to the winners for the damage caused by a war. See the glossary box., and French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr, the coal and steel heartland. The government printed money to pay striking workers, and hyperinflationHY-per-in-FLAY-shunA rapid and extreme rise in prices that makes money almost worthless. See the glossary box. followed. A loaf of bread cost billions of marks. The savings of the middle class vanished.

Recovery followed. American loans under the Dawes Plan of 1924 eased the burden, and the foreign minister Gustav Stresemann made peace with France at Locarno in 1925. For a few years Germany seemed to be stable. But the republic stood on borrowed money and borrowed time.

THE GREAT DEPRESSION

In October 1929 the American stock market crashed. Banks failed, and American lenders called in their loans from abroad. World trade shrank as nations raised tariffs to protect their own industries. By 1932 about one American worker in four had no job. In Germany roughly six million were out of work. Governments in every democracy struggled to respond. Britain and the United States kept their constitutions and tried new policies. In Germany and several other countries, frightened citizens began to listen to people who promised strong action.

It is 1932. Banks are failing, trade is shrinking, and about one worker in four has no job. You lead a democratic government.

Britain and the United States kept their constitutions and tried new policies. In Germany the republic already stood on borrowed money, and the collapse in the value of money in 1923 had wiped out the savings of the middle class. Hard times did not push every country the same way. Frightened people did listen to those who promised strong action, but what happened next still depended on choices made by particular leaders, which is the question this lesson keeps asking.
STALIN: A REVOLUTION FROM ABOVE

In the Soviet Union, Lenin's successor was Joseph Stalin, a cautious-looking man who held the dull-sounding post of General Secretary of the party. He used it to build a following, and by 1928 he had outmaneuvered the more brilliant Leon Trotsky, who was later exiled and, in 1940, murdered in Mexico on Stalin's order. Stalin then launched a Five-Year Plan to turn the Soviet Union into an industrial power at breakneck speed. New steel towns such as Magnitogorsk rose out of the plains, and heavy industry grew very fast.

The price was paid by the peasants. Under collectivizationkuh-lek-tih-vih-ZAY-shunThe Soviet policy of taking farmland and animals from peasant families and putting them into large state-run farms. See the glossary box., the state seized their farms and herds and combined them into large collective farms. The wealthier peasants, called kulaks, were labeled enemies, and many were shot or deported to camps. Peasants killed their animals instead of surrendering them. Between 1932 and 1933 a terrible famine struck Ukraine, Kazakhstan, and southern Russia, and millions starved while the state continued to take grain. Historians estimate that five to seven million died in the whole Soviet Union. Then, in the Great Purge of 1936 to 1938, Stalin turned on the party itself. Show trials forced old Bolsheviks to confess to imaginary crimes, and hundreds of thousands were shot. Millions more went to the labor camps of the Gulag. MUSSOLINI: THE FIRST FASCIST

Italy was on the winning side but felt cheated, and many Italians spoke of a mutilated victory. Prices rose, strikes spread, and the government seemed helpless. Benito Mussolini, a former socialist journalist, formed a movement in 1919 that took its name from the fasces, the bundle of rods and an axe carried by Roman officials. His Blackshirts beat up socialists and broke strikes. This movement is known as fascismFASH-iz-umA political movement that puts the nation and its leader above the individual, rules through one party, and uses force against opponents. See the glossary box.. It praised the nation above the individual, rejected democracy, and glorified action and war.

In October 1922 the Fascists announced a March on Rome. The army could have stopped them, but King Victor Emmanuel III chose not to call it out, and he asked Mussolini to form a government. A different decision by one man might have ended Fascism that week. By 1926 Mussolini had banned other parties and taken the title DuceDOO-chayAn Italian word meaning "leader." It was the title Benito Mussolini took for himself. See the glossary box.. He made peace with the Church in the Lateran Treaty of 1929, which created Vatican City. In 1935 he invaded Ethiopia, and the League of Nations failed to stop him.

HITLER: THE ROAD TO POWER

Adolf Hitler was born in Austria in 1889. He served as a corporal in the German army, and the defeat of 1918 left him bitter. He joined a small party that became the Nazi Party, and its violent hatred of Jews, communists, and the Versailles treaty became its message. After his failed attempt to seize power in Munich in 1923, he spent months in prison, where he wrote Mein Kampf. He decided to win power by elections.

For years it did not work. In 1928 the Nazis won under three percent of the vote. Then the Depression hit. By July 1932 the party won thirty-seven percent, more than any other, though never a majority. A group of conservatives, including the former chancellor Franz von Papen, thought they could use Hitler and control him. They persuaded President Hindenburg, and on January 30, 1933, Hitler was named chancellor. This was legal, and it was not inevitable. A different decision by a few old men in Berlin might have kept him out.

Hitler moved fast. After a fire destroyed the Reichstag building in February, he persuaded the president to suspend civil liberties. In March the parliament passed the Enabling Act, giving him power to make laws alone. Other parties were banned, unions dissolved, and the press brought under control. The first concentration camp opened at Dachau. When Hindenburg died in August 1934, Hitler took his powers and the title FührerFYOO-rerA German word meaning "leader." It was the title Adolf Hitler took for himself. See the glossary box.. The Nuremberg Laws of 1935, which you will read, took citizenship from German Jews and began the legal persecution that led to catastrophe.

    WHY DID FREE PEOPLES CHOOSE THEM?

    The three men ruled differently, but they shared much: one party, one leader, a secret police, control of the press and schools, and the identification of an enemy within. They took advantage of fear, humiliation, and economic ruin. But in each case the outcome rested on particular decisions, by kings, presidents, and generals who could have acted otherwise. In this week's discussion you will weigh a question that follows: was the Treaty of Versailles too harsh or too weak, and how much does it explain? Think about all the causes you have met, and decide which ones mattered most.
