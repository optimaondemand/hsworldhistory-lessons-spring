🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

In 1789 the richest kingdom in Europe could not pay its bills. Today you will see why France could not fix the problem for twenty years, and how a hailstorm, a bad harvest, and a king's hesitation led to a meeting that France had not held since 1614.

THE BILL FOR AMERICA

Louis XVI became king in 1774 at nineteen. He was kind, devout, and shy, and he loved hunting and tinkering with locks. He was also not trained to rule. His queen, Marie Antoinette, came from Austria, and many French people distrusted her from the start.

Louis chose able ministers, but they met the same wall. The economist Turgot wanted free trade in grain and fewer privileges, and he was dismissed in 1776. The Swiss banker Jacques Necker paid for the American war by borrowing, not by taxing. In 1781 he published an account of the budget that looked healthy because it left out the cost of the war. When that cost was clear, the national debtNASH-uh-nul DETThe total amount of money a government has borrowed and still owes. See the glossary box. had swelled. By 1788 about half of the king's income went just to pay interest.

THE REFORMS THAT FAILED

In 1786 a new minister named Calonne proposed a land tax on every owner of land, with no exemptions. The king called an Assembly of Notables, a hand-picked group of nobles, bishops, and officials, to approve it. They refused. Calonne was dismissed, and his successor Brienne tried again. Some of those who demanded it were honestly seeking reform. Others hoped to protect their own exemptions. The king tried to force the parlements to obey. Riots broke out in Grenoble and elsewhere, and the army was reluctant to act. In August 1788 Louis gave in and promised to call the Estates-General for the next spring. He also brought Necker back.

HAIL AND HUNGER

Then nature added to the crisis. In July 1788 a terrible hailstorm flattened crops across northern France. The harvest failed, grain grew scarce, and the winter of 1788 to 1789 was among the coldest in memory. The price of bread soared. A working family might spend most of its income on bread. By spring many were hungry and angry, in the cities and in the countryside.

Think about how different things might have looked with an ordinary harvest. Reform would still have been hard, but hungry crowds would not have filled the streets. Great events often turn on chances like this, which no minister could control.

WHAT IS THE THIRD ESTATE?

Across France, people wrote lists of complaints to send to the king. Most of them were respectful and asked for fair taxes, regular meetings of the Estates-General, and a written constitution. Few asked to end the monarchy. A priest named Emmanuel Joseph Sieyès wrote a pamphlet that was more daring. It said that the Third Estate was the whole nation, that it had been treated as nothing in politics, and that it now deserved to be something.

The leading question was how to vote. If each estate had one vote, the clergy and nobles could always outvote the commoners two to one. If every deputy voted as an individual, the Third Estate would have the strength to win, because it had as many deputies as the other two combined. The king allowed that doubling, but he left the method of voting undecided. It was a dangerous silence.

VERSAILLES, SPRING 1789

On May 5, 1789, about 1,200 deputies filed into Versailles in a grand ceremony. The Third Estate wore plain black. For weeks nothing happened. The nobles and clergy insisted on voting by estate, and the Third Estate refused to start work on those terms.

On June 17 the deputies of the Third Estate declared themselves the National Assembly, speaking for the nation. Three days later they found their meeting hall locked, so they gathered on a nearby indoor tennis court and swore not to separate until France had a constitution. On June 27 Louis told the nobles and clergy to join them. The king had given way, but he was also quietly moving troops toward Paris and Versailles. Why did he do both?

A KING WHO COULD NOT CHOOSE

Compare the two kingdoms. Britain's ruling class was taxed, sat in Parliament, and could argue its way to a new settlement. France's ruling class was exempt, and the one body that could call everyone together had been silent for 175 years. The Old Regime was strained by debt, but what finally broke it was the unwillingness of the privileged to share the load, and a king who could neither lead reform nor crush resistance. The Old Regime did not fall because of one thing, and a different king, a better harvest, or a steadier minister might have changed the outcome. Next week you will see what happened next.
