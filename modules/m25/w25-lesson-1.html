🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

For most of a century after Louis XIV, Britain and France fought over trade, colonies, and the seas. By 1763 Britain had won almost everything. Today you will see how it happened, and how the bills from that victory, and the revenge that followed, pushed both nations toward crisis.

TWO RIVALS, TWO WAYS OF PAYING

France was the giant. It had about three times as many people as Britain and the largest army in Europe. Britain had the Royal Navy, the protection of an island, and something less obvious: credit. In 1694 the government helped found the Bank of England. Its first subscribers lent the crown about 1.2 million pounds for a war against Louis XIV. Parliament set aside particular taxes to pay the interest, so a lender could point to the tax that would pay him. Lenders learned that Parliament, which controlled taxes, would repay what the nation borrowed. Over time this borrowing grew into the national debtNASH-uh-nul DETThe total amount of money a government has borrowed and still owes. See the glossary box..

Because lenders trusted Parliament, Britain could borrow cheaply and borrow a great deal. The French king had to pay higher interest, because kings had been known to delay payment or cancel their debts. In 1720 a Scottish financier, John Law, had tried to rescue the French treasury with a bank and a company that traded with Louisiana. Its shares soared and then collapsed, and many investors were ruined. Many French people stayed wary of paper money and banks for a long time. You have seen what Louis XIV's wars cost. A war is a test of whether a state can raise money and soldiers quickly, and here Britain had a real advantage. The advantage came from its laws and its habits of trust, not from its size.

There was a second difference. In Britain even great landowners paid a land tax, and their representatives in Parliament voted for it. Officers of the excise collected more on goods such as beer, salt, and tea. Those who gave the money also had a say in how it was spent. In France, as you will see next lesson, the people with the most wealth paid the least, and no regular body of the kingdom met to settle such questions.

A SHOT IN THE FOREST

By 1750 about 1.5 million colonists lived in Britain's thirteen colonies along the Atlantic coast. New France stretched from Quebec up the St. Lawrence River and down the Mississippi to New Orleans, but it had only about 70,000 settlers. Franklin worked out that the American population doubled about every twenty-five years. France had barred Protestants from settling in New France, so the Huguenots whom Louis XIV drove out in 1685 went to other lands, and some of them went to the British colonies. Both sides claimed the Ohio Valley. In 1754 a young Virginia officer named George Washington led a small force into it and was forced to surrender at Fort Necessity. He was twenty-two. In May his men and some Native allies had surprised a French party at a glen in the woods and killed its commander, Jumonville, along with several others. The French said that Jumonville had come in peace with a message. In July a larger French force trapped Washington in a hastily built fort in a rainstorm. He signed the surrender in a language he could not read, and its French text spoke of the "assassination" of Jumonville. That skirmish helped start what Europeans call the Seven Years' War and Americans call the French and Indian War.

Native nations had their own interests and fought on both sides. A French fur trader needed Native hunters, while a British farmer needed their land, and that difference drew many of the nations of the Ohio country toward France. The fighting soon spread to Europe, India, the Caribbean, West Africa, and the oceans in between, so it became the first war that was truly global. At first Britain lost battle after battle. In 1755 an army led by General Braddock was cut to pieces in the woods near the Ohio, and Braddock died of his wounds. The next year the French took Minorca, and the British government had the admiral who failed to save it shot on his own ship. Voltaire, in Candide, joked that the British did this from time to time to encourage the others.

The colonies themselves could not agree on how to meet the danger. In 1754 delegates from several of them met at Albany, New York, to bargain with the Iroquois and to plan a common defense. Benjamin Franklin proposed a union with a grand council for all the colonies, and no colonial assembly would accept it, because each guarded its own powers. His newspaper printed a picture of a snake cut into pieces beneath the words "Join, or Die." The colonies that fought side by side in 1775 had refused to unite in 1754.

PITT AND THE YEAR OF VICTORIES

In 1757 William Pitt took charge of British strategy. He had told a duke that he was sure he could save the country and that no one else could. He believed France could be beaten where it was weakest, at sea and in the colonies. Meanwhile Frederick the Great of Prussia, paid by British money, kept French armies busy in Europe. France had to fight a land war in Germany as well as a sea war, and its money went into both. Pitt borrowed boldly and trusted the nation's credit to carry it. Frederick himself came close to defeat, and he was saved in 1762 when the Russian empress died and her successor, who admired him, made peace.

In 1759 the British won victory after victory. The most famous came at Quebec. General James Wolfe, aged thirty-two, sailed up the St. Lawrence. According to a story, on the way he recited Gray's poem about a country churchyard to his officers and said that he would rather have written it than take Quebec. On the night of September 12 his men climbed a cliff path and formed their lines on the Plains of Abraham, outside the city. By the traditional account, a sentry challenged the boats in the dark, and a British officer who spoke French answered him, and the boats went on. Wolfe himself was ill. The French commander, the Marquis de Montcalm, chose to attack at once instead of waiting for reinforcements. The British held their fire until the French were close. One British volley broke his lines, and the battle was over in about a quarter of an hour. Both generals died. Think about how little it would have taken to change this. A sentry who was not satisfied by the answer, or a Montcalm who waited a day, could have turned the whole campaign. Montreal fell in 1760.

Pitt's plan rested on a blockade. The Royal Navy kept watch on French ports such as Brest and Toulon, which kept the French fleet from reaching its colonies or protecting its trade. French merchant ships that did get out were hunted down, and French overseas trade shrank sharply. A king whose treasury had always needed the customs money from that trade now had less to spend on his armies.

Sea power did as much as soldiers. In November 1759, at Quiberon Bay, off the coast of France, a British fleet destroyed a French fleet that had been gathered to invade Britain. Cut off by the Royal Navy, French forces in Canada and the Caribbean could not be reinforced or supplied.

A COMPANY BECOMES A POWER

In India the French and British East India companies hired Indian soldiers, called sepoysSEE-poyA word from the Persian language for an Indian soldier who served in a European army or in a European company's army. See the glossary box., and trained them in European drill. In 1757 Robert Clive, a company clerk turned officer, led about 3,000 men against a far larger army of the ruler of Bengal, Siraj-ud-Daulah, at Plassey. Clive won, helped by the betrayal of one of the ruler's own commanders. Soon the British company controlled Bengal, one of the richest provinces in India. In 1765 the Mughal emperor granted the Company the right to collect Bengal's taxes. A trading firm was becoming the ruler of millions of people, a strange new kind of power.

The Company collected its taxes through good years and bad. In 1770 the rains failed in Bengal, and the famine that followed killed a very large share of the province's people, in numbers that estimates put in the millions. The Company's tax demand did not fall by much. News of the famine, and of the fortunes that Company men were bringing home, reached London, and in 1773 Parliament passed a law to regulate the Company.

THE TREATY OF PARIS, 1763

The war ended with the Treaty of Paris in 1763. France had to cedeSEEDTo give up land or power to another nation, usually in a treaty. See the glossary box. Canada and its claims east of the Mississippi to Britain. Spain gave up Florida to Britain, and France gave Louisiana to Spain. Spain had joined France in 1762, and a British expedition then captured Havana and Manila. Spain got Havana back by giving up Florida, and France paid Spain for its loss with Louisiana. French power in India was cut down to a few trading towns. France did keep two small sugar islands, Guadeloupe and Martinique. Its ministers judged that sugar was worth more than the snows of Canada, as you learned in Week 21.

Britain now dominated North America, India, and the seas. Pitt himself had left office in 1761, when the new king, George III, would not follow him into war with Spain. Yet Pitt's method had a price. During the war the British national debt almost doubled, to about 130 million pounds.

THE WINNER'S BILL

Peace brought new problems on the frontier. Native nations that had fought alongside the French feared the flood of settlers. In 1763 an Ottawa leader named Pontiac led a rising that captured several British forts west of the mountains. He besieged Detroit through the summer. Many colonists asked why a standing army was needed now that France was gone. London answered with the Proclamation of 1763, which forbade colonists to settle beyond the Appalachian Mountains. Keeping order there meant keeping soldiers there, about ten thousand of them, and soldiers cost money. Many colonists, who had hoped to claim the Ohio Valley, were angry.

Parliament looked for ways to pay. British ministers thought the colonists should help to pay for defending the frontier, since the war had begun there. They passed new taxes, beginning with the Stamp Act of 1765, which required a stamped paper for legal documents, newspapers, and even playing cards. In Boston a crowd attacked the house of the man appointed to sell the stamps, and in other colonies the stamp sellers were pressed to resign, so the stamps often could not be sold. The colonists, who had their own assemblies and prized the rights of Englishmen, replied that they could not be taxed without their own consent. Delegates from nine colonies met in New York that October to say so. Parliament repealed the Stamp Act in 1766, and in the same breath declared that it could make laws for the colonies in all cases. Last week you read Locke's argument about consent. Many colonists had read it too.

British ministers had an answer to the colonists' complaint. They said that every member of Parliament spoke for all British subjects, whether or not those subjects had voted for him, and that the colonists were represented just as the people of Manchester and Birmingham were, towns that sent no members at all. The colonists thought this a thin argument. A member who had never seen Boston, they said, could tax it without ever feeling the tax himself.

The quarrel grew over eleven years. Neither side planned a break. Each took one more step, and then another. In 1767 Parliament put new duties on glass, paper, and tea. In 1770 soldiers fired on a crowd in Boston and killed five people. In 1773 a crowd threw a shipload of tea into Boston harbor. In 1775 fighting broke out at Lexington and Concord. In 1776 the colonies declared independence. You will read their Declaration this week and judge its argument for yourself.

THE LOSER'S REVENGE

France saw its chance. The foreign minister, Vergennes, had long wanted revenge for 1763. In 1778, after the Americans won at Saratoga, the young King Louis XVI joined their cause. Benjamin Franklin, who had been in Paris since 1776 asking for help, was the most celebrated American in France. French ships and soldiers followed. Even earlier, France had been sending guns and gunpowder in secret. Among the volunteers was the Marquis de Lafayette, a French nobleman of nineteen who joined Washington's army. In 1781 the French fleet under Admiral de Grasse and an army led by General Rochambeau helped trap a British force under Lord Cornwallis at Yorktown, Virginia. Cornwallis had been waiting for the Royal Navy to carry him off, and a French fleet turned up in its place. In 1783 Britain accepted American independence.

France had its revenge, but it gained no new land and spent more than a billion livres. Britain lost thirteen colonies yet kept its credit, and under the young prime minister William Pitt the Younger it put its finances back in order. France's king had no Parliament to vouch for his promises and no way to raise money without a fight. The winning side in 1783 was already in trouble. Next lesson you will see how that trouble came to a head.
