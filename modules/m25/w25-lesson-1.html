🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

For most of a century after Louis XIV, Britain and France fought over trade, colonies, and the seas. By 1763 Britain had won almost everything. Today you will see how it happened, and how the bills from that victory, and the revenge that followed, pushed both nations toward crisis.

TWO RIVALS, TWO WAYS OF PAYING

France was the giant. It had about three times as many people as Britain and the largest army in Europe. Britain had the Royal Navy, the protection of an island, and something less obvious: credit. In 1694 the government helped found the Bank of England. Lenders learned that Parliament, which controlled taxes, would repay what the nation borrowed. Over time this borrowing grew into the national debtNASH-uh-nul DETThe total amount of money a government has borrowed and still owes. See the glossary box..

Because lenders trusted Parliament, Britain could borrow cheaply and borrow a great deal. The French king had to pay higher interest, because kings had been known to delay payment or cancel their debts. You have seen what Louis XIV's wars cost. A war is a test of whether a state can raise money and soldiers quickly, and here Britain had a real advantage. The advantage came from its laws and its habits of trust, not from its size.

There was a second difference. In Britain even great landowners paid a land tax, and their representatives in Parliament voted for it. Those who gave the money also had a say in how it was spent. In France, as you will see next lesson, the people with the most wealth paid the least, and no regular body of the kingdom met to settle such questions.

A SHOT IN THE FOREST

By 1750 about 1.5 million colonists lived in Britain's thirteen colonies along the Atlantic coast. New France stretched from Quebec up the St. Lawrence River and down the Mississippi to New Orleans, but it had only about 70,000 settlers. Both sides claimed the Ohio Valley. In 1754 a young Virginia officer named George Washington led a small force into it and was forced to surrender at Fort Necessity. That skirmish helped start what Europeans call the Seven Years' War and Americans call the French and Indian War.

Native nations had their own interests and fought on both sides. The fighting soon spread to Europe, India, the Caribbean, West Africa, and the oceans in between, so it became the first war that was truly global. At first Britain lost battle after battle.

PITT AND THE YEAR OF VICTORIES

In 1757 William Pitt took charge of British strategy. He believed France could be beaten where it was weakest, at sea and in the colonies. Meanwhile Frederick the Great of Prussia, paid by British money, kept French armies busy in Europe. Pitt borrowed boldly and trusted the nation's credit to carry it.

In 1759 the British won victory after victory. The most famous came at Quebec. General James Wolfe, aged thirty-two, sailed up the St. Lawrence. On the night of September 12 his men climbed a cliff path and formed their lines on the Plains of Abraham, outside the city. The French commander, the Marquis de Montcalm, chose to attack at once instead of waiting for reinforcements. One British volley broke his lines. Both generals died. Think about how little it would have taken to change this. A sentry who challenged the boats, or a Montcalm who waited a day, could have turned the whole campaign. Montreal fell in 1760.

A COMPANY BECOMES A POWER

In India the French and British East India companies hired Indian soldiers, called sepoysSEE-poyA word from the Persian language for an Indian soldier who served in a European army or in a European company's army. See the glossary box., and trained them in European drill. In 1757 Robert Clive, a company clerk turned officer, led about 3,000 men against a far larger army of the ruler of Bengal at Plassey. Clive won, helped by the betrayal of one of the ruler's own commanders. Soon the British company controlled Bengal, one of the richest provinces in India. A trading firm was becoming the ruler of millions of people, a strange new kind of power.

THE TREATY OF PARIS, 1763

The war ended with the Treaty of Paris in 1763. France had to cedeSEEDTo give up land or power to another nation, usually in a treaty. See the glossary box. Canada and its claims east of the Mississippi to Britain. Spain gave up Florida to Britain, and France gave Louisiana to Spain. French power in India was cut down to a few trading towns. France did keep two small sugar islands, Guadeloupe and Martinique. Its ministers judged that sugar was worth more than the snows of Canada, as you learned in Week 21.

Britain now dominated North America, India, and the seas. Yet Pitt's method had a price. During the war the British national debt almost doubled, to about 130 million pounds.

THE WINNER'S BILL

Peace brought new problems on the frontier. Native nations that had fought alongside the French feared the flood of settlers. In 1763 an Ottawa leader named Pontiac led a rising that captured several British forts west of the mountains. London answered with the Proclamation of 1763, which forbade colonists to settle beyond the Appalachian Mountains. Keeping order there meant keeping soldiers there, and soldiers cost money. Many colonists, who had hoped to claim the Ohio Valley, were angry.

Parliament looked for ways to pay. British ministers thought the colonists should help to pay for defending the frontier, since the war had begun there. They passed new taxes, beginning with the Stamp Act of 1765. The colonists, who had their own assemblies and prized the rights of Englishmen, replied that they could not be taxed without their own consent. Last week you read Locke's argument about consent. Many colonists had read it too.

The quarrel grew over eleven years. Neither side planned a break. Each took one more step, and then another. In 1776 the colonies declared independence. You will read their Declaration this week and judge its argument for yourself.

THE LOSER'S REVENGE

France saw its chance. In 1778, after the Americans won at Saratoga, the young King Louis XVI joined their cause. French ships and soldiers followed. In 1781 the French fleet under Admiral de Grasse and an army led by General Rochambeau helped trap a British force under Lord Cornwallis at Yorktown, Virginia. In 1783 Britain accepted American independence.

France had its revenge, but it gained no new land and spent more than a billion livres. Britain lost thirteen colonies yet kept its credit, and under the young prime minister William Pitt the Younger it put its finances back in order. France's king had no Parliament to vouch for his promises and no way to raise money without a fight. The winning side in 1783 was already in trouble. Next lesson you will see how that trouble came to a head.
