🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

What makes a government legitimate, and what keeps it from becoming a tyranny? Today five writers give five different answers. Read them as arguments to weigh, not as lists to memorize.

JOHN LOCKE: CONSENT

John Locke lived through the English Civil War and the Glorious Revolution. He spent years in exile in the Dutch Republic and came home in the fleet that carried Mary to England in 1689. That year he published his Two Treatises of Government, without his name on the title page, because an author who attacked kings could be charged with treason. He had written much of it in the 1680s. Its first part answered a writer, Robert Filmer, who had argued that kings inherit the power God gave to Adam. Before there was any government, Locke wrote, people lived in a state of nature under a natural law that every person could understand by reason. Each person had rights to life, liberty, and property. The same year he published An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, which argued that the mind at birth is like blank paper and that all our ideas come from experience.

His book answered a crisis. In June 1688 the wife of King James II, a Catholic, gave birth to a son, and English Protestants saw that a Catholic line of kings might now last for generations. Seven English leaders sent an invitation to James's Protestant son-in-law and nephew, William of Orange, who landed with an army that November. James fled the country. Parliament declared the throne vacant and offered it to William and Mary on terms set out in the Bill of Rights of 1689, which limited what a king could do. Many readers took Locke's argument as a defense of what had just happened, though he had begun writing it earlier.

People set up governments through a social contractSO-shul KON-traktThe idea that a government gets its authority from an agreement among the people it governs. See the glossary box.. They agreed to obey a ruler so that he would protect those rights. If a ruler attacked the rights he was supposed to guard, he lost his claim to obey and the people could resist him. Who judges whether a ruler has gone too far? Locke said that in the end the people must judge, since no higher court stood above a ruler who broke the law. Few of these ideas were new in every detail, but Locke stated them so clearly that later generations treated his book as a handbook of free government. He also helped draft a constitution for the colony of Carolina. That document gave every free man absolute power over his slaves. Set it beside the opening arguments of his Treatises and ask what you make of both.

Locke also wrote a Letter Concerning Toleration. In it he argued that government exists to protect people's lives and property, not to save their souls, and that force cannot make anyone truly believe. His tolerance had limits, since he excluded Catholics and atheists. He also believed that property begins when a person mixes his labor with something in nature, a claim that Smith and many others would later draw on.

MONTESQUIEU: CHECKS

Charles-Louis de Montesquieu was a French noble and judge. He had inherited his judge's office, the kind of office you read about last week, and he later sold it. He traveled for about three years around Europe, much of it in England, where he was elected to the Royal Society. He wrote a witty book about Paris, and then, in 1748, a vast study called The Spirit of the Laws, which took about twenty years. It went through many editions within two years. He compared governments as a scientist compares plants. A republic, he said, runs on virtue, a monarchy on honor, and a despotism on fear. His main fear was that anyone who holds power is tempted to abuse it. His remedy was that power should check power. Decades later, James Madison would call Montesquieu the oracle who is always consulted and cited on this subject.

⚡ DECIDE FIRST
Montesquieu feared that anyone who holds power is tempted to abuse it.
Decide first, and jot your answer in your notebook: if you were designing a government and shared this fear, what would you do about it? Then open to compare.
Montesquieu's remedy was that power should check power. Instead of trusting one good person, he wanted authority divided so that one holder of power could restrain another. He found his model in England, whose government he thought divided its authority among lawmakers, the king, and independent judges. This is the separation of powerssep-uh-RAY-shun uv POW-erzThe idea that a government should divide its authority among separate branches, such as lawmaking, enforcing, and judging, so that no one holds all power. See the glossary box.. In his scheme, one body makes the laws, another carries them out, and a third judges, and each can stop the others. His picture of England was a little too neat, since the king's ministers sat in Parliament, but it was influential. His thinking drew on Polybius, who had praised the mixed constitution of Rome. Montesquieu also argued that laws should suit a people's habits, history, and land. He wrote a book on the Romans that traced their rise to citizen virtue and their fall to ambition and luxury, which is just the kind of lesson that a Classical Republican would draw. His earlier book, the Persian Letters, had mocked Louis XIV's court through the eyes of two imaginary Persian visitors.

Montesquieu put his fear into a sentence that people still quote. In Thomas Nugent's English translation of 1750, he wrote that "every man invested with power is apt to abuse it," and that to prevent this abuse "power should be a check to power." (Montesquieu, The Spirit of the Laws, Book 11, Chapter 4, trans. Thomas Nugent.) He added that even virtue has need of limits. The remark was aimed at kings, but it fits any office, including the offices of a republic.

VOLTAIRE: TOLERATION

François-Marie Arouet, known as Voltaire, was the most famous of all the Enlightenmenten-LYT-un-mentThe movement of the 1700s that held that human reason could be used to improve knowledge, government, and society. See the glossary box. writers. He was witty, vain, and fearless. A nobleman's servants beat him after he insulted their master, and he was thrown into the Bastille. The nobleman refused to fight him, and it was Voltaire who went to prison. He then spent three years in England. There he admired Newton, the freedom of the press, and the mix of religions. His book of letters about England was burned in Paris in 1734. Voltaire spent his last decades at Ferney, a house near the Swiss border, where he could slip across the line if the French authorities moved against him.

Voltaire's cause was toleration. In 1762 a Protestant merchant named Jean Calas was executed in Toulouse on a false charge. He was broken on the wheel. The charge was that he had killed his son to stop the young man from becoming a Catholic, though the evidence suggests that the son took his own life. Voltaire interviewed the family and wrote letter after letter. He spent three years campaigning until the verdict was overturned. His Treatise on Toleration came out in 1763. He was no democrat, and he admired enlightened kings such as Frederick the Great of Prussia. In his story Candide, which appeared in 1759, he mocked people who said that everything is for the best in this best of all possible worlds. The earthquake that destroyed Lisbon in 1755 and killed tens of thousands helped inspire it. He also wrote history and made a point of describing arts and customs along with kings and battles. His battle cry against what he saw as cruelty and superstition in the church was to crush the infamous thing.

Locke and Voltaire both argued for toleration of belief. How far did Locke's own toleration reach?

ROUSSEAU: THE GENERAL WILL

Jean-Jacques Rousseau was born in Geneva, the son of a watchmaker, and his mother died days after his birth. He ran away from his apprenticeship at sixteen. He was an outsider among the philosophesfee-loh-ZOFFA French word meaning "philosophers." It was used for the writers and thinkers of the French Enlightenment. See the glossary box.. In 1750 he won a prize from the academy of Dijon with an essay arguing that the arts and sciences had corrupted morals. Voltaire read his next essay, on inequality, and joked in a letter that it made him want to walk on all fours. Where they praised progress, he worried that civilization had made people vain and unequal. In The Social Contract, published in 1762, he opened with a famous line: "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains."

For Rousseau, a government is legitimate only when it follows the general will, the common good of the whole people, expressed when citizens make their own laws. He separated it from the will of all, which is only the sum of what each person wants for himself. A citizen who refused to obey the general will, he wrote, could be compelled to, and he called this being forced to be free. He admired the citizen virtue of early Rome and of Sparta. Critics ask a hard question: who decides what the general will is? Revolutionaries would later claim to speak for it. Rousseau did not choose how others would use his words. In 1762 French authorities condemned his Social Contract, and he had to flee France. Geneva, the city he was proud to call home, burned it too. He also believed that citizens would only love their country if they shared festivals, schools, and a common education that taught them to put the public good first.

Rousseau had little patience with the English government that Montesquieu admired. In The Social Contract he wrote that the English people thought themselves free but were free only on election day. Once they had chosen their representatives, he said, they were slaves again. A people who let others make their laws had given up the very thing that made them free. He thought that real self-government was possible only in small states, such as the Swiss cantons and the old city-republics he admired.

ADAM SMITH: MARKETS AND MORALS

Adam Smith was a Scottish professor of moral philosophy and a friend of the philosopher David Hume. In the 1760s he traveled in France as a tutor and met Voltaire and the French economists. In 1759 he argued that people feel sympathy for one another and judge their own conduct by imagining how a fair observer would see it. In 1776 he published The Wealth of Nations. In it he described the division of labordih-VIZH-un uv LAY-berA way of producing goods in which each worker does one small step of the work rather than the whole job. See the glossary box.. In a pin factory, he said, ten workers who each do one step can make thousands of pins a day. Smith counted about eighteen separate steps in a pin: one worker draws the wire, another straightens it, a third cuts it, a fourth sharpens the point. Ten workers who each made whole pins might make only a few hundred. By Smith's count, the ten men together could make more than forty thousand pins in a day.

Smith had left his professor's chair at Glasgow in 1764 to tutor a young duke on a tour of France, and the lifetime pension he earned for the work gave him time to write. The Wealth of Nations took about ten years. It was published in March 1776, and the first edition sold out in about six months. Smith later took a post as a customs commissioner in Edinburgh, which let him see for himself the trade rules he had criticized.

Smith attacked mercantilism, the idea you met last week with Colbert, and the trade monopolies you have studied, including great trading companies. He wrote that a nation's wealth lay in what its people could make and buy, not in a pile of gold and silver. He thought that, under just laws, people who pursue their own interest can serve the public good as if guided by an invisible hand. He used that phrase only once in The Wealth of Nations. In the same book he wrote, "It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest." He also criticized slavery as unjust and wasteful. But he took justice, law, and good character for granted. A market without them would not work. Smith was also no flatterer of businessmen. He warned that when merchants of the same trade meet, the conversation usually ends in a plot against the public or a scheme to raise prices.

Tap these books and arguments in the order they appeared, earliest first.
    WHO IS RIGHT?

    Notice how the five answers differ. Locke said that government rests on the consent of the governed and exists to protect rights. Rousseau agreed on consent but placed it in the whole community. Montesquieu doubted that any person or group could be trusted with unchecked power. Voltaire wanted tolerance and justice, and Smith wanted free people working within just laws.

    Try a test case. A government jails a writer for a pamphlet that attacks the ruler. Locke would ask whether the ruler is protecting life, liberty, and property or attacking them. Montesquieu would ask whether the writer was tried by judges who were independent of the ruler. Voltaire would ask whether anyone was harmed by the words. Rousseau would ask whether the law the writer broke was made by the citizens themselves. The five would not reach the same verdict, and the differences are worth seeing.

    Each of them was answering a problem of his own time. Locke wrote against kings who claimed their power from God. Montesquieu wrote against rulers who recognized no check. Voltaire wrote against persecution. Rousseau wrote against vanity and inequality. Smith wrote against monopolies and trade rules. Each of them looked back at the ancient world and drew on it. Each assumed that good laws need good habits to support them. As you meet the American and French Revolutions in the coming weeks, ask which of these ideas helped liberty and which was dangerous in the wrong hands. Nothing in these arguments was settled by the thinkers themselves. Their ideas would be tested by real people, in real crises, with real consequences.
