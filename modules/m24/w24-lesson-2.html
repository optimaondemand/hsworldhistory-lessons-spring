🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

What makes a government legitimate, and what keeps it from becoming a tyranny? Today five writers give five different answers. Read them as arguments to weigh, not as lists to memorize.

JOHN LOCKE: CONSENT

John Locke lived through the English Civil War and the Glorious Revolution. He spent years in exile in the Dutch Republic and came home in the fleet that carried Mary to England in 1689. That year he published his Two Treatises of Government. Before there was any government, Locke wrote, people lived in a state of nature under a natural law that every person could understand by reason. Each person had rights to life, liberty, and property.

People set up governments through a social contractSO-shul KON-traktThe idea that a government gets its authority from an agreement among the people it governs. See the glossary box.. They agreed to obey a ruler so that he would protect those rights. If a ruler attacked the rights he was supposed to guard, he lost his claim to obey and the people could resist him. Few of these ideas were new in every detail, but Locke stated them so clearly that later generations treated his book as a handbook of free government. He also helped draft a constitution for the colony of Carolina.

Locke also wrote a Letter Concerning Toleration. In it he argued that government exists to protect people's lives and property, not to save their souls, and that force cannot make anyone truly believe. His tolerance had limits, since he excluded Catholics and atheists. He also believed that property begins when a person mixes his labor with something in nature, a claim that Smith and many others would later draw on.

MONTESQUIEU: CHECKS

Charles-Louis de Montesquieu was a French noble and judge. He wrote a witty book about Paris, and then, in 1748, a vast study called The Spirit of the Laws. He compared governments as a scientist compares plants. His main fear was that anyone who holds power is tempted to abuse it. His remedy was that power should check power.

He found his model in England, whose government he thought divided its authority among lawmakers, the king, and independent judges. This is the separation of powerssep-uh-RAY-shun uv POW-erzThe idea that a government should divide its authority among separate branches, such as lawmaking, enforcing, and judging, so that no one holds all power. See the glossary box.. His picture of England was a little too neat, but it was influential. His thinking drew on Polybius, who had praised the mixed constitution of Rome. Montesquieu also argued that laws should suit a people's habits, history, and land. He wrote a book on the Romans that traced their rise to citizen virtue and their fall to ambition and luxury, which is just the kind of lesson that a Classical Republican would draw. His earlier book, the Persian Letters, had mocked Louis XIV's court through the eyes of two imaginary Persian visitors.

VOLTAIRE: TOLERATION

François-Marie Arouet, known as Voltaire, was the most famous of all the Enlightenmenten-LYT-un-mentThe movement of the 1700s that held that human reason could be used to improve knowledge, government, and society. See the glossary box. writers. He was witty, vain, and fearless. A nobleman's servants beat him after he insulted their master, and he was thrown into the Bastille. He then spent three years in England. There he admired Newton, the freedom of the press, and the mix of religions.

Voltaire's cause was toleration. In 1762 a Protestant merchant named Jean Calas was executed in Toulouse on a false charge. Voltaire spent three years campaigning until the verdict was overturned. He was no democrat, and he admired enlightened kings such as Frederick the Great of Prussia. In his story Candide he mocked people who said that everything is for the best in this best of all possible worlds. He also wrote history and made a point of describing arts and customs along with kings and battles. His battle cry against what he saw as cruelty and superstition in the church was to crush the infamous thing.

ROUSSEAU: THE GENERAL WILL

Jean-Jacques Rousseau was born in Geneva and was an outsider among the . Where they praised progress, he worried that civilization had made people vain and unequal. In The Social Contract, published in 1762, he opened with a famous line: "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains."

For Rousseau, a government is legitimate only when it follows the general will, the common good of the whole people, expressed when citizens make their own laws. He admired the citizen virtue of early Rome and of Sparta. Critics ask a hard question: who decides what the general will is? Revolutionaries would later claim to speak for it. Rousseau did not choose how others would use his words. He also believed that citizens would only love their country if they shared festivals, schools, and a common education that taught them to put the public good first.

ADAM SMITH: MARKETS AND MORALS

Adam Smith was a Scottish professor of moral philosophy. In 1759 he argued that people feel sympathy for one another and judge their own conduct by imagining how a fair observer would see it. In 1776 he published The Wealth of Nations. In it he described the division of labordih-VIZH-un uv LAY-berA way of producing goods in which each worker does one small step of the work rather than the whole job. See the glossary box.. In a pin factory, he said, ten workers who each do one step can make thousands of pins a day. Ten workers who each made whole pins might make only a few hundred.

Smith attacked mercantilism and the trade monopolies you have studied, including great trading companies. He thought that, under just laws, people who pursue their own interest can serve the public good as if guided by an invisible hand. He also criticized slavery as unjust and wasteful. But he took justice, law, and good character for granted. A market without them would not work. Smith was also no flatterer of businessmen. He warned that when merchants of the same trade meet, the conversation usually ends in a plot against the public or a scheme to raise prices.

WHO IS RIGHT?

Notice how the five answers differ. Locke said that government rests on the consent of the governed and exists to protect rights. Rousseau agreed on consent but placed it in the whole community. Montesquieu doubted that any person or group could be trusted with unchecked power. Voltaire wanted tolerance and justice, and Smith wanted free people working within just laws.

Each of them looked back at the ancient world and drew on it. Each assumed that good laws need good habits to support them. As you meet the American and French Revolutions in the coming weeks, ask which of these ideas helped liberty and which was dangerous in the wrong hands. Nothing in these arguments was settled by the thinkers themselves. Their ideas would be tested by real people, in real crises, with real consequences.
