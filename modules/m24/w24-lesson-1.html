🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

In 1687 a quiet Englishman published a book that explained why planets move and apples fall. Today you will see how Newton's discoveries changed how educated people thought, and how books, coffeehouses, and salons carried new ideas across Europe.

THE MAN WHO WEIGHED THE HEAVENS

Isaac Newton was born in 1642 on a farm in England and studied at Cambridge. In 1687 he published a book in Latin, usually called the . In it he set out three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation. The same force that pulled an apple to the ground, he showed, held the moon in its orbit. Nature, it seemed, ran on laws that mathematics could describe. The astronomer Edmond Halley urged Newton to write the book and paid to print it. Newton later ran the Royal Mint, the government's coin-making office, and he hunted counterfeiters with great energy.

Newton built on earlier thinkers. Francis Bacon had urged scholars to learn by observation and experiment. René Descartes had urged them to doubt everything until it could be proven by reason. Newton was also a deeply religious man, who wrote more about the Bible than about physics. He thought his laws revealed the order that God had given to the world.

A NEW WAY OF LOOKING

Newton was one star among many. The Irish scientist Robert Boyle showed that the pressure of a gas rises as its volume shrinks. The Dutch cloth merchant Antonie van Leeuwenhoek ground tiny lenses and was the first to see bacteria, which he called little animals. The Swedish naturalist Carl Linnaeus gave every plant and animal a two-part Latin name that scientists still use. Later, in America, Benjamin Franklin flew a kite to study lightning.

Each of them worked by looking carefully, measuring, and testing. Soon others asked a bold question. If nature follows laws, might human society follow laws too? Could they be found by the same patient method?

He believed that God made the universe like a clockmaker but did not interfere in it. A few thinkers went further and denied God altogether. Many of them also admired the ancient writers Cicero, Tacitus, and Plutarch.

THE REPUBLIC OF LETTERS

Scholars across Europe wrote to each other in Latin and French, and they called their network the Republic of Letters. Learned societies helped. The Royal Society met in London from 1660, and the Academy of Sciences began in Paris in 1666. Cheaper printing made books, newspapers, and journals easier to buy. The Dutch Republic printed works that French censors had banned.

One of its writers was Pierre Bayle, a HuguenotHYOO-guh-notA French Protestant of the 1500s to 1700s. See the glossary box. who fled France and lived in Rotterdam. There he edited a journal and wrote a huge dictionary that questioned old beliefs. You can see how Louis XIV's revocation of the Edict of Nantes helped to spread the ideas he feared. In London, coffeehouses charged a penny at the door. For that price, anyone could read the newspapers and join the argument. People called them penny universities.

THE SALONS

In Paris the great meeting places were the , regular gatherings in the drawing rooms of wealthy women. Madame Geoffrin held dinners twice a week for writers and artists. Madame du Deffand and Julie de Lespinasse ran rival gatherings. The hostess chose the subjects, guided the conversation, helped struggling writers, and kept debates polite enough to continue. One remarkable woman of the era, Émilie du Châtelet, did more than host. She was a gifted mathematician who translated Newton's Principia into French and added her own explanations, so that readers who knew no Latin could study it.

It was in these rooms that the  tried out their ideas. The word means philosophers, and it was used for the French writers who led the Enlightenment. Among them were Voltaire and Denis Diderot. Diderot and Jean d'Alembert edited the Encyclopédie, a huge work in twenty-eight volumes published from 1751 to 1772. It explained science, crafts, and ideas to a general audience. Church and royal censors tried to ban it more than once. It was published anyway.

LIMITS AND DANGERS

The Enlightenment did not reach everyone. Most Europeans were peasants who could not read. Its audience was mostly the middle and upper classes of cities, and it needed rising literacy, cheap printing, good roads, and trade wealth to flourish. Without those things, the ideas could not have spread as quickly.

Writers who went too far could pay for it. Voltaire was locked in the Bastille and later driven into exile. Diderot spent months in prison. Books were burned, and authors often published without their names. The thinkers learned to hide a sharp point inside polite words. The movement also had blind spots. Its writers disagreed about slavery, for example. Some condemned it, some mocked it in clever satires, and a few defended it. Women were welcome in the salons, but they were shut out of universities and learned societies.

ENLIGHTENED RULERS

Some kings and queens liked the new ideas, at least when it helped them. Frederick the Great of Prussia corresponded with Voltaire and ended the use of torture in his courts. Catherine the Great of Russia read the Encyclopédie. Emperor Joseph II of Austria ended serfdom in parts of his lands. But these rulers did not give up their own power. They wanted to reform from above, not to share authority. The Enlightenment could serve absolute monarchs as easily as it could challenge them.

IDEAS WITH CONSEQUENCES

Be careful about the old story that reason simply marched forward and defeated ignorance. That is a Whig version of history. The Enlightenment was a human movement, with human motives. Its ideas could be used wisely or recklessly, and its heroes sometimes failed to live by their own principles.

Its habit of questioning inherited institutions would one day reach the throne of Louis XIV's great-great-grandson. Next lesson, you will meet five of the writers and ask what each of them thought holds a free society together.
