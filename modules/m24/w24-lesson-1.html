🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

In 1687 a quiet Englishman published a book that explained why planets move and apples fall. Today you will see how Newton's discoveries changed how educated people thought, and how books, coffeehouses, and salons carried new ideas across Europe.

THE MAN WHO WEIGHED THE HEAVENS

Isaac Newton was born in 1642 on a farm in England and studied at Cambridge. When plague closed the university in 1665 and 1666, he went home to the farm and worked out ideas that later grew into his laws. He told friends in later life that watching an apple fall helped set him thinking about gravity. He also used a prism to show that white light is a mixture of colors. In 1687 he published a book in Latin, usually called the Principia. In it he set out three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation. The same force that pulled an apple to the ground, he showed, held the moon in its orbit. Nature, it seemed, ran on laws that mathematics could describe. The astronomer Edmond Halley urged Newton to write the book and paid to print it. Halley had gone to Cambridge in 1684 to ask what path a planet would follow if the sun's pull weakened with distance. Newton answered at once that it would be an ellipse, and he said he had worked it out years before. He could not find his papers, so he did the work again. It took about eighteen months of almost constant labor, and the result was the Principia. Few readers could follow its mathematics, but a comet proved its point. Using Newton's laws, Halley decided that the comets seen in 1531, 1607, and 1682 were one object returning about every seventy-six years, and he predicted its return in 1758. He died in 1742. The comet appeared on Christmas night, 1758, and it carries his name.

Newton later ran the Royal Mint, the government's coin-making office, and he hunted counterfeiters with great energy. He sometimes went into taverns in disguise to gather evidence, and his work sent the counterfeiter William Chaloner to the gallows in 1699.

Newton built on earlier thinkers. Francis Bacon had urged scholars to learn by observation and experiment. René Descartes had urged them to doubt everything until it could be proven by reason. Descartes found one certainty that could not be doubted: if he was thinking, he must exist. Newton was also a deeply religious man, who wrote more about the Bible than about physics, and who also filled notebooks with alchemy. He thought his laws revealed the order that God had given to the world.

Newton also stood on the work of astronomers. Johannes Kepler had shown early in the 1600s that the planets move in ellipses rather than perfect circles. Galileo Galilei turned a telescope on the sky in 1609 and 1610 and saw mountains on the moon and four moons circling Jupiter, sights that did not fit the old picture of perfect, unchanging heavens. In 1633 the Inquisition in Rome made Galileo, by then an old man, declare that the Earth does not move, and he spent his last years under house arrest. Newton wrote in 1676 that if he had seen further than others, it was by standing on the shoulders of giants.

A NEW WAY OF LOOKING

Newton was one star among many. The Irish scientist Robert Boyle, using a pump he built to draw the air out of glass jars, showed that the pressure of a gas rises as its volume shrinks. The Dutch cloth merchant Antonie van Leeuwenhoek ground tiny lenses and was the first to see bacteria, which he called little animals. His lenses were beads of glass smaller than a pea, and he never told anyone how he made his best ones. The Swedish naturalist Carl Linnaeus gave every plant and animal a two-part Latin name that scientists still use. Before him, one plant might have several names, depending on who was talking. Later, in America, Benjamin Franklin flew a kite in a thunderstorm in 1752 to study lightning, and he showed that lightning is a form of electricity.

Each of them worked by looking carefully, measuring, and testing. Soon others asked a bold question. If nature follows laws, might human society follow laws too? Could they be found by the same patient method?

Newton's ideas had to win over doubters, and they did it by measurement. Newton's theory said that the Earth bulges at the equator and is flatter at the poles. Many French scientists, who had followed Descartes, expected the opposite. In the 1730s the Academy of Sciences sent expeditions to Peru and to Lapland, in the far north of Europe, to measure the length of a degree of the Earth's curve. The results favored Newton.

DARING TO KNOW

Newton's success gave educated people a new confidence, and that confidence became the Enlightenmenten-LYT-un-mentThe movement of the 1700s that held that human reason could be used to improve knowledge, government, and society. See the glossary box.. Its supporters believed that human reason could be used to improve knowledge, government, and society. The German thinker Immanuel Kant gave it a motto in 1784: "Dare to know." Have the courage to use your own understanding. Kant wrote that enlightenment was the way out of a kind of childhood that people bring on themselves, when laziness or fear lets others do their thinking for them.

Many Enlightenment thinkers favored an empiricalem-PEER-ih-kulBased on observation and experiment rather than on tradition or pure reasoning. See the glossary box. approach, one that begins with what can be observed and tested. But the movement was not simply reason against religion. Newton and John Locke were devout Christians. Voltaire was a deistDEE-iz-umThe belief that God made the universe and its laws but does not step in to change them. See the glossary box.. He believed that God made the universe like a clockmaker but did not interfere in it. A few thinkers went further and denied God altogether. Many of them also admired the ancient writers Cicero, Tacitus, and Plutarch.

Voltaire's path shows how the two ideas met. He was in London in 1727 for the funeral of Isaac Newton, and he wrote about it afterward: England was burying a mathematician in Westminster Abbey with the honors of a king. He came home with a book of letters about what he had seen in England, including its open argument and its religious toleration, and he praised Newton and Locke.

THE REPUBLIC OF LETTERS

Scholars across Europe wrote to each other in Latin and French, and they called their network the Republic of Letters. Learned societies helped. The Royal Society met in London from 1660, and the Academy of Sciences began in Paris in 1666. Cheaper printing made books, newspapers, and journals easier to buy. The Dutch Republic printed works that French censors had banned. Henry Oldenburg, the Royal Society's secretary, wrote to scholars across Europe and printed their reports in a journal, the Philosophical Transactions, beginning in 1665. The Society's own motto told its members to take nobody's word for it. A claim had to be shown by experiment.

One of its writers was Pierre Bayle, a HuguenotHYOO-guh-notA French Protestant of the 1500s to 1700s. See the glossary box. who fled France and lived in Rotterdam. There he edited a journal and wrote a huge dictionary that questioned old beliefs. His journal, begun in 1684, was called News from the Republic of Letters. His dictionary slipped many of its doubts into long footnotes. His brother, who had stayed behind in France, died in prison in 1685. You can see how Louis XIV's revocation of the Edict of Nantes helped to spread the ideas he feared. In London, coffeehouses charged a penny at the door. For that price, anyone could read the newspapers and join the argument. People called them penny universities. In 1711 the essayists Joseph Addison and Richard Steele began a daily paper, the Spectator, with the stated hope of bringing philosophy out of libraries and into clubs and coffeehouses. Ship owners and insurers gathered at one London coffeehouse to swap news, and the business that grew from their meetings is still called Lloyd's.

THE SALONS

In Paris the great meeting places were the salonssuh-LONA French word for a regular gathering in a private drawing room where writers, scientists, and nobles talked about ideas. See the glossary box., regular gatherings in the drawing rooms of wealthy women. Madame Geoffrin held dinners twice a week for writers and artists. She is said to have ended dangerous talk with the words, "That is quite enough." Madame du Deffand and Julie de Lespinasse ran rival gatherings. The hostess chose the subjects, guided the conversation, helped struggling writers, and kept debates polite enough to continue.

One remarkable woman of the era, Émilie du Châtelet, did more than host. She was a gifted mathematician who translated Newton's Principia into French and added her own explanations, so that readers who knew no Latin could study it. Voltaire wrote a popular guide to Newton's ideas with her help. In Italy, Laura Bassi became a professor at the University of Bologna and taught Newton's physics. For years she lived and studied with Voltaire at the country house of Cirey. Her notes argued, from experiments by other scientists, that the energy of a moving body depends on the square of its speed. She finished the translation in 1749, just before she died, soon after giving birth, and it appeared ten years later.

It was in these rooms that the philosophesfee-loh-ZOFFA French word meaning "philosophers." It was used for the writers and thinkers of the French Enlightenment. See the glossary box. tried out their ideas. The word means philosophers, and it was used for the French writers who led the Enlightenment. Among them were Voltaire and Denis Diderot. Diderot and Jean d'Alembert edited the Encyclopédie, a huge work in twenty-eight volumes published from 1751 to 1772. It explained science, crafts, and ideas to a general audience. Church and royal censors tried to ban it more than once. It was published anyway. It was sold by subscription, and several thousand people signed up, which shows how many readers there were. In 1759 the pope condemned it. Its plates showed step by step how pins were made and glass was blown. Salon hostesses such as Madame Geoffrin helped pay for it. The chief royal censor, Malesherbes, is said to have warned Diderot before a police search in 1759 and offered to hide his papers at his own house.

LIMITS AND DANGERS

The Enlightenment did not reach everyone. Most Europeans were peasants who could not read. In France around 1700, a bit more than a quarter of men could sign their names, and fewer women could. By the 1780s nearly half of men could. Its audience was mostly the middle and upper classes of cities, and it needed rising literacy, cheap printing, good roads, and trade wealth to flourish. Without those things, the ideas could not have spread as quickly.

The learned world was mostly closed to women as well. The Royal Society did not elect a woman as a member until 1945. A story told about Émilie du Châtelet says that a Paris café where scientists met turned her away at the door, and that she came back dressed in men's clothes and was let in. Women who studied nature in this age did it through salons, through family, and through the friendship of men who would teach them, and a woman needed a good deal of luck and money to do it at all.

Writers who went too far could pay for it. Voltaire was locked in the Bastille and later driven into exile. Diderot spent months in prison. Books were burned, and authors often published without their names. The Church kept its own list of forbidden books. Smugglers carried banned books across the border, and a book might claim to have been printed in some other city to throw the police off. Voltaire's own letters on England were burned in 1734 by order of the high court of Paris. The thinkers learned to hide a sharp point inside polite words. Even so, ideas moved quickly. A pamphlet printed in London could be read in Vienna within weeks, and a single copy of a banned book might be passed from hand to hand until it fell apart.

ENLIGHTENED RULERS

Some kings and queens liked the new ideas, at least when it helped them. Frederick the Great of Prussia corresponded with Voltaire and ended the use of torture in his courts. He called himself the first servant of the state, and he invited Voltaire to live at his palace, though the friendship ended in quarrels. Catherine the Great of Russia read the Encyclopédie, and she bought Diderot's library to help him with money. Emperor Joseph II of Austria ended serfdom in parts of his lands. But these rulers did not give up their own power. They wanted to reform from above, not to share authority. The Enlightenment could serve absolute monarchs as easily as it could challenge them.

Reform from above met resistance from below. Catherine called a commission in 1767 to rewrite Russia's laws, using ideas she had borrowed from Montesquieu, and then sent it home. She never freed Russia's serfs, and serfdom grew heavier during her reign. Joseph's reforms angered nobles and clergy, and after his death in 1790 much of his work was undone.

IDEAS WITH CONSEQUENCES

Be careful about the old story that reason simply marched forward and defeated ignorance. That is a Whig version of history. The Enlightenment was a human movement, with human motives. Its ideas could be used wisely or recklessly, and its heroes sometimes failed to live by their own principles.

Its habit of questioning inherited institutions would one day reach the throne of Louis XIV's great-great-grandson. Next lesson, you will meet five of the writers and ask what each of them thought holds a free society together.
