🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

In 1960 many observers believed that communism might win the contest. By 1991 the Soviet Union no longer existed. Today you will see how communist states ruled, why they struggled, and how the system fell almost without a shot in Europe. Keep this week's discussion question in mind: why did communism collapse?

LIFE UNDER COMMUNIST RULE

Communist states shared a pattern. A single party ruled and no rival was allowed. The government owned the factories and farms and ran a command economykuh-MAND ih-KON-uh-meeAn economy in which the government, not buyers and sellers, decides what is produced, how much of it is made, and what it costs. See the glossary box.: planners in the capital set targets for steel, shoes, and wheat. A secret police, such as the Soviet KGB or the East German Stasi, watched the people. In East Germany, one in every few dozen adults was said to work as an informer. Newspapers, schools, and churches were controlled, and open criticism could mean prison.

The system brought some real gains. Illiteracy fell, and the Soviet Union produced rockets, scientists, and in 1957 the first artificial satellite, Sputnik. But planners could not know what millions of people wanted. Factories met their quotas by making goods nobody needed, and shops had long lines and empty shelves. Without free prices, there was no signal to say what was scarce. Without the chance to profit, there was little reward for good work or new ideas. Sources of real information were closed off, so leaders were often told what they wished to hear.

CRACKS IN THE BLOC

Stalin died in March 1953. In 1956 his successor, Nikita Khrushchev, shocked a party congress with a secret speech describing Stalin's crimes. The hope that this brought for change reached the satellite statesSAT-uh-lyt STAYTA country that is officially independent but is actually controlled by a more powerful nation. See the glossary box.. In October 1956 Hungarians rose up, demanding free elections and an end to Soviet control. Soviet tanks entered Budapest, and thousands died. In 1968 the Czechoslovak leader Alexander Dubcek tried to build "socialism with a human face" in the Prague Spring, with more freedom of speech. Soviet leader Leonid Brezhnev sent in troops from the Warsaw Pact. The Brezhnev Doctrine declared that the Soviets would act whenever socialism was threatened in a member state.

MAO'S CHINA

In China, the communists under Mao Zedong won a long civil war against the Nationalists in 1949. The defeated Nationalists fled to the island of Taiwan. Mao's government seized land and redistributed it, and then, in the Great Leap Forward of 1958, forced peasants into huge communes and ordered them to make steel in backyard furnaces. The harvest was neglected and officials reported false numbers to please their superiors. The result was the worst famine in history. Scholars estimate that between thirty and forty-five million people died by 1962.

In 1966 Mao, fearing that he was losing control of the party, launched the Cultural Revolution. He urged young Red Guards to attack teachers, officials, and anyone linked to the old culture. Schools closed, temples were wrecked, and many were beaten, jailed, or killed. China split from the Soviet Union in the early 1960s, and its quarrel with Moscow weakened the unity of world communism. After Mao died in 1976, Deng Xiaoping allowed farmers and entrepreneurs to work for profit, and China's economy began to grow quickly. The party held on to political power, as it showed in June 1989 when it crushed protests in Tiananmen Square.

POPE, UNION, AND PRESIDENT

By the late 1970s the Soviet economy was stagnating. In 1979 the Soviets invaded Afghanistan, expecting a short campaign, and became trapped in a costly war against local fighters supplied by the West and others. In the same year Karol Wojtyla, a Pole, became Pope John Paul II. When he visited Poland, huge crowds gathered, and the Poles found they were not alone. In August 1980 shipyard workers in Gdansk, led by an electrician, Lech Walesa, formed an independent trade union called Solidarity, which soon had about ten million members. Poland's government declared martial law in 1981 and jailed its leaders, but could not kill the movement.

In 1981 Ronald Reagan became president of the United States. He called the Soviet Union an "evil empire," built up American arms, backed anti-communist movements, and spoke of freedom's power. Historians still argue about how much this pressure mattered compared with the Soviet system's own failures. The Soviet economy could not match American spending on arms without sacrificing the needs of its people.

In 1986 an explosion at the Chernobyl nuclear plant was hidden at first, and the lie damaged trust in the government. Gorbachev withdrew from Afghanistan and made arms agreements with Reagan. He also told the satellite states' leaders that the Soviet Union would no longer send in tanks to keep them in power.

This was the decisive choice. Gorbachev expected reformed communism to survive. Instead, once people were free to speak and the fear of Soviet tanks was gone, the whole system began to unravel.

1989 AND AFTER

In 1989 Poland held partly free elections and Solidarity won nearly every seat it was allowed to contest. In May, Hungary began opening its border to Austria, and thousands of East Germans poured through. Demonstrations grew in Leipzig. On November 9 an East German official, confused about new travel rules, announced at a press conference that they took effect "immediately." Crowds rushed to the Berlin Wall. The guards, without orders, gave way, and Germans began tearing the wall down. A government that would have shot people in 1961 had no will to do so. In weeks the communist governments of Czechoslovakia, Bulgaria, and Romania fell. Romania's dictator, Nicolae Ceausescu, was shot after a hasty trial on Christmas Day. In October 1990 Germany was reunited.

The Soviet Union itself lasted only two more years. Its republics demanded independence, and in August 1991 hard-line officials tried to seize power from Gorbachev. The coup failed within three days, after Boris Yeltsin, president of Russia, stood on a tank in Moscow and called on citizens to resist. On December 25, 1991, Gorbachev resigned and the Soviet flag came down over the Kremlin. The Soviet Union broke into fifteen countries.

Why did it fall? The system's weak economy, its loss of belief, and the pressure of the West all counted. But no one in 1985 predicted collapse, and different choices by Gorbachev, by the guards at the Wall, or by the plotters might have given a different result. As you prepare for the discussion, weigh how much was built into the system and how much rested on people.
