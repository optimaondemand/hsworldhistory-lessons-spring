🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

Louis XIV ruled France for seventy-two years and built a palace that was also a political machine. Today you will see how he turned proud, rebellious nobles into courtiers, and what that cost.

A BOY WHO WATCHED PARIS RISE

Louis XIV was born in 1638 and became king at the age of four. His mother, Queen Anne, ruled for him, and the man who did the daily work of government was a churchman, Cardinal Mazarin. Mazarin was clever and unpopular. He had been born in Italy, so he was a foreigner to the French lords who had to take his orders. France was also at war with Spain, and the war grew more expensive every year. Mazarin's answer was higher taxes.

In 1648 judges, nobles, and city mobs rose against his taxes in a series of revolts called the FrondeFROHNDA French word for a child's slingshot. It names the revolts against the crown from 1648 to 1653. See the glossary box.. A fronde was a child's slingshot, and the name mocked the rebels. The trouble began in a law court. The high court of Paris, a body of judges and not a lawmaking assembly like Congress, refused to approve some of the new taxes. When Mazarin had a popular judge arrested that August, the city answered by throwing barricades across its streets.

Then came a night in January 1649. Louis was ten. His mother woke him in the dark, and the royal family slipped away from Paris, the boy king shivering in the cold. They fled to a palace at Saint-Germain, west of the city. It was so poorly furnished that, by some accounts, the king slept on straw.

The revolts dragged on until 1653. Early in 1651, with Paris restless again, a rumor spread that the queen meant to carry the king off once more. A crowd came to the palace and demanded to see the boy with their own eyes. The guards let them in. A lady of the queen's household wrote later that Louis lay in bed pretending to sleep while the people filed silently past. He was twelve.

Louis never forgot it. He learned that rebellious nobles and an angry capital could be deadly. Mazarin died in March 1661, and every ambitious man at court began doing sums, because someone would surely take the cardinal's place. The king was only twenty-two. Most people thought of him as a young man who liked dancing and hunting. Within a day he called his ministers together and shocked them. He announced that he would rule himself and name no chief minister. He kept that promise for fifty-four years.

He filled his council with men from lawyer and merchant families, who owed everything to him. The great princes he kept out of the small inner council where the real decisions were made.

THE SUPERINTENDENT'S PARTY

That same summer, the king's finance chief, Nicolas Fouquet, invited him to dinner. Fouquet had grown rich managing money under Mazarin, and he had spent a good deal of it on a country house, Vaux-le-Vicomte, with gardens that were the talk of France. On the evening of August 17 he fed the king and the court on dishes of gold, or so the story has been told ever since. He staged a new play by Molière, and he ended the night with fireworks.

Louis watched all of it. Less than three weeks later the captain of his musketeers, d'Artagnan, arrested Fouquet. Behind the arrest stood Jean-Baptiste Colbert, an official who had served Mazarin and had been quietly gathering evidence of Fouquet's money dealings. A court tried Fouquet for years. When it sentenced him to exile, the king made the punishment harsher: life in prison. Fouquet died in a fortress in 1680.

Louis then took the three artists who had built Fouquet's glory, the architect Le Vau, the painter Le Brun, and the gardener Le Nôtre, and set them to work on his own father's hunting lodge.

THE SUN KING

Louis chose the sun as his emblem, since everything in the kingdom revolved around him. He believed in absolute monarchyAB-suh-loot MON-ar-keeA government in which a king holds final authority and no council, court, or parliament can override him. See the glossary box., in which the king held the final word and answered to God alone. Louis worked for hours each day, reading reports and signing orders.

His finance minister, Colbert, put mercantilismMER-kun-til-iz-umThe belief that a nation grows rich by selling more to other countries than it buys, and that the government should guide trade to achieve this. See the glossary box. into practice. The idea was simple. A country had only so much gold and silver. Each time French buyers bought Dutch cloth or Venetian glass, coins flowed out of France. Colbert wanted France to make such goods itself and to sell abroad more than it bought. So he taxed foreign goods heavily, built state workshops for tapestries and glass, expanded the navy, and dug the great Canal du Midi. The canal, designed by the engineer Pierre-Paul Riquet, ran about 150 miles across southern France and joined the Atlantic side of the country to the Mediterranean, so goods could cross France without a ship sailing around Spain.

Louis also spent lavishly on the arts, and the playwrights Molière and Racine and the composer Lully made his court the envy of Europe. Colbert helped draw up lists of writers and scholars who deserved royal pensions. A writer's income could depend on the king's goodwill.

THE PALACE

Louis could have built his palace in Paris. The Louvre, the old royal residence by the river, was waiting to be rebuilt, and in 1665 he invited the famous Italian sculptor Bernini to design it. The plans came to nothing. Louis had good reason to distrust the city that had frightened him as a boy, and his attention drifted to a quiet spot in the countryside.

Louis's greatest project was the palace of Versailles, twelve miles from Paris. It began as his father's hunting lodge. Over forty years, architects, painters, and gardeners turned it into a palace the size of a town. The Hall of Mirrors was nearly 240 feet long and lined with hundreds of mirrors, 357 in all, which were a marvelous luxury at the time. Venice had long held the secret of fine mirror glass, and Colbert had lured Venetian glassmakers to France to teach the craft.

The site had no river of its own, so the fountains needed machines. Fountains played in the gardens, with water pumped up by machines from the Seine. In 1682 the whole court moved in. Thousands of nobles and servants lived there. The king's own officers decided who got which room, so even a bed was a favor.

THE ART OF POWER

Louis understood that a king must look like a king. In 1653, as a fourteen-year-old, he danced in a court ballet that ran through the night. As dawn came he appeared on stage dressed as Apollo, the sun god. Painters, sculptors, and medal makers filled France with his image. Statues of him went up in the squares of Paris, the city that had once driven him out.

Royal academies set rules for painting, music, and writing, and the best artists worked for the court. Printed histories told the story of his victories. In an age without television or newspapers, Louis made himself the most recognizable man in Europe.

This was image-making, and it was also a tool of government. A king who seems larger than life makes obedience feel natural.

THE DAILY SHOW

Life at Versailles ran on ceremony. The king's day was a kind of play, with the same scenes in the same order, and the nobles were both its cast and its audience. Every morning a few nobles earned the honor of watching the king get out of bed. The highest-ranking man present had the right to hand him his shirt. After the morning rising came Mass in the chapel and meetings with his ministers, then a hunt or a walk in the gardens. In the evening there were cards, billiards, and music in the state rooms. Supper was served in public: Louis ate at a table with his family while courtiers without the right to sit stood and watched. At night, a few nobles held his candle as he went to bed.

Rank decided who sat on a chair and who on a stool. A few great ladies had the right to a stool in the queen's presence, and quarrels over such rights could last for years. A duke who was missing from court would hear the king say, "I do not know him." The memoirist Saint-Simon, whom you will read this week, described how closely Louis noticed who was absent. Saint-Simon wrote his memoirs decades later, and he held a grudge against a king who gave high posts to men of lower birth. He is a vivid witness, though not a neutral one.

The ceremony had a purpose. Only the king could grant pensions, offices, and army commands, so nobles had to compete for his favor. Picture a count whose family has held a castle in the countryside for centuries. If he stays home, he is lord of a district, but he has no pension, no post for his sons, and no chance of an army command. If he goes to Versailles, he gets a tiny room, a pile of bills for clothes and cards, and a chance.

A noble who spent his days at Versailles was spending his money on clothes and parties, not on a private army. The proud lords of the Fronde had become courtiersKOR-tee-erA person who attends a king's court, often seeking favors or position. See the glossary box.. The Prince of Condé, the great general of the Fronde, had once led a rebel army and then fought for Spain against France. The king pardoned him in 1659, and Condé later commanded French armies for Louis.

Louis's private life was less orderly than his court. He had several mistresses in his younger years. After his queen died in 1683, he secretly married a devout widow, Madame de Maintenon. She had first come to the palace as governess to the king's children. She encouraged his piety, and her influence grew as he aged. The palace itself was crowded and drafty, and its nobles often lived in tiny rooms. Yet they did not complain, since a room at Versailles was a sign of favor.

THE COST OF VERSAILLES

All this glory was expensive. Building Versailles took tens of thousands of workers over decades, and at times soldiers were put to digging and hauling as well. Its upkeep cost a large part of the king's income. Meanwhile most French people were poor farmers. They paid a land tax called the taille and a heavy tax on salt, and they were often forced to work on the roads. The taille was collected parish by parish, and the neighbors chosen to collect it answered for any shortfall.

Nobles and clergy were largely exempt. The nobles held that they paid in blood, since they served as officers and fought the king's wars. The clergy sent the crown a voluntary gift every few years instead of taxes. Most of the burden fell on the farming population, and bad harvests brought real hardship.

THE KING'S MEN

Louis did not rule only through his court. In the provinces he relied on intendantsan-tahn-DAHNA French word for a royal official sent to govern a region in the king's name. See the glossary box., royal officials chosen from lawyers and townsmen rather than great families. They had no local power of their own, so they depended on the king. They collected taxes, kept order, and recruited soldiers. The old rulers of the provinces, the governors, were great nobles. Louis let them keep their titles and their parades. Within a province a governor outranked an intendant, but the intendant held the pen.

Louis also built a standing armySTAN-ding AR-meeA permanent professional army that is kept in being during peacetime as well as war. See the glossary box. that grew to several hundred thousand men, the largest in Europe. Armies had once been raised for a war and sent home when it ended. Louis kept his soldiers in service all year, paid by the crown. It had uniforms, regular pay, and supply depots stocked in advance, which let his armies take the field in early spring instead of waiting for grass and grain to grow. Many officers were nobles. For men who might once have joined a rebellion, the army offered honor and wages from the king. A king with such a force could defend his borders and also threaten his neighbors.

HOW ABSOLUTE WAS IT?

Louis's rule was less absolute than it looked. Many government jobs were bought and could be inherited, so the king could not easily remove the people who held them. Provinces, towns, courts, and the Church all had old privileges that he had to respect. Nobles and clergy paid few taxes, and the burden fell on peasants. A famous line says Louis declared, "I am the state." No reliable record shows that he said it, and the real king worked inside limits that the line leaves out.

The crown was often short of cash, so it sold government offices to raise money. A man who bought a judge's post could pass it on to his son. Here is how it worked. A buyer paid a large sum for a post as judge or tax official. The post gave him a salary and respect, and sometimes noble rank. Since 1604 the crown had let officeholders pay a small yearly fee so that their sons could inherit the post. The king got steady money, but the officeholder now owned his job like a farm. To remove him, the king had to pay back the price, and a king who sold offices to pay his bills could rarely afford to. This brought in quick money, but it created a class of office-holders who defended their own interests and resisted reform. The king could only do what these groups would let him do. Even so, France had internal peace for the rest of his reign, and that was a real achievement after the Fronde.

BRILLIANT AND BRITTLE

A Classical Republican would ask what this system did to the character of the nobility. The old nobles had won honor in war and by giving independent advice. At Versailles honor came increasingly from royal favor and display. A man whose income came from the king's pension thought twice before giving the king advice he would not like. Later the French thinker Montesquieu would argue that honor was the spirit of monarchies, but he also saw how easily it could turn into display.
