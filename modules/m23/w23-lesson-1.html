🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

Louis XIV ruled France for seventy-two years and built a palace that was also a political machine. Today you will see how he turned proud, rebellious nobles into courtiers, and what that cost.

A BOY WHO WATCHED PARIS RISE

Louis XIV was born in 1638 and became king at the age of four. A churchman, Cardinal Mazarin, ruled France for him. Mazarin was clever and unpopular. In 1648 judges, nobles, and city mobs rose against his taxes in a series of revolts called the . A fronde was a child's slingshot, and the name mocked the rebels. The royal family had to slip away from Paris at night, with the boy king shivering in the cold.

Louis never forgot it. He learned that rebellious nobles and an angry capital could be deadly. When Mazarin died in 1661, the twenty-two-year-old king shocked his court. He announced that he would rule himself and name no chief minister. He kept that promise for fifty-four years.

THE SUN KING

Louis chose the sun as his emblem, since everything in the kingdom revolved around him. He believed in absolute monarchyAB-suh-loot MON-ar-keeA government in which a king holds final authority and no council, court, or parliament can override him. See the glossary box., in which the king held the final word and answered to God alone. He worked for hours each day, reading reports and signing orders.

His finance minister, Jean-Baptiste Colbert, put mercantilismMER-kun-til-iz-umThe belief that a nation grows rich by selling more to other countries than it buys, and that the government should guide trade to achieve this. See the glossary box. into practice. Colbert taxed foreign goods heavily, built state workshops for tapestries and glass, expanded the navy, and dug the great Canal du Midi. Louis also spent lavishly on the arts, and the playwrights Molière and Racine and the composer Lully made his court the envy of Europe.

THE PALACE

Louis's greatest project was the palace of Versailles, twelve miles from Paris. It began as his father's hunting lodge. Over forty years, architects, painters, and gardeners turned it into a palace the size of a town. The Hall of Mirrors was nearly 240 feet long and lined with hundreds of mirrors, which were a marvelous luxury at the time. Fountains played in the gardens, with water pumped up by machines from the Seine. In 1682 the whole court moved in. Thousands of nobles and servants lived there.

THE ART OF POWER

Louis understood that a king must look like a king. As a teenager he danced in court ballets dressed as Apollo, the sun god. Painters, sculptors, and medal makers filled France with his image. Royal academies set rules for painting, music, and writing, and the best artists worked for the court. Printed histories told the story of his victories. In an age without television or newspapers, Louis made himself the most recognizable man in Europe.

This was propaganda, but it also worked as policy. A king who seems larger than life makes obedience feel natural.

THE DAILY SHOW

Life at Versailles ran on ceremony. Every morning a few nobles earned the honor of watching the king get out of bed, handing him his shirt, or holding his candle at night. Rank decided who sat on a chair and who on a stool. A duke who was missing from court would hear the king say, "I do not know him." The memoirist Saint-Simon, whom you will read this week, described how closely Louis noticed who was absent.

The ceremony had a purpose. Only the king could grant pensions, offices, and army commands, so nobles had to compete for his favor. A noble who spent his days at Versailles was spending his money on clothes and parties, not on a private army. The proud lords of the Fronde had become courtiersKOR-tee-erA person who attends a king's court, often seeking favors or position. See the glossary box..

Louis's private life was less orderly than his court. He had several mistresses in his younger years. After his queen died in 1683, he secretly married a devout widow, Madame de Maintenon. She encouraged his piety, and her influence grew as he aged. The palace itself was crowded and drafty, and its nobles often lived in tiny rooms. Yet they did not complain, since a room at Versailles was a sign of favor.

THE PRICE OF VERSAILLES

All this glory was expensive. Building Versailles took tens of thousands of workers over decades, and its upkeep cost a large part of the king's income. Meanwhile most French people were poor farmers. They paid a land tax called the taille and a heavy tax on salt, and they were often forced to work on the roads. Nobles and clergy were largely exempt. The French countryside paid for the brilliance of the court, and many peasants went hungry in bad harvest years.

THE KING'S MEN

Louis did not rule only through his court. In the provinces he relied on intendantsan-tahn-DAHNA French word for a royal official sent to govern a region in the king's name. See the glossary box., royal officials chosen from lawyers and townsmen rather than great families. They had no local power of their own, so they depended on the king. They collected taxes, kept order, and recruited soldiers.

Louis also built a standing armySTAN-ding AR-meeA permanent professional army that is kept in being during peacetime as well as war. See the glossary box. that grew to several hundred thousand men, the largest in Europe. It had uniforms, regular pay, and supply depots. A king with such a force could defend his borders and also threaten his neighbors.

HOW ABSOLUTE WAS IT?

Louis's rule was less absolute than it looked. Many government jobs were bought and could be inherited, so the king could not easily remove the people who held them. Provinces, towns, courts, and the Church all had old privileges that he had to respect. Nobles and clergy paid few taxes, and the burden fell on peasants.

The crown was often short of cash, so it sold government offices to raise money. A man who bought a judge's post could pass it on to his son. This brought in quick money, but it created a class of office-holders who defended their own interests and resisted reform. The king could only do what these groups would let him do. Even so, France had internal peace for the rest of his reign, and that was a real achievement after the Fronde.

BRILLIANT AND BRITTLE

A Classical Republican would ask what this system did to the character of the nobility. The old nobles had won honor in war and by giving independent advice. At Versailles honor came from flattery and show. Later the French thinker Montesquieu would argue that honor was the spirit of monarchies, but he also saw how empty it could become.

The system also depended on one man. It worked while Louis had talent and energy, and it strained when he grew old. Whether he would use his army to make France strong or to bleed her is the story of the next lesson.
