🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

Louis XIV wanted glory abroad and one faith at home. Today you will see how his wars frightened Europe into joining against him, how he drove out a talented minority, and what he left behind.

THE FIRST WARS

Louis wanted glory, and in his day glory meant conquest. Absolute monarchy gave him the power to choose war without asking anyone's leave. His first war, in 1667, was over lands in the Spanish Netherlands. In 1672 he attacked the Dutch Republic with an enormous army. The Dutch called it the Disaster Year. Their government opened the dikes and flooded the land to stop the French. A mob murdered the leaders of the merchant party, and the young William of Orange came to power as stadtholder. He held on, and in 1678 the war ended with Louis taking new territory.

Louis was not satisfied. Even in times of peace, he seized towns on his borders and claimed they were rightfully his. In 1681 he took the city of Strasbourg. His neighbors began to wonder where he would stop.

Louis also looked beyond Europe. Colbert supported colonies in Canada and the Caribbean. In 1682 the explorer La Salle paddled down the Mississippi River and claimed its whole basin for France. He named the region Louisiana in honor of the king.

ONE KING, ONE LAW, ONE FAITH

A French saying summed up Louis's goal: one king, one law, one faith. The HuguenotsHYOO-guh-notA French Protestant of the 1500s to 1700s. See the glossary box. were French Protestants, and there were perhaps a million of them. In 1598 King Henri IV had issued the Edict of Nantes to end the wars of religion. Louis believed that a united kingdom needed a single faith, and his bishops agreed. From the 1660s he cut away Huguenot rights. He closed their schools and shut them out of many professions. From 1681 his agents used the dragonnadedrag-uh-NAYDA French word for the practice of quartering soldiers in Protestant homes to force the family to convert. See the glossary box.. They quartered soldiers in Huguenot homes and let them behave as roughly as they liked until the family gave in. Thousands converted to make it stop. In October 1685, the Edict of Fontainebleau revoked the Edict of Nantes completely. Protestant churches were to be torn down, pastors were ordered out, and worship was forbidden. Louis was told that Protestantism in France was finished.

⚡ DECIDE FIRST
In 1685 Louis was told that Protestantism in France was finished. He had cut Protestant rights, closed their schools, and sent soldiers into their homes to force conversions.
Decide first, and jot your answer in your notebook: what could go wrong with a king's belief that one order can end a faith? Then open to compare.
A decree can close churches and forbid worship, but it cannot make every person stay and obey. People with skills, savings, and strong beliefs can choose to leave, and they take their skills with them. Rival rulers can decide to welcome them. Louis was sure that force could end a faith, but that was a belief about people, not a fact, and the next part of the lesson shows what it cost.

A GIFT TO HIS RIVALS

It was not finished. Though the borders were closed to Protestants, perhaps two hundred thousand escaped. Many were skilled. They included silk weavers, bankers, watchmakers, and army officers. They settled in England, the Dutch Republic, Switzerland, and the German state of Brandenburg-Prussia, whose ruler invited them. Some sailed to the Dutch colony at the Cape of Good Hope and to North America. Many Huguenot officers went to serve William of Orange. Their descendants include famous Americans. Paul Revere's father, for example, was a Huguenot boy who came to Boston from France and changed his name.

Louis had meant to strengthen France, but he had armed and enriched his enemies. Protestant Europe was shocked, and it helped to turn opinion against France in the very years William was preparing his invasion of England.

THE KING WHO BACKED THE LOSER

When William took the English crown in 1689, Louis took the side of the exiled James II. He gave James ships, money, and soldiers to retake Ireland. William defeated them at the Boyne in 1690. Louis had now made himself the enemy of the new English government, and England would be at war with France for most of the next twenty-five years.

    THE GRAND ALLIANCE

    In 1688 Louis's attack on the German lands of the Rhine began the Nine Years' War. This time England, the Dutch Republic, Austria, and others joined together to stop him. The war ended in 1697 with Louis giving up many of his gains. This was an early example of the principle of the balance of powerBAL-unss uv POW-erThe idea that no single state should be allowed to grow so strong that it can dominate its neighbors, so that other states join together to stop it. See the glossary box.. When one state grows strong enough to dominate its neighbors, the others unite against it.

    THE SPANISH SUCCESSION

    In 1700 the childless king of Spain died. His will left the entire Spanish empire to Louis's grandson, Philip. If the French accepted, France and Spain might one day be ruled by one family. Louis accepted anyway, and Europe went to war again.

    In the War of the Spanish Succession, England, the Dutch, and Austria fought France and Spain. The English general Marlborough and the Austrian prince Eugene of Savoy won a famous victory at Blenheim in 1704. French armies had seemed unbeatable, and now they were not. Taxes soared, and in the terrible winter of 1709 hunger killed hundreds of thousands. In the Treaties of Utrecht, in 1713 and 1714, Philip kept Spain but his family could never also inherit France. Britain won Gibraltar, Minorca, and Newfoundland, along with the right to sell enslaved Africans in Spain's colonies. Philip stayed on the Spanish throne until 1746. The family he founded, the Spanish Bourbons, still reigns in Spain today.

    THE COST TO ORDINARY PEOPLE

    The burden of war fell heavily on ordinary people. Soldiers had to be fed and clothed, and taxes grew heavier. The cold winters of 1693 and 1709 ruined harvests, and many starved. The military engineer Marshal Vauban, who built the great fortresses that guarded France, saw the misery. He proposed a fairer tax that nobles and clergy would also pay. Louis rejected the idea and banned the book. Vauban died soon after. His plan went unused, and the problem he named stayed with France for the rest of the century.

    ⚡ HOW MUCH WAS HIS DOING?
    The winters of 1693 and 1709 ruined harvests, and the wars made taxes heavier. How much of the suffering of ordinary French people came from the king's own choices?
    STRONG OR HOLLOW?

    Louis died on September 1, 1715, after seventy-two years on the throne. He left a splendid culture and the largest population in Europe. He also left enormous debts. To his five-year-old great-grandson and heir he is reported to have said, "Do not imitate me in my taste for building, nor in my love of war."

    So was he strong or hollow? His rule solved the first-order problems of order and defense for many years. But his tax system favored the privileged. His wars drained the treasury, and his religious policy drove skilled people into the arms of his rivals. His successors inherited a government that was proud, rich in show, and short of money. France remained a great power, but the balance had shifted. Britain, with its Parliament, its bank, and its navy, was becoming the leading sea power and the best borrower in Europe. The struggle between the two would shape the next century, and you will see it in next week's lessons.
