🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

The richest corner of Europe rebelled against the most powerful king in Europe, and won. Today you will meet the king, the rebel leader, and the strange weapon that saved a town: the sea itself.

THE SEVENTEEN PROVINCES

The Low Countries lay along the North Sea, in what is now the Netherlands, Belgium, and Luxembourg. In the 1500s they were a patchwork of seventeen provinces. Their cities were crowded and wealthy. Antwerp was one of the busiest ports on earth. Each town held old charters that protected its rights, and its citizens guarded those rights jealously.

In 1555 and 1556, the emperor Charles V gave these lands, along with Spain and its silver empire, to his son Philip II. Philip was Spanish, devout, and hardworking. He sat at a desk in Madrid reading reports, and he never learned to speak Dutch. He was determined to keep his lands Catholic and to rule them from the center. He sent Spanish officials, planned new bishops, and enforced harsh laws against heresyHAIR-uh-seeA religious belief that a church officially rejects as false. See the glossary box.. At the time that meant Calvinism, a Protestant faith that was spreading quickly through the northern towns. He also raised taxes to pay for his wars. Each of these moves angered a people who believed they had rights.

Charles V had been born in the Low Countries, in the city of Ghent, and he spoke the language of his subjects. Philip left in 1559 and never came back. He left his half-sister Margaret of Parma to govern in his name, but the real decisions were made in a small private committee led by a churchman loyal to the king, Cardinal Granvelle. The great local nobles, men such as William of Orange and the Count of Egmont, had always expected to help rule the provinces. They found that they sat in a council that no longer decided anything. The new bishoprics, fourteen of them, were a second grievance, because they put the church under tighter royal control. A noble who felt shut out of power and a merchant who felt his town's charter was under attack had different complaints, but they began to speak to each other.

BEGGARS AND BROKEN STATUES

In 1566 a group of lesser noblemen, most of them Catholic, brought Philip's governor a petition asking him to soften the heresyHAIR-uh-seeA religious belief that a church officially rejects as false. See the glossary box. laws. One of the governor's advisers sneered that they were nothing but beggars. The nobles seized on the insult. They wore it proudly as a badge and called themselves the GeuzenKHUH-zuhnA Dutch word meaning "Beggars." The rebels adopted it as a proud name. See the glossary box., the Beggars.

A few days later the petitioners held a banquet in Brussels. Their host filled a wooden bowl, the kind a beggar carried, and drank to the health of the Beggars. The guests took up the toast and the cry. They hung small beggars' bowls and bags from their clothes, and they wore medals that carried the king's likeness and the words, in French, loyal to the king, even to the beggar's bag. Note what they claimed. They did not call themselves rebels. They said they were the king's most faithful servants, asking him to hear his own subjects.

That summer, angry Calvinist crowds broke into churches across the provinces and smashed statues and stained glass, which they thought were idols. The wave began in a small town in August, reached the great cathedral of Antwerp within ten days, and in about two weeks hundreds of churches had been stripped. The destruction horrified many Catholics, including many who had sympathized with the petition. Several great nobles, William of Orange among them, now helped the governor restore order.

Philip now faced a choice. He could calm the provinces with concessions, or he could crush them. He chose force. It was a decision, not a fate. Had he chosen otherwise, the Low Countries might have stayed in his empire for generations.

A KING FAR AWAY

Philip II ruled from Madrid, more than a thousand miles from Brussels. He was known as the paper king, because he tried to manage his enormous realm by reading and answering reports himself. Every important question traveled to him by courier, and the answer took weeks to come back. Meanwhile events in the Low Countries moved quickly. A governor in Brussels could watch a crisis grow, but he could not settle it without waiting for the king's word. By the time orders arrived, the situation had often changed.

This is a useful lesson about power. A king can have the largest army and the most silver in the world and still govern badly if he is too far from the problem. Philip's delays made his officials look weak, and when he finally acted, he acted harshly.

Even the army had to travel the long way. Philip first said that he would come north himself with troops, and he never did. Instead he chose a veteran commander and sent him by land, because the sea route past France and England was dangerous. The soldiers gathered in Italy and marched through the Alps and eastern France to Brussels, which took weeks. Spain kept its hold on the provinces by keeping that road open. A force that has to walk so far cannot easily be sent a second time.

THE DUKE OF ALBA

In 1567 Philip sent the Duke of Alba north with about ten thousand soldiers. Alba set up a court that the people soon called the Council of Blood. It tried thousands of people and put more than a thousand to death. In 1568 Alba beheaded the Counts of Egmont and Hoorn in Brussels. Both were Catholic noblemen who had remained loyal to the king. Alba also imposed a heavy new sales tax. In a few years he had managed to unite Catholics and Protestants, nobles and merchants, against the same ruler.

Egmont's case shows how much Alba misjudged the country. He had won famous battles for Philip and for Philip's father, and he was beloved in the provinces. Alba arrested him and Hoorn in 1567 and had them beheaded in the main square of Brussels the next year. The tax that followed was just as damaging. It took a tenth of the price of every sale of goods. Because a product is sold many times on its way from the producer to the customer, the tax fell again at each step, and a merchant in a trading country could be ruined by it. The Beggars had once asked for mercy. Alba taught the provinces to ask for something else.

WILLIAM THE SILENT

One of the great nobles, William of Orange, escaped to Germany before Alba could arrest him. Born in 1533 into a German Lutheran family, William had inherited the small principality of Orange as a boy. Charles V then raised him at the imperial court as a Catholic. He grew up rich, charming, and trusted. Tradition says he earned his nickname "the Silent" after he once heard Philip's secret plan to wipe out Protestants and kept quiet about it. In truth he was a talker and a persuader, and the name really means discreet.

Escaping cost him dearly. Alba seized William's property, and his eldest son, a student at the university of Leuven, was taken to Spain. The boy was kept in Spain for nearly thirty years. William's brother Louis won an early battle in 1568 and then lost a larger one, and William's own invasion that autumn went nowhere. Alba would not fight him in the open. He simply waited, and William's soldiers, who were mercenaries and had not been paid, drifted away.

William led armies against Alba, and at first they failed. He lost battle after battle, ran out of money, and pawned his own jewels. What kept him going was perseverance, one of the virtues Plutarch would have admired. He was also unusually willing to work with people of different faiths. He joined the Calvinists later, but he never stopped urging Catholics and Protestants to live under one government. The revolt survived because it had someone who would not quit.

THE SEA BEGGARS AND THE SIEGE OF LEIDEN

The turning point came from the water. Rebel sailors, called the Sea Beggars, attacked Spanish shipping from the coast. In April 1572 they captured the port of Brielle.

The capture owed a great deal to accident. The Sea Beggars were privateers, sailors who attacked enemy ships with a prince's permission, and they had been using English ports as their base. In the spring of 1572 Queen Elizabeth closed those ports to them. Some six hundred men in a fleet of small ships were suddenly without a harbor, and they went looking for one on the Dutch coast. They found Brielle almost undefended and took it on the first of April. A Dutch rhyme of the time plays on the word for spectacles, which sounds like the name of the town: on the first of April, Alba lost his glasses. The men who had landed there to find shelter had given the revolt a foothold.

Towns in Holland and Zeeland, two northern provinces, declared for William. In July the representatives of Holland met and recognized him as their stadtholderSTAD-hole-derIn the Low Countries, a governor who ruled a province in the name of a king or ruling council. See the glossary box.. Spain's troops fought back by laying siege to town after town. In 1574 they surrounded Leiden and began to starve it. The people ate dogs, rats, and boiled leather while they waited for help.

Thousands in the city died of hunger and plague that summer. The city's walls were strong, and the besiegers relied on hunger to do the work. William had no army strong enough to fight through the Spanish lines, and the only way to reach the city lay across flat land cut by waterways.

The help came from an idea that only a people living below sea level would have. The Dutch had spent centuries building dikesDYKEA wall of earth built to hold back water. See the glossary box. to keep the sea out. Now William ordered them cut to let the sea in. The flood spread over the low fields, and rebel ships with shallow bottoms sailed over farmland toward the city. The Spanish soldiers pulled back as the water rose. Tradition says a strong wind shifted at just the right moment and pushed the sea farther inland. On October 3 the ships reached the walls. The grateful prince rewarded the city with a university, which still stands.

The people of Leiden still celebrate the day with herring and white bread, the food the first boats carried to the starving. The university was founded the following year. A story says that William offered the city a choice between ending its taxes and founding a university, and that Leiden chose the university. Whether or not the choice was really offered, the university became one of the great centers of learning in Europe.

A KING WHO RAN OUT OF MONEY

Here the story links back to last week. Philip was fighting the Turks in the Mediterranean and the rebels in the north, and even his bullionBUL-yunGold or silver in bars or lumps, valued by weight rather than as coins. See the glossary box. from Peru and Mexico could not cover the bills. In 1575 his government went bankrupt. His unpaid soldiers mutinied, and in 1576 they sacked Antwerp, killing thousands of its people. The outrage briefly united all seventeen provinces. It did not last. The southern provinces, mostly Catholic, made peace with Philip by 1579. The northern provinces formed their own alliance, the Union of Utrecht. In 1581 they formally declared that Philip had lost the right to rule them because he had broken his duties as a king.

Follow the money to see why the soldiers turned on a city. Their pay was months and then years behind. A mutinous army is dangerous to everyone near it, and Antwerp was the richest place within reach. After the sack, even loyal Catholic provinces turned on the troops sent to protect them. Later a new Spanish commander, the Duke of Parma, won back the southern provinces one siege at a time, and in 1585 Antwerp itself fell. Many Protestant merchants and craftsmen left for the north, taking their skills and their capital with them. The line that divides the Netherlands from Belgium today follows roughly where Parma's armies stopped.

Philip answered by offering a reward for William's life. In 1584 an assassin shot William at his home in Delft. It was a harsh blow, and had the rebellion depended on William alone, it might have ended there. But his son Maurice rebuilt the army, and local councils governed in the name of the provinces. Each province kept its own stadtholderSTAD-hole-derIn the Low Countries, a governor who ruled a province in the name of a king or ruling council. See the glossary box. and its own councils, and in 1609 Spain agreed to a long truce. In 1648 Spain accepted Dutch independence. The Dutch Republic had no king. It was a union of towns and provinces, and its survival owed as much to luck and perseverance as to any plan.

Maurice was only seventeen when his father died, and he grew into a careful soldier. He paid his troops regularly, drilled them until they could fire and reload in rotation, and taught them to dig as well as to fight. In 1590 a small party of his men hid in the hold of a peat barge and took the city of Breda by surprise. The cities of Holland, the richest of the provinces, paid for most of the army. A republic of merchants could afford a better army than a king who owed his soldiers years of pay. Next you will see what that republic did with its freedom.
