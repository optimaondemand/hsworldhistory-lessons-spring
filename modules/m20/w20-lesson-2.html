🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

A small republic with no king, few natural resources, and a damp climate became the richest country in Europe. Today you will see how Dutch merchants did it, and what it cost.

A CITY BUILT ON MUD

Amsterdam sat on soft ground at the mouth of a small river. Its builders had to drive thousands of wooden piles into the mud to hold up a single house. It began as a fishing town, but in 1585 Spain captured Antwerp and closed the river that served it. Merchants, bankers, and skilled craftsmen fled north by the thousands, and many of them settled in Amsterdam. Within a few decades the city had tripled in size. It had gained experienced traders almost overnight, and it knew what to do with them.

SHIPS AND FREIGHT

The Dutch had little land, so they lived off the sea. They caught and salted herring, and they sailed to the Baltic to bring back grain, timber, and tar. This grain trade was so large that the Dutch called it the mother of all trades. A new kind of ship made it cheaper. The Dutch called it the fluytFLOYTA Dutch word for a broad, cheap cargo ship that needed only a small crew. See the glossary box.. It was broad, plain, and cheap to build, and it needed fewer sailors than rival ships. The Dutch used it to haul goods for other nations as well as their own. Soon they had the largest merchant fleet in Europe, and a Dutch captain would carry your cargo for less than your own countrymen would.

A NATION OF READERS

The Dutch were also unusually well educated. Many ordinary townspeople could read and do arithmetic, and the Republic's printers were bold. Amsterdam publishers produced maps, atlases, and books that were banned in other countries. Willem Blaeu's maps guided Dutch captains around the world. Thinkers who were not safe at home came to live in Dutch cities. The French philosopher René Descartes spent most of twenty years in the Republic, and Baruch Spinoza grew up in Amsterdam's Jewish community.

The lawyer Hugo Grotius gave the Republic's merchants an argument as well as a map. In 1609 he published a pamphlet claiming that the sea was open to the ships of every nation, so that Portugal had no right to shut the Asian trade to the Dutch. The VOC liked that argument. It did not always apply it to itself. Once its own ships had control of a trade, the company often shut out competitors. Principles are easiest to defend when they help your side.

THE EAST INDIA COMPANY

The most valuable cargo in the world was spice. Pepper, cloves, nutmeg, and cinnamon grew in Asia, and the Portuguese had controlled that trade for a century. In the 1590s Dutch merchants sent their own fleets to Asia. They soon found themselves competing against one another and driving up the price of spices at the source. In 1602 the government of the Republic brought them together. The result was the Dutch East India Company, known by its Dutch initials, the VOC.

The VOC was a joint-stock companyjoint-STOK KUM-puh-neeA business owned by many investors who each buy shares. Each owner risks only the money he or she put in. See the glossary box.. Anyone with money, from a rich merchant to a widow with some savings, could buy a share. If the voyages failed, an investor lost only what he or she had put in. Shares could be resold, and that was the clever part. A buyer no longer had to wait years for a fleet to return. He could sell his share to someone else in the meantime. THE SPICE ISLANDS

The company was a trading firm, but it also had soldiers, forts, and warships, and it behaved like a government. In 1619 it founded a headquarters at Batavia, on the island of Java. In 1621 its governor-general, Jan Pieterszoon Coen, attacked the Banda Islands, the main source of nutmeg in the world. The islanders had refused to sell only to the Dutch. Coen's forces killed or drove out or enslaved most of the people there and brought in enslaved laborers to work the nutmeg groves. This was the price of a monopoly. The riches of Amsterdam were not all earned by honest trade.

TOLERANCE AND REGENTS

The Dutch also became known for a practical kind of toleration. The Union of Utrecht had promised that no one would be questioned about his beliefs. Jews who had fled Spain and Portugal settled in Amsterdam and built a synagogue. French Protestants arrived after them. Catholics were allowed to worship quietly in hidden churches, though not in public. It was not equality. Only members of the Calvinist church held high office. But it was a tolerance with good business sense in it, and skilled people came to the country as a result.

The Republic had no king. Each town was run by a small circle of wealthy merchant families called regents. They governed through councils, and they usually preferred peace and low risks. The stadtholderSTAD-hole-derIn the Low Countries, a governor who ruled a province in the name of a king or ruling council. See the glossary box., the office William of Orange had once held, was a military command, and its holders often rivaled the regents. In 1618 the stadtholder Maurice quarreled with the leading statesman of Holland, Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, over religion and the war with Spain. The next year Oldenbarnevelt was executed for treason. A republic of merchants could be ruthless to its own leaders.

FREE, OR JUST RICH?

By the middle of the century Dutch painters such as Rembrandt were filling the walls of merchant houses with portraits. The Republic had solved its first-order problems better than any state of its day. It kept order, raised taxes people would pay, and defended itself with a navy. Its habits of thrift, hard work, and trust in contracts helped create wealth that a king could not command.

But the classical thinkers you have met would ask a harder question. Polybius and Machiavelli feared that riches could soften a people and turn citizens into customers. Was the Republic held together by common love of country, or only by profit? And how free was a prosperity that rested, in the Spice Islands and soon in the Atlantic, on the labor of people who had no freedom at all? The Dutch West India Company, founded in 1621, would carry thousands of enslaved Africans to the Americas. That brings us to next week.
