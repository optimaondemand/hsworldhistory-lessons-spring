🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

Twenty years after the Great War, the world went to war again, and this time it was larger and deadlier. Today you will follow the road from the Munich conference of 1938 to the end of the fighting in 1945, and you will ask at each turn whether the outcome had to be what it was.

HITLER TESTS THE PEACE

Once in power, Adolf Hitler broke the Treaty of Versailles one clause at a time. In 1935 he announced that Germany would rebuild its army. In March 1936 he sent troops into the Rhineland, which the treaty had kept free of German soldiers. His generals were nervous, and some had orders to retreat if France resisted. France did nothing. Britain, still grieving the dead of the last war, saw little reason to fight over German soldiers marching into German territory. Italy under Mussolini, the first leader of fascismFASH-iz-umA political movement that puts the nation and its leader above the individual, rules through one party, and uses force against opponents. See the glossary box., drew closer to Germany, and the two formed a partnership known as the Axis. Japan, whose army was conquering parts of China, later joined them.

Other fires were already burning. In 1936 a civil war began in Spain, and Hitler and Mussolini sent planes, tanks, and men to General Franco while the Soviet Union backed the Republic. In April 1937 German bombers destroyed the Basque town of Guernica, a warning of what air power could do to cities. In 1937 Japan launched a full invasion of China, and in the winter its troops killed civilians and prisoners by the tens of thousands at Nanjing.

In March 1938 Hitler annexed Austria, which was welcomed by many Austrians and opposed by none of the great powers. His next target was the Sudetenland, a border region of Czechoslovakia where about three million Germans lived. The Czechs had strong mountain defenses, a modern army, and an alliance with France.

MUNICH, 1938

In September 1938 the leaders of Britain, France, Germany, and Italy met in Munich. No Czech was in the room. Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain of Britain and Premier Edouard Daladier of France agreed that Germany could take the Sudetenland. This was the policy of appeasementuh-PEEZ-muntThe policy of giving in to the demands of an aggressive nation in the hope of avoiding war. See the glossary box.. Chamberlain was an honest man who had seen what war cost and who believed that Hitler wanted only to correct the wrongs of Versailles. He returned to London waving a paper and speaking of "peace for our time."

Chamberlain, who was sixty-nine, took an airplane for the first time that month, and he flew to Germany three times in two weeks. Hitler raised his demands at each meeting. Britain and France told the Czech government to give up the Sudetenland or face Germany alone, and the Soviet Union, which had a treaty with Czechoslovakia that depended on French action, was not asked to come. On October 5 Winston Churchill, then out of office, told the House of Commons, "We have sustained a total and unmitigated defeat." Many members disagreed.

Chamberlain's reasons were not foolish. Britain's army was small, its air defenses were incomplete, and the public did not want war. Yet the choice had a cost. Czechoslovakia lost its mountain defenses, and in March 1939 Hitler took the rest of the country. This broke his promise and showed that his aims went beyond Germans living outside Germany. Britain and France now promised to defend Poland, which was clearly Hitler's next target. Some historians argue that war in 1938 would have found Germany weaker and the Allies better placed. Others reply that Britain and its dominions were not ready, and that the delay gave them time to build fighter planes and radar. Think about both claims as you prepare for this week's discussion.

THE PACT AND THE INVASION

In August 1939 the world was stunned. Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union, bitter enemies in word and deed, signed a non-aggression pact. In a secret part of the agreement, they divided Poland and the Baltic lands between them. Hitler was now free to attack without fear of a war on two fronts, and Stalin hoped to gain time and territory. On September 1, 1939, German forces invaded Poland. Britain and France declared war two days later. Soviet troops entered Poland from the east on September 17, and Poland was crushed in weeks.

Stalin had tried the Western powers first. In the summer of 1939 Britain and France sent a mission to Moscow by slow ship and gave it little power to make promises. Poland refused to let Soviet troops cross its land, since its leaders feared they would never leave. Stalin also suspected that the West hoped to turn Hitler eastward, and he turned to Hitler's foreign minister, Joachim von Ribbentrop, who flew to Moscow in August. In November the Soviet Union attacked Finland, which held out for months, and in 1940 the Soviets shot about twenty-two thousand captured Polish officers and officials at Katyn and other places.

THE FALL OF FRANCE

Armored divisions, supported by dive bombers, broke through and surrounded the Polish armies before they could regroup. After a quiet winter, the FührerFYOO-rerA German word meaning "leader." It was the title Adolf Hitler took for himself. See the glossary box. struck west. In April 1940 Germany seized Denmark and Norway. On May 10 it attacked the Netherlands, Belgium, and France.

The French had the Maginot Line, a chain of strong forts along the German border, and a large army. The Germans went around it. They sent tanks through the Ardennes, a hilly forest that French planners had judged too difficult for armor. The tanks reached the English Channel in ten days and trapped the British and some French forces at Dunkirk. A sudden order halted the German tanks for a time, and over nine days more than 330,000 men were taken off the beaches by navy ships and a fleet of small private boats. Paris fell on June 14, and France signed an armistice on June 22. A plan that most experts had called risky had worked, and the war in the west that was meant to last years was over in six weeks.

The German plan nearly went another way. At first the generals meant to repeat the old Schlieffen Plan through Belgium, but a staff officer, Erich von Manstein, urged a thrust through the Ardennes, and Hitler took up the idea. The French kept their best troops in the north and their reserves thin. On May 13 German tanks crossed the Meuse River at Sedan, and a week later they reached the sea. On May 24 the tanks halted for about three days, which gave the British time to hold a ring around Dunkirk. Almost all of the army's heavy equipment was left on the beaches, and Churchill told Parliament on June 4, "Wars are not won by evacuations." Hitler had the armistice signed in the same railway car in the forest of Compiègne where Germany had signed in 1918. A French general, Charles de Gaulle, broadcast from London on June 18 that the war was not lost. In the town of Vichy, a government under Marshal Pétain, the hero of Verdun, ruled the unoccupied south of France.

BRITAIN STANDS ALONE

Chamberlain had resigned in May, and Winston Churchill, long a critic of appeasement, became prime minister. He refused all talk of peace. Hitler needed to win control of the air before he could invade across the Channel, so in the summer and autumn of 1940 the German air force attacked British airfields and then British cities. In this Battle of Britain, fighter pilots flying Spitfires and Hurricanes, guided by a new radar network, held on. Hitler called off the invasion, and the Luftwaffe turned to night bombing of cities, the Blitz.

The Battle of Britain was close. The Royal Air Force had fewer planes than the Germans but fought over its own land, with a chain of radar stations and a system of ground controllers that sent each squadron where it was needed. On August 20 Churchill said of the pilots, "Never in the field of human conflict was so much owed by so many to so few." In early September, after British bombers struck Berlin, Hitler switched the main attack from the airfields to London. That gave the battered fighter squadrons time to recover. On September 15 a great German raid was turned back, and the invasion was put off.

Britain could not have lasted long without help. President Franklin Roosevelt, who faced an isolationist public, found ways to give it. See the glossary box., which let America supply Britain, and later the Soviet Union and others, with weapons and goods. Roosevelt called the United States the "arsenal of democracy."

THE WAR WIDENS

On June 22, 1941, Hitler broke his pact with Stalin and invaded the Soviet Union with about three million men. Stalin had ignored many warnings, and the Red Army, whose officer corps he had purged, suffered enormous losses. German armies came within sight of Moscow, but the Russian winter and fresh Soviet divisions stopped them in December.

Hitler's plan, which he called Barbarossa, sent three great armies east along a front of nearly two thousand miles. In the first months the Germans took millions of prisoners, many of whom died in captivity. Hitler meant to settle Germans on the land and treat the people already living on it as expendable. Stalin had ignored warnings from spies and from Britain. When the Germans neared Moscow, the cold fell far below zero and German troops lacked winter clothing. On December 5 fresh Soviet divisions brought from Siberia struck back.

That same month, the war became global. Japan, whose conquests in China had led America to cut off its oil, decided to seize the oil-rich Dutch East Indies and Southeast Asia. To protect that move it planned a surprise strike on the American Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor, Hawaii. On December 7, 1941, Japanese carrier planes sank or damaged much of the battle fleet and killed more than 2,400 Americans. The American carriers were away at sea. Roosevelt asked Congress for a declaration of war, which you will read. Hitler then declared war on the United States, a choice that many historians think he did not need to make.

Japan's choice was a gamble by men who knew they could not win a long war with America. The United States had cut off oil sales in 1941, and Japan's fuel would run out within a couple of years. Admiral Yamamoto, who had studied in America and doubted that the war could be won, planned the strike, and six carriers crossed the Pacific in radio silence. Japan attacked British Malaya and Hong Kong and the American Philippines at the same time, and Singapore fell in February 1942. On December 8 Roosevelt told Congress that December 7 was "a date which will live in infamy." Hitler and Mussolini declared war on the United States on December 11.

THE TIDE TURNS

By mid-1942 Germany and Japan held vast territory. Then came the turn. In June 1942 American dive bombers sank four Japanese carriers at Midway, a loss from which Japan's navy never recovered. In North Africa, the British under Montgomery won at El Alamein in November. At Stalingrad, on the Volga River, a German army of more than 200,000 was surrounded and in February 1943 surrendered. Hitler had forbidden retreat, and the Soviets began a long advance west.

At Midway the Americans had broken Japan's codes and knew where the blow would fall. On June 4, as Japanese planes were being refueled and rearmed on the carrier decks, American dive bombers arrived overhead, and within minutes three carriers were burning. A fourth sank later that day. At Stalingrad, in November 1942, Soviet armies swung around both sides of the German Sixth Army and shut it in. Hitler would not let its commander, Friedrich Paulus, break out. About ninety thousand starving survivors surrendered, and few of them ever saw Germany again.

The Western Allies invaded Italy in 1943, and Mussolini was toppled. On June 6, 1944, called D-Day, General Dwight Eisenhower led the largest sea invasion in history onto the beaches of Normandy. Hitler's last great gamble, a counterattack through the Ardennes in December 1944, failed. Soviet troops entered Berlin in April 1945. Hitler took his own life on April 30, and Germany surrendered on May 8.

The invasion nearly came on the wrong day. Storms forced Eisenhower to wait, and a gap in the weather let the landings begin on June 6. The Allies had used fake armies and false radio messages to make the Germans expect an attack at Calais, farther north. About 156,000 troops landed from the sea and air that day on five beaches, and at Omaha Beach the Americans suffered heavy losses. Eisenhower had written a note taking the blame in case the landings failed.

THE PACIFIC AND THE BOMB

In the Pacific, American forces advanced island by island, with Marines and soldiers paying heavily for each one. The battles of Iwo Jima and Okinawa in 1945 showed how fiercely Japan would defend its home islands. Planners feared that an invasion might cost many lives on both sides. Meanwhile American scientists had built the atomic bomb. Roosevelt had died in April, and the decision fell to President Harry Truman.

Iwo Jima cost nearly seven thousand American lives, and almost all of its twenty thousand defenders fought to the death. On Okinawa more than twelve thousand Americans died, and so did many times that number of Japanese soldiers and civilians. In March 1945 American bombers burned much of Tokyo in a single night. Truman learned of the bomb only after he became president. On July 16 the first test took place in the desert of New Mexico, and ten days later the Allies called on Japan to surrender or face destruction.

On August 6, 1945, an atomic bomb destroyed Hiroshima, and on August 9 a second struck Nagasaki. Between those dates, the Soviet Union declared war on Japan and attacked Japanese forces in Manchuria.

The Japanese government did not yield at once. On the night of August 9 and 10 the six members of its inner council split three against three over whether to accept the Allied terms on the single condition that the emperor remain. The prime minister asked Hirohito to decide, and he chose to end the war. Some young officers tried to stop the surrender by seizing the palace, and they failed.

On August 15 Emperor Hirohito told his people by radio that Japan would surrender. Historians still debate how much the bombs and how much the Soviet entry persuaded Japan's leaders to yield, and whether other choices were open to Truman. The war ended formally on September 2. It had killed tens of millions of people, most of them civilians. In the next lesson you will study the greatest crime committed within it.
