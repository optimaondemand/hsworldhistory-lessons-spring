🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

This lesson deals with the murder of millions of people. It asks you to read carefully and soberly. The goal is to understand how it happened, who made it happen, who resisted, and what the world did afterward.

THE ROOTS OF HATRED

Hatred of Jews, called antisemitism, was far older than the Nazis. For centuries in Europe, Jews had been barred from many trades, forced into separate quarters, and blamed for plagues, wars, and economic troubles. Hitler gave this hatred a new and deadly form. He taught that humanity was divided into races, that the Germans were a superior one, and that the Jews were a poison in the body of the nation. This false idea of race was not science, but it was backed by the power of a modern state.

In 1933 about half a million Jews lived in Germany, less than one percent of the population. Many were proud citizens who had fought for Germany in the last war. Within weeks of taking power, the Nazis began a boycott of Jewish shops. Jews were dismissed from the civil service, then from universities and the professions. The Nuremberg Laws of 1935, which you will read, took away German citizenship from Jews and forbade marriage between Jews and other Germans. These were the first stages of persecutionper-sih-KYOO-shunCruel and unjust treatment of people because of who they are or what they believe. The Nuremberg Laws defined a Jew by ancestry, not by belief. A person with three or four Jewish grandparents counted as a Jew, even if that person had been baptized as a Christian or had never set foot in a synagogue. In the schools, new textbooks taught the idea of race, and the weekly newspaper Der Stürmer spread vile cartoons of Jews on street corners. In May 1933 students and party members burned books by Jewish authors and others in the squares of German cities. Each step was taken in public, and each was met with little protest.

FROM LAWS TO VIOLENCE

On November 9 and 10, 1938, the Nazis used the shooting of a German diplomat in Paris by a young Jew as an excuse. Mobs, led by party members, burned synagogues, smashed shop windows, and beat and killed Jews. About ninety were murdered, and some thirty thousand Jewish men were sent to concentration camps. See the glossary box.. Afterward Jews were forced to pay for the damage, and the pressure to leave grew. About half of Germany's Jews did emigrate before the war began, but many countries, including the United States, limited the number they would admit.

The diplomat, Ernst vom Rath, had been shot by a seventeen-year-old Jewish refugee, Herschel Grynszpan, whose family had been driven from Germany. The attacks were organized from above. Fire brigades were told to protect only the nearby buildings that were not Jewish, and the police were told not to interfere. About 1,400 synagogues and prayer rooms were damaged or burned. Thirty-two nations had already met at the French town of Evian in July 1938 to discuss the refugees, and nearly all of them declined to take more. In May 1939 a ship, the St. Louis, carried more than nine hundred Jewish refugees to Cuba, which refused them. The United States did not admit them either, and the ship returned to Europe, where many of its passengers were later killed.

When Germany conquered Poland in 1939, it took in some three million Polish Jews, the largest Jewish community in Europe. The Germans forced them into crowded districts sealed by walls, each called a ghettoGET-ohA walled or fenced part of a city where a group of people is forced to live, cut off from the rest of the population. See the glossary box.. The Warsaw ghetto held about 400,000 people in a few square miles. Hunger, cold, and disease killed tens of thousands, and the Germans meant them to.

Life inside the ghetto was shaped by rules the Jews did not make. The Warsaw ghetto was walled off in 1940, and the food rations allowed to its people came to less than two hundred calories a day, so that smuggling by children became the way many families lived. The Germans made councils of Jewish leaders carry out their orders, which placed them in terrible dilemmas. A doctor and writer, Janusz Korczak, ran an orphanage in the ghetto, and in 1942 he refused offers to leave and went with his children when they were taken away. In the summer of that year the Germans began to empty the ghetto, and in about two months more than a quarter of a million people were sent by train to Treblinka.

MASS MURDER BEGINS

When Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, killing took a new form. Special units called Einsatzgruppen followed the army. With help from local police and army units, they rounded up Jewish men, women, and children, took them to the edge of towns, and shot them into pits. At Babi Yar, near Kiev, more than thirty thousand were shot in two days in September 1941. By the end of 1941 hundreds of thousands had been killed in this way.

The shootings were slow and hard on the men who carried them out, and German leaders looked for a more efficient method. Experiments with poison gas had already been used in a program of murder against disabled Germans. In January 1942 senior officials met at a villa at Wannsee, near Berlin, to coordinate what they called the Final Solution of the Jewish question. You will read part of the record of that meeting. It lists about eleven million Jews across Europe. Its language is dry and official, and that is part of what makes it so chilling.

The meeting did not begin the killing, which was already underway in the East. It brought together officials from many ministries so that the railways, the police, and the civil service would work together. Heydrich, the head of the security service, led it, and Adolf Eichmann took the minutes. Within months the camps in Poland were killing thousands of people each day. A crime on this scale was not the work of a few men in uniform. It required timetables, trains, lists, and clerks.

THE KILLING CAMPS

The Germans built camps whose main purpose was killing, at Chelmno, Belzec, Sobibor, and Treblinka in occupied Poland. At Auschwitz-Birkenau and Majdanek, killing centers stood beside forced-labor camps. Trains carried Jews from nearly every country the Germans controlled or influenced, from France and the Netherlands to Greece and Hungary. Many were told that they were being resettled. Most were killed within hours of arrival, in gas chambers. A smaller number were selected for forced labor and died of hunger, disease, beating, or exhaustion. At Auschwitz alone, about one million Jews were murdered, along with tens of thousands of others.

In all, about six million Jews died, roughly two of every three Jews in Europe. This was a genocideJEN-uh-sydThe deliberate attempt to destroy a whole people, religion, or nation, in whole or in part. See the glossary box., the attempt to destroy a whole people. The Nazis also murdered about two hundred thousand Roma, and killed disabled Germans, Soviet prisoners of war, and millions of Polish and Soviet civilians. Political prisoners, Jehovah's Witnesses, and others were sent to the camps. Yet the Jews were the one people the regime intended to kill down to the last person.

The killing went on as Germany was losing the war. In the spring of 1944, after the Germans occupied Hungary, nearly 440,000 Hungarian Jews were sent to Auschwitz in less than two months, at a time when the railways were needed for the army. In January 1945, as Soviet troops drew near, the guards forced tens of thousands of prisoners to march west in the winter, and many were shot or died of cold and exhaustion along the way.

COMPLICITY, INDIFFERENCE, AND RESISTANCE

A crime of this size needed many hands. Railway clerks, bureaucrats, police, soldiers, doctors, engineers, and businessmen all played a part. Many were not fanatics. Historians have studied police battalions of older, middle-class men who shot thousands of Jews at close range, and found that most of them did so even though they had been told they could step aside. Others saw what was happening and said nothing, out of fear, habit, or hate.

One such unit, Reserve Police Battalion 101, was made up of middle-aged men from Hamburg, many of them workers and clerks who had never been soldiers. On the morning of July 13, 1942, in the Polish village of Józefów, their commander told them that they were to round up the Jews of the village and shoot most of them, and he offered to excuse anyone who did not want to take part. Only about a dozen of nearly five hundred men stepped out at first. Within a few hours, the village's Jews were dead.

Yet some resisted. In April 1943 starving Jewish fighters in the Warsaw ghetto rose up with a few smuggled pistols and homemade bombs and held out for almost a month. Jews also revolted at Treblinka and Sobibor. In Denmark, ordinary citizens helped ferry nearly all of the country's roughly seven thousand Jews to neutral Sweden in October 1943. In the French village of Le Chambon, villagers led by a Protestant pastor hid thousands of Jewish refugees. Individuals such as Raoul Wallenberg, a Swedish diplomat in Budapest, and Oskar Schindler, a German factory owner, saved many lives. Such rescuers were rare. The nations that fought Germany also made hard choices. Allied leaders did not bomb the rail lines to Auschwitz, though they had been told of the camp, and historians debate what was possible.

Rescue was dangerous. In occupied Poland, the penalty for hiding a Jew was death for the whole household, yet more Poles have been honored as rescuers than the people of any other country. In Denmark, a German official, Georg Duckwitz, warned Danish leaders of the planned roundup, and fishing boats took the Jews across the narrow sea in a few nights. Raoul Wallenberg issued protective papers to thousands of Jews in Budapest in 1944, and Schindler protected about twelve hundred workers in his factory. The Allies had information as well. A Polish officer, Witold Pilecki, had allowed himself to be arrested in 1940 so that he could report from inside Auschwitz, and a Polish courier, Jan Karski, described the killing to Roosevelt in 1943.

    LIBERATION AND JUSTICE

    As Allied armies advanced in 1945, they came upon the camps. Soviet troops reached Auschwitz in January. American and British troops entered Buchenwald, Dachau, and Bergen-Belsen in April. Soldiers who had thought they understood war were shaken by what they found: bodies stacked in the open, and survivors who were near death. General Eisenhower ordered local civilians to be brought to see the camps, and he invited journalists and members of Congress to witness them so that no one could say later that it had not happened. He wrote to General Marshall that he had gone deliberately, in order to give firsthand evidence if anyone ever dismissed the reports as propaganda.

    From November 1945 to October 1946, the Allies tried the surviving top Nazi leaders at Nuremberg. The court held that obeying orders was not a defense for crimes against humanity. Twelve defendants were sentenced to death, and several others received prison terms. Three were found not guilty. Hermann Göring, the head of the air force, took poison in his cell the night before he was to be hanged. Many smaller trials followed. A Polish-Jewish lawyer, Raphael Lemkin, who lost nearly his whole family in the Holocaust, had coined the word genocide in 1944, and the United Nations adopted a convention against the crime in 1948. The survivors, left homeless in many cases, faced the question of where they could go. You will see in a coming lesson how that question helped shape the creation of Israel.

    Allied leaders were told of Auschwitz but did not bomb the rail lines leading to it. How certain can we be about what bombing would have changed?
