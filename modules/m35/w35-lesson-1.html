🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

In 1945 European empires ruled about a third of humanity. By 1975 almost all of it was independent. Today you will see why the empires gave way, how India won freedom and was divided in the process, and how different the roads to independence could be.

WHY THE EMPIRES WEAKENED

Several forces worked together. The war had drained Britain, France, and the Netherlands of money and men, and Japan's early victories in 1941 and 1942 had shown Asians that European armies could be beaten. Colonial subjects had served in the war and had been told it was fought for freedom. The Atlantic Charter of 1941, signed by Churchill and Roosevelt, spoke of the right of all peoples to choose their government. Churchill said it applied to Europe. Many others saw no reason it should stop there.

Rising nationalismNASH-uh-nul-iz-umStrong loyalty to one's own nation and the wish for it to govern itself. See the glossary box. mattered most. Educated leaders, many trained in London or Paris, had learned the language of liberty and used it against their rulers. The two new superpowers, the United States and the Soviet Union, both opposed old-style empires, though for different reasons. Imperial rule also now cost more than it earned. The result was decolonizationdee-kol-uh-nih-ZAY-shunThe process by which colonies gained independence from the empires that ruled them. See the glossary box.. It came peacefully in some places and by long war in others.

GANDHI AND THE ROAD TO INDEPENDENCE

Britain's most valued possession was India, home to some 300 million people by the 1930s. The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, had long asked for a larger share in government. In 1930 he led a march of some 240 miles to the sea to make salt in defiance of the British tax on it. Tens of thousands were jailed.

Gandhi was not alone. Nehru, a barrister from a wealthy family and a gifted writer, spoke for the modern, secular side of the movement, while Gandhi spoke the language of the villages. Their partnership brought together very different groups and kept Congress broad enough to claim that it spoke for all of India. The British answered with a mix of reform and force, and in 1919 troops opened fire on an unarmed crowd at Amritsar, killing hundreds, which turned many Indians against British rule for good.

Gandhi's method depended on an opponent who could be shamed by the sight of suffering. The British, having a free press and a conscience of their own, could be moved in this way. Historians note that the same tactics would have met a different fate against a regime like Stalin's or Hitler's. In 1942 Congress demanded that Britain quit India, and Gandhi and other leaders were jailed for most of the war.

PARTITION

About one in four Indians was Muslim. Many Muslims feared being a permanent minority in a state ruled by a Hindu majority. The leader of the Muslim League, Muhammad Ali Jinnah, a lawyer who had once worked with Congress, came to demand a separate Muslim homeland, Pakistan. Gandhi and Jawaharlal Nehru, his chief ally, opposed dividing the country. Talks failed, and in August 1946 riots in Calcutta killed thousands.

A new British government under Clement Attlee decided to leave. The last viceroy, Lord Mountbatten, was sent in early 1947 and set a date for independence that was earlier than expected. A British lawyer who had never seen India, Cyril Radcliffe, was given a few weeks to draw the new borders through Punjab and Bengal. On August 15, 1947, India became independent, and Pakistan was born a day earlier in two parts, far apart on the map. The partitionpar-TISH-unThe division of a country or territory into two or more separate parts. See the glossary box. set off one of the largest migrations in history. Perhaps ten to fifteen million people crossed the new lines, Hindus and Sikhs one way and Muslims the other. Mobs on all sides murdered their neighbors, and the dead are counted in the hundreds of thousands, with some estimates going to a million or more. Gandhi, who walked through riot-torn villages pleading for peace, was shot dead in January 1948 by a Hindu extremist who thought he had been too kind to Muslims. Nehru became India's first prime minister and led it as a democracy that has lasted ever since. Pakistan and India have fought several wars since.

SOUTHEAST ASIA

The Philippines, promised independence by the United States, received it in 1946. Elsewhere the road was harder. When Japan surrendered, Indonesian nationalists led by Sukarno declared independence, and the Dutch fought to regain control. After four years, with American pressure against them, the Dutch gave way in 1949.

In Vietnam, Ho Chi Minh, a communist who had spent years abroad, declared independence in September 1945. France refused to accept it, and war followed. In 1954 the French suffered a crushing defeat at Dien Bien Phu, and a peace conference at Geneva divided the country at the 17th parallel, with a communist north and a non-communist south. The Cold War turned this division into a long struggle involving America, whose own war there lies beyond this lesson.

THE MIDDLE EAST AND AFRICA

In 1956 Egypt's president, Gamal Abdel Nasser, seized the Suez Canal, which Britain and France owned. They joined Israel in an attack, but the United States and the Soviet Union both demanded they stop, and they withdrew. The affair showed that Britain and France were no longer great powers able to act alone.

In Africa, the Gold Coast, led by Kwame Nkrumah, became the first sub-Saharan colony to win independence, as Ghana, in 1957. Many leaders followed, and in 1960 alone seventeen African nations became independent. Britain left most of its colonies by negotiation. In Kenya a rebellion by the Mau Mau movement was put down, but the British still moved toward independence in 1963. France gave up most of its African lands in 1960, but Algeria, home to over a million French settlers, was different. A savage war lasted from 1954 to 1962, and the war brought down France's Fourth Republic and brought Charles de Gaulle back to power. He finally granted independence, and most of the settlers fled. In the Belgian Congo, Belgium left in 1960 with almost no preparation. The country plunged into civil war within days, and its first prime minister, Patrice Lumumba, was murdered in 1961.

The new states often inherited borders drawn by Europeans that cut across peoples and joined enemies, and many soon fell under one-party or military rule. Independence was a real achievement, and the way each country got there shaped what came after. In the next lesson you will see how one more mandate, Palestine, ended in the birth of a state.
