🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

Two peoples have deep ties to one small piece of land, and each tells the story of 1948 differently. Today you will learn how the state of Israel came to be, what that meant for Jews and for Arabs, and how war and diplomacy have followed since. Try to hold both accounts in mind as you read.

A LAND WITH TWO CLAIMS

The land called Palestine lay in the Ottoman Empire for four hundred years. Most of its people were Arab Muslims, with Christian Arabs and a small Jewish community. For Jews it was the ancient homeland, the land of David and the Temple, from which their ancestors had been exiled by Rome, and toward which they had prayed for nearly two thousand years. For Arabs it was the home in which their families had lived for generations.

In the late 1800s, many Jews in Russia and Eastern Europe suffered from violent attacks, and in France the Dreyfus Affair, in which a Jewish officer was falsely convicted of treason, showed that hatred was alive even in a free country. The journalist Theodor Herzl concluded that Jews would never be safe until they had a state of their own. In 1897 he held the First Zionist Congress in Switzerland. The movement he started was called ZionismZY-uh-niz-umThe movement, begun in the late 1800s, to create a Jewish homeland in the land of Israel. See the glossary box.. Jews began to settle in Palestine, buying land and building farms and towns.

PROMISES IN WAR

During the Great War, Britain made promises that could not all be kept. In 1915 and 1916 a British official promised the Arab leader Hussein of Mecca support for an Arab kingdom if the Arabs rose against the Ottomans, who had sided with Germany. Britain and France also agreed in secret, in the Sykes-Picot Agreement, to divide much of the region between them. And in November 1917, the Balfour Declaration stated that Britain viewed "with favour the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people," while adding that nothing should harm the rights of the existing communities.

After the war Britain received Palestine as a mandate, as you saw earlier. Jewish immigration rose, especially after Hitler took power. Arab leaders feared losing their land and their future. Tensions turned to violence, and an Arab revolt lasted from 1936 to 1939. Britain, wishing to keep Arab goodwill as war approached, issued a White Paper in 1939 that sharply limited Jewish immigration, at the moment when Jews most needed a refuge. During the Holocaust, many who might have been saved were turned away.

PARTITION AND WAR, 1947 TO 1949

After the war, tens of thousands of Jewish survivors waited in camps in Europe, and many tried to reach Palestine by sea. Britain, worn out and unable to satisfy either side, handed the problem to the United Nations. In November 1947 the General Assembly voted to recommend the partitionpar-TISH-unThe division of a country or territory into two or more separate parts. See the glossary box. of Palestine into a Jewish state and an Arab state, with Jerusalem under international control. Jewish leaders accepted the plan. Arab leaders rejected it, arguing that it gave too much to a minority that owned a small share of the land.

Fighting began at once. On May 14, 1948, David Ben-Gurion, the head of the Jewish community, proclaimed the State of Israel, as the British left. The next day armies from Egypt, Jordan, Syria, Iraq, and Lebanon entered the country. The Israelis, though outnumbered in population, were better organized, and after months of war they held more territory than the United Nations plan had given them. Jordan took the West Bank and East Jerusalem, and Egypt held Gaza. Armistice agreements in 1949 ended the fighting but no peace followed.

THE WARS OF 1956, 1967, AND 1973

The new state was surrounded by hostile neighbors. In 1956 it joined Britain and France against Egypt in the Suez crisis and captured the Sinai Peninsula, which it later gave back. In 1967 Egypt's Nasser expelled United Nations peacekeepers from Sinai, blockaded Israel's southern port, and massed troops on the border while Syria and Jordan prepared to join. Israel struck first. In six days it destroyed the Egyptian air force and took the Sinai and Gaza from Egypt, the West Bank and East Jerusalem from Jordan, and the Golan Heights from Syria. The old city of Jerusalem, with its holy places, came under Israeli control. Israel won the war, but now ruled over more than a million Palestinians.

In October 1973, on Yom Kippur, the holiest day of the Jewish year, Egypt and Syria attacked by surprise. Israel, nearly caught off guard, recovered after heavy losses. The Palestine Liberation Organization, founded in 1964 and led after 1969 by Yasser Arafat, used guerrilla raids and terrorist attacks against Israel, while Israel struck back.

STEPS TOWARD PEACE

The war of 1973 changed the thinking of Egypt's president, Anwar Sadat. In 1977 he flew to Jerusalem and spoke to the Israeli parliament, a bold step no Arab leader had taken. President Jimmy Carter hosted Sadat and Israeli Prime Minister Menachem Begin at Camp David in 1978, and in 1979 Egypt and Israel signed a peace treaty, with Israel returning the Sinai. Sadat was assassinated by extremists in 1981, but the peace held. In 1993, after secret talks in Norway, Israel and the PLO signed the Oslo Accords, which created a Palestinian Authority to govern parts of the West Bank and Gaza and promised further talks. Israel and Jordan made peace in 1994.

Peace between governments proved easier than peace between peoples. Many Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza lived under Israeli military rule after 1967, and in 1987 a widespread uprising began there. Israelis, for their part, faced bombings and rocket fire that made them wary of giving up land. Each side came to the table with real fears and real grievances, and each could point to leaders on the other side who seemed unwilling to compromise.

The hardest questions were left unsettled: the borders, the status of Jerusalem, the future of the refugees, and Israeli settlements in the West Bank. The prime minister who signed Oslo, Yitzhak Rabin, was assassinated in 1995 by an Israeli extremist who opposed it. The history of the region shows how promises made in war, the failures of great powers, and the choices of single leaders shaped a conflict that still goes on.
