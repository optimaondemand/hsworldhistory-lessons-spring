🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

England had cut off one king's head. Forty years later it removed another king without a battle. Today you will see how the invitation to a Dutch prince, a lucky wind, and a list of rights created a government that the rest of Europe would study.

A KING RETURNS

After Cromwell's death, England was tired of experiments. In 1660 Charles II came home to cheers. He was witty, clever, and wiser than his father. He had learned that a king who wanted to keep his head should not push Parliament too far. Parliament kept its control of taxes. In 1679 it passed a law protecting , which meant that no one could be held in prison without being brought before a judge.

The trouble was the succession. Charles had no legitimate children, so his brother James, an open Catholic, was next in line. Parliament split over whether to bar him. Those who wanted to bar him were later called Whigs. Those who defended his right to inherit were called Tories. Charles protected his brother, and he ruled his last years without calling Parliament. When he died in 1685, he had secretly become a Catholic.

A CATHOLIC KING

Many Protestants put up with James II because his two daughters, Mary and Anne, were Protestants. The crown would pass to them one day. They did not have to wait long to see his plans. When a rebellion led by Charles II's illegitimate son was crushed in 1685, the king's judge hanged hundreds. THE BABY AND THE INVITATION

In June 1688 James's wife gave birth to a son. Now a Catholic boy stood ahead of his Protestant sisters. A rumor spread that the baby had been smuggled into the room in a warming pan. It was untrue, but it shows how frightened people were.

Not everyone saw the baby as a Protestant threat. Some saw in him the rightful heir, and later his followers would be called Jacobites, from Jacobus, the Latin form of James. They would rise in rebellion in Scotland in 1715 and again in 1745.

Within weeks, seven leading Englishmen sent a secret letter to William of Orange. William was the Dutch stadtholder and the great-grandson of William the Silent. He was married to Mary, James's Protestant daughter. He had his own reasons to say yes. Louis XIV threatened the Dutch Republic, and William wanted England as an ally rather than as a friend of France.

THE PROTESTANT WIND

In November 1688 William sailed with about 450 ships and 20,000 men, a fleet larger than the Spanish Armada. For days an east wind kept James's navy bottled up in port, and it carried William's fleet down the Channel. When the wind turned west, William landed at Torbay on November 5. People called it the Protestant wind.

William marched slowly toward London. James had the larger army, but his officers began to desert him. One was John Churchill, the future Duke of Marlborough. Another was James's own daughter Anne. James lost heart and fled to France in December. Had the wind blown the other way, or had James stood and fought, England might have had another civil war. As it was, there was almost no fighting in England.

A NEW SETTLEMENT

In early 1689 Parliament declared that James had abandoned the throne. It offered the crown to William and Mary together, on one condition. They had to accept a list of limits on royal power, which became the Bill of Rights. The king could not suspend laws or make them without Parliament. He could not tax without its consent, and he could not keep a standing army in peacetime unless Parliament agreed. Elections had to be free, and members of Parliament could speak freely in debate. Subjects had the right to petition the king, and cruel punishments were forbidden. Protestants could keep arms for their defense, and no Catholic could wear the crown.

England had become a constitutional monarchykon-stih-TOO-shun-ul MON-ar-keeA government in which a king or queen reigns but must obey the laws and the limits of a constitution. See the glossary box.. The king was still powerful, but he now ruled under rules he did not control. In the same year the Toleration Act allowed Protestants who disagreed with the Church of England to hold their own services. Catholics were not included.

A KING AND QUEEN

William and Mary were an unusual pair. Mary was warm and popular and had been reluctant to take her father's throne. William was cold in public, asthmatic, and more interested in his war against Louis than in English politics. When Mary died of smallpox in 1694, he ruled alone. He died in 1702 after his horse stumbled in a molehill. Jacobites drank a toast to "the little gentleman in black velvet," the mole.

The throne passed to Mary's sister Anne. When none of her children lived, Parliament passed the Act of Settlement in 1701, which gave the crown to a Protestant German cousin, George of Hanover. In 1714 he became King George I. Parliament had chosen the king, which no one could have imagined in 1600.

DUTCH GIFTS

William brought more than a fleet. He brought Dutch ways of finance. In 1694 England chartered the Bank of England, and Parliament promised to repay the king's loans from taxes. Lenders trusted a Parliament more than a king, so England could borrow enormous sums. That power would help it fight Louis XIV for the next twenty years. The merchants of Amsterdam had taught Europe how credit worked, and England learned the lesson.

WAS IT GLORIOUS?

Its defenders called it glorious because it protected Protestantism, limited the crown, and was nearly bloodless. But it was not welcomed everywhere. James fought on in Ireland until William beat him at the Boyne in 1690. In Scotland, the Jacobite cause kept its support in the Highlands, and a massacre at Glencoe in 1692 deepened the bitterness. In England, the vote remained limited to a small share of men.

Even so, the settlement worked. King, Lords, and Commons now balanced one another, much as Polybius had praised in Rome's mixed constitution. Visitors from other lands noticed, and next week you will meet the thinkers who wrote about it.
