🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

England had cut off one king's head. Forty years later it removed another king without a battle. Today you will see how the invitation to a Dutch prince, a lucky wind, and a list of rights created a government that the rest of Europe would study.

A KING RETURNS

After Cromwell's death, England was tired of experiments. In 1660 Charles II came home to cheers. He was witty, clever, and wiser than his father. He had learned that a king who wanted to keep his head should not push Parliament too far. Parliament kept its control of taxes. See the glossary box., which meant that no one could be held in prison without being brought before a judge.

Charles's court was famous for its pleasures, but the age had a serious side. In 1662 he chartered the Royal Society, a group of learned men who met to test ideas by experiment, and its early members included the chemist Robert Boyle. Charles also kept a secret from his people. In 1670 he signed a treaty with Louis XIV at Dover in which he promised, when the time was right, to declare himself a Catholic. In return Louis paid him large sums. French money meant that Charles could do without Parliament's money, which was the point. In 1678 a liar named Titus Oates invented a Catholic plot to murder the king, and thousands believed him. A country that feared Catholic plots was not going to be calm about a Catholic heir.

The trouble was the succession. Charles had no legitimate children, so his brother James, an open Catholic, was next in line. Parliament split over whether to bar him. Those who wanted to bar him were later called Whigs. Those who defended his right to inherit were called Tories. Both names began as insults. Charles protected his brother, and he ruled his last years without calling Parliament. When he died in 1685, he had secretly become a Catholic.

A CATHOLIC KING

Many Protestants put up with James II because his two daughters, Mary and Anne, were Protestants. The crown would pass to them one day. They did not have to wait long to see his plans. When a rebellion led by Charles II's illegitimate son was crushed in 1685, the king's judge hanged hundreds. The rebel was the Duke of Monmouth, who landed in the west of England in June 1685 and was defeated within weeks. The trials that followed were so harsh that they became known as the Bloody Assizes. James kept his new army camped each summer at Hounslow Heath, near London, where it could be seen. In 1687 he issued a Declaration of Indulgence suspending the laws against Catholics and Protestant dissenters alike. Some dissenters welcomed it, but many did not trust the offer, because it rested on a power no king had been granted. Meanwhile ships kept arriving from France with Huguenots, Protestants whom Louis XIV had driven out of his kingdom in 1685. Tens of thousands settled in England. Their stories gave English Protestants a picture of what a Catholic king with a strong army could do.

In 1688 he ordered the clergy to read a declaration in their churches. Seven bishops petitioned him to excuse them. James put them on trial, and a jury found them not guilty. London rang with celebration. Many Englishmen saw a king who believed in the divine right of kingsdih-VINE RITE uv KINGZThe belief that a king's authority comes directly from God, so that he answers to God alone. The bishops had refused because Parliament had never approved the king's power to suspend laws, and the Archbishop of Canterbury, William Sancroft, led them. On the day the jury cleared them, James was visiting the army camp at Hounslow. He heard a great roar go up from his soldiers and asked what it meant. He was told that the men were cheering the verdict. His own soldiers were glad to see him lose.

THE BABY AND THE INVITATION

In June 1688 James's wife gave birth to a son. Now a Catholic boy stood ahead of his Protestant sisters. A rumor spread that the baby had been smuggled into the room in a warming pan. It was untrue, but it shows how frightened people were.

Not everyone saw the baby as a Protestant threat. Some saw in him the rightful heir, and later his followers would be called Jacobites, from Jacobus, the Latin form of James. They would rise in rebellion in Scotland in 1715 and again in 1745.

Within weeks, seven leading Englishmen sent a secret letter to William of Orange. William was the Dutch stadtholder and the great-grandson of William the Silent. He was married to Mary, James's Protestant daughter. He had his own reasons to say yes. Louis XIV threatened the Dutch Republic, and William wanted England as an ally rather than as a friend of France.

The seven who signed the letter included nobles and a bishop. They told William that nineteen of every twenty Englishmen wanted a change and would welcome him if he came with an army. The figure was a guess, and a hopeful one. William needed little urging. The Dutch government gave him ships and money, because the Dutch feared an English king allied with Louis. William's printed declaration said that he came only to see that a free Parliament was called, and his banners carried a motto promising to maintain the liberties of England and the Protestant religion.

THE PROTESTANT WIND

In November 1688 William sailed with about 450 ships and 20,000 men, a fleet larger than the Spanish Armada. For days an east wind kept James's navy bottled up in port, and it carried William's fleet down the Channel. When the wind turned west, William landed at Torbay on November 5. People called it the Protestant wind.

William had already tried to sail in October, and a storm drove his ships back to port. When he sailed again, the wind was in his favor. The English fleet lay at anchor at the mouth of the Thames, and the east wind that held it there carried the Dutch down the Channel, so close to the English coast that people on shore watched the ships pass. William landed on November 5, a date English Protestants already celebrated for the failure of the Gunpowder Plot.

William marched slowly toward London. James had the larger army, but his officers began to desert him. One was John Churchill, the future Duke of Marlborough. Another was James's own daughter Anne. James lost heart and fled to France in December. Had the wind blown the other way, or had James stood and fought, England might have had another civil war. As it was, there was almost no fighting in England.

William moved slowly and let events work for him. At Exeter he waited for English nobles to come over, and few did at first, until Churchill left James's camp on November 24 and Princess Anne left London two days later. James had repeated nosebleeds and was sick with worry, and he turned back from Salisbury without a battle. In December he tried to flee. Reports say he dropped the Great Seal, the instrument that made his orders law, into the Thames. Fishermen caught him and brought him back. A second time William's guards let him go, and this time he reached France.

A NEW SETTLEMENT

In early 1689 Parliament declared that James had abandoned the throne. It offered the crown to William and Mary together, on one condition. They had to accept a list of limits on royal power, which became the Bill of Rights. The king could not suspend laws or make them without Parliament. He could not tax without its consent, and he could not keep a standing army in peacetime unless Parliament agreed. Elections had to be free, and members of Parliament could speak freely in debate. Subjects had the right to petition the king, and cruel punishments were forbidden. Protestants could keep arms for their defense, and no Catholic could wear the crown.

William made it plain that he would not be his wife's gentleman usher, and Mary would not rule without him, so they agreed to reign jointly, with the power in his hands. The Bill of Rights said what a king could not do, but a promise on paper was weak, as Charles I had shown with the Petition of Right. Parliament added a stronger tool. The Mutiny Act of 1689 allowed officers to punish soldiers for desertion and disobedience, and it expired after a year. Without a new act each year, the law could not hold an army together. A king who wanted an army had to call Parliament every year to ask for it.

England had become a constitutional monarchykon-stih-TOO-shun-ul MON-ar-keeA government in which a king or queen reigns but must obey the laws and the limits of a constitution. See the glossary box.. The king was still powerful, but he now ruled under rules he did not control. In the same year the Toleration Act allowed Protestants who disagreed with the Church of England to hold their own services. Catholics were not included.

A KING AND QUEEN

William and Mary were an unusual pair. Mary was warm and popular and had been reluctant to take her father's throne. William was cold in public, asthmatic, and more interested in his war against Louis than in English politics. When Mary died of smallpox in 1694, he ruled alone. He died in 1702 after his horse stumbled in a molehill. Jacobites drank a toast to "the little gentleman in black velvet," the mole.

William spent most summers across the Channel, commanding armies in the long war against Louis XIV. He lost more battles than he won, but he held his alliance together. English taxes paid for the campaigns, and not everyone was glad to see English money spent on a Dutch king's war. His closest friends were Dutchmen, such as Hans Willem Bentinck, whom he made Earl of Portland, and the English grumbled about foreigners at court.

The throne passed to Mary's sister Anne. Anne had been pregnant at least seventeen times, and the one boy who survived infancy died in 1700 at the age of eleven. When none of her children lived, Parliament passed the Act of Settlement in 1701, which gave the crown to a Protestant German cousin, George of Hanover. In 1714 he became King George I. George spoke little English, and more than fifty relatives with a better claim by blood were passed over because they were Catholic. Parliament had chosen the king, which no one could have imagined in 1600.

DUTCH GIFTS

William brought more than a fleet. He brought Dutch ways of finance. In 1694 England chartered the Bank of England, and Parliament promised to repay the king's loans from taxes. Lenders trusted a Parliament more than a king, so England could borrow enormous sums. That power would help it fight Louis XIV for the next twenty years. The merchants of Amsterdam had taught Europe how credit worked, and England learned the lesson.

Think about what a lender needed to know. A king might simply refuse to pay, as the kings of Spain had done more than once. A Parliament could pledge particular taxes to pay the interest, and it was the same Parliament that voted and collected them. The loans became a national debt, and the Bank of England, whose first loan to the government was 1.2 million pounds, managed the payments. Dutch investors soon bought English government debt, trusting it for the same reason they had trusted their own cities. In the wars that followed, England could borrow at lower interest than France, whose absolute king found it harder to persuade lenders.

    WAS IT GLORIOUS?

    Its defenders called it glorious because it protected Protestantism, limited the crown, and was nearly bloodless. But it was not welcomed everywhere. James fought on in Ireland until William beat him at the Boyne in 1690. In Scotland, the Jacobite cause kept its support in the Highlands, and a massacre at Glencoe in 1692 deepened the bitterness. In England, the vote remained limited to a small share of men.

    In Ireland, William came in person, and his army won at the river Boyne in July 1690. Protestants in Northern Ireland still celebrate the victory. For most Irish Catholics it meant that laws passed in the years that followed took away their land, their vote, and their right to hold office. In Scotland, the clans that had backed James were ordered to swear loyalty to William by the start of 1692. The chief of the MacDonalds of Glencoe was a few days late. Soldiers who had been staying with the clan as guests for nearly two weeks turned on their hosts one February morning and killed about forty people. An inquiry later blamed the king's Scottish ministers, but the killing shocked people across Britain. And in England itself, only a minority of men, and no women, could vote.

    Even so, the settlement worked. King, Lords, and Commons now balanced one another, much as Polybius had praised in Rome's mixed constitution. Visitors from other lands noticed, and so did Englishmen who had lived abroad. One was the philosopher John Locke, who came home from exile in the fleet that carried Princess Mary to England.
