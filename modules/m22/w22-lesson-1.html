🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

In 1649 the English did something no other European nation had done. They put their king on trial and cut off his head. Today you will see how a quarrel over money, religion, and power grew into a civil war, and why the republic that followed did not last.

A KING AND HIS PARLIAMENT

By 1625 England had a long habit of mixed government. The king ruled, but he needed Parliament to grant him taxes. Parliament had two houses. The House of Lords held great nobles and bishops. The House of Commons held country gentlemen and townsmen chosen by men who owned property. Only the king could call Parliament, and it met only when he wished.

In 1625 Charles I became king. He was dignified, devout, and honest in his private life. He was also stubborn, secretive, and sure that he answered to God alone. He married a French Catholic princess, Henrietta Maria, which made many of his Protestant subjects uneasy.

THE KING RUNS SHORT OF MONEY

Charles needed money for wars with Spain and France, and Parliament was slow to give it. Its members feared waste and wanted a voice in policy. Charles forced his subjects to lend him money and jailed gentlemen who refused. In 1628 Parliament made him accept the Petition of Right. It said that the king could not tax without Parliament's consent or jail people without cause. Charles agreed, and then he ignored it.

In 1629 he sent Parliament home and ruled without it for eleven years. He raised money from old fees and from a tax called ship money, which had once been charged only to port towns and which he now charged to the whole kingdom. A country gentleman named John Hampden refused to pay. In 1637 the judges ruled for the king by only a narrow vote, which showed how many people agreed with Hampden.

RELIGION AND REBELLION

Religion made the quarrel dangerous. Charles's Archbishop of Canterbury, William Laud, wanted more ceremony and ritual in church services. Many PuritansPYOOR-ih-tunAn English Protestant who wanted to purify the Church of England of ceremonies he or she thought were left over from Catholicism. See the glossary box. saw this as Catholicism creeping back in. In 1637 Charles and Laud tried to force an English prayer book on Scotland, whose church had no bishops. Riots broke out in Edinburgh. The Scots signed a National Covenant and raised an army.

Not all Puritans stayed to fight over the church. During the 1630s thousands of them sailed across the Atlantic and founded colonies in New England, including Massachusetts Bay. They wanted to build communities in which they could worship in the way that they thought was right. Their departure drained England of some of its most determined reformers, but it planted a Puritan culture in America that would shape its history.

Charles had no money to fight them, so in 1640 he called a Parliament. It would not help him until he listened to its complaints, and he dismissed it after three weeks. When the Scots invaded England, he had to call another. This one met in November 1640 and became known as the Long Parliament, because it sat for years.

THE ROAD TO WAR

The Long Parliament ended ship money and executed the king's chief minister. In October 1641 a rebellion broke out in Ireland, and the English argued over who should command an army to put it down. Neither side trusted the other with a sword.

Then in January 1642 Charles made a serious mistake. He marched into the House of Commons with armed men to arrest five of its leaders. The five had been warned and were gone. "I see all the birds have flown," he said. No king had ever entered the Commons by force. Both sides now prepared for war, and in August Charles raised his royal banner at Nottingham.

CAVALIERS AND ROUNDHEADS

The king's supporters were called Cavaliers, and Parliament's were called Roundheads, after the short haircuts of London apprentices. The war was not simply rich against poor. Families divided and brothers fought on opposite sides. The king was strong in the north and west. Parliament held London, the great ports, and the navy.

The early battles settled little. Then a gentleman farmer named Oliver Cromwell rose to prominence. He was a devout Puritan with no military training, but he built a cavalry force of disciplined, religious men who won at Marston Moor in 1644. In 1645 Parliament created the New Model Army, in which officers were promoted for ability rather than birth. It crushed the king at Naseby that June, and Charles gave himself up the next year.

A WAR OF IDEAS

The war also stirred up ideas that frightened the men who had started it. In 1647 army officers and common soldiers met in the church at Putney to debate what kind of government England should have. A group called the Levellers wanted most men to have the vote. Colonel Thomas Rainsborough said that "the poorest he that is in England hath a life to live, as the greatest he." Cromwell's son-in-law, Henry Ireton, answered that those with no property had no lasting stake in the kingdom. If they could vote, they might vote to take property away from others.

Neither side won the argument that day. But it showed that once the old order cracked, people began to ask who should rule and why. These questions would be debated for centuries.

THE TRIAL OF A KING

Even in defeat, Charles would not settle. He plotted in secret with the Scots, who invaded England again in 1648. The army was furious. In December soldiers kept out the members of Parliament who still wanted a deal with the king. The members left in the Commons put Charles on trial. He refused to recognize the court. He was found guilty of treason and beheaded outside his palace at Whitehall on January 30, 1649. England had become a republic.

THE REPUBLIC THAT FAILED

The republic was not a golden age of liberty. Cromwell's troops killed most of the garrison and many civilians at Drogheda in Ireland. In 1653 he dissolved Parliament by force and took the title Lord Protector, which began what is called the Protectoratepruh-TEK-tuh-ritThe government of England under Oliver Cromwell from 1653 to 1659, in which he ruled as Lord Protector. See the glossary box.. He refused the crown, but he ruled with the army behind him. When he died in 1658, no one could hold the system together. In 1660 the English invited Charles's son home as Charles II.

Machiavelli had warned that republics born in civil war often end under the sword, and England seems to prove him right. Nothing was settled in 1660 either. How could a crown be limited without destroying it? The question had to be answered again, and the next lesson shows how.
