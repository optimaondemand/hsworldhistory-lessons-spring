🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

In 1649 the English did something no other European nation had done. They put their king on trial and cut off his head. Today you will see how a quarrel over money, religion, and power grew into a civil war, and why the republic that followed did not last.

A KING AND HIS PARLIAMENT

By 1625 England had a long habit of mixed government. The king ruled, but he needed Parliament to grant him taxes. Parliament had two houses. The House of Lords held great nobles and bishops. The House of Commons held country gentlemen and townsmen chosen by men who owned property. Only the king could call Parliament, and it met only when he wished.

In 1625 Charles I became king. He was dignified, devout, and honest in his private life. He was also stubborn, secretive, and sure that he answered to God alone. He married a French Catholic princess, Henrietta Maria, which made many of his Protestant subjects uneasy.

Charles's father, James I, had written books defending the divine right of kings, and he had also learned to work with Parliament when he had to. Charles had less patience. Parliament's strongest tool was the purse. Custom allowed the king to collect duties on trade, called tonnage and poundage, and Parliament usually granted them to each new king for life. In 1625 it granted them for one year only, so that Charles would have to ask again. He collected them anyway. The quarrel was not about whether the king could rule. It was about whether he could rule without asking.

THE KING RUNS SHORT OF MONEY

Charles needed money for wars with Spain and France, and Parliament was slow to give it. Its members feared waste and wanted a voice in policy. Charles forced his subjects to lend him money and jailed gentlemen who refused. In 1628 Parliament made him accept the Petition of Right. It said that the king could not tax without Parliament's consent or jail people without cause. Charles agreed, and then he ignored it.

The wars were run by the king's favorite, the Duke of Buckingham, and two of his expeditions, one against Spain in 1625 and one to help French Protestants in 1627, failed badly. Parliament wanted Buckingham punished, and Charles sent its members home rather than lose him. A gentleman who refused a forced loan went to prison. The Petition of Right was written to answer exactly that. In August 1628 a discontented officer stabbed Buckingham to death, and much of the country was glad. Charles was shaken by the loss of his closest friend.

In 1629 he sent Parliament home and ruled without it for eleven years. He raised money from old fees and from a tax called ship money, which had once been charged only to port towns and which he now charged to the whole kingdom. A country gentleman named John Hampden refused to pay. In 1637 the judges ruled for the king by only a narrow vote, which showed how many people agreed with Hampden.

Here is how ship money worked. Each county was told how much to pay for the navy, and the sheriff collected it. The king needed no Parliament to approve the order, and the sums were large and regular, which was the point. Charles also revived old fees. Landowners who had never been made knights could be fined for it, and the boundaries of the royal forests, unchanged since the Middle Ages, were redrawn so that landowners could be fined for trespass. Each demand could be defended in law. Together they made the gentry feel that no property was safe. When Hampden's case came before the judges, seven voted for the king and five against.

RELIGION AND REBELLION

Religion made the quarrel dangerous. Charles's Archbishop of Canterbury, William Laud, wanted more ceremony and ritual in church services. Many PuritansPYOOR-ih-tunAn English Protestant who wanted to purify the Church of England of ceremonies he or she thought were left over from Catholicism. See the glossary box. saw this as Catholicism creeping back in. In 1637 Charles and Laud tried to force an English prayer book on Scotland, whose church had no bishops. Riots broke out in Edinburgh. The Scots signed a National Covenant and raised an army.

Laud's changes were easy to see. He had the communion table moved to the east end of the church and fenced off with rails, where the Puritans wanted a plain table in the middle of the room. Writers who attacked his policies were punished harshly. The lawyer William Prynne, who had already lost part of his ears for an earlier pamphlet, was branded and lost what remained of them in 1637. When the new prayer book was read in St. Giles' Cathedral in Edinburgh in July 1637, a riot broke out. Tradition says a market woman named Jenny Geddes threw her stool at the preacher. In February 1638 Scots of every rank signed the Covenant, a promise to defend their church. Many of the officers who led their army had served in the great wars in Germany.

Not all Puritans stayed to fight over the church. During the 1630s thousands of them sailed across the Atlantic and founded colonies in New England, including Massachusetts Bay. They wanted to build communities in which they could worship in the way that they thought was right. Their departure drained England of some of its most determined reformers, but it planted a Puritan culture in America that would shape its history.

Charles had no money to fight them, so in 1640 he called a Parliament. It would not help him until he listened to its complaints, and he dismissed it after three weeks. When the Scots invaded England, he had to call another. This one met in November 1640 and became known as the Long Parliament, because it sat for years.

The Scots army took Newcastle and would not leave until it was paid, and only Parliament could vote the money. The Long Parliament used the moment. Within a year it ended ship money and the other fees, passed a law that Parliament must meet at least every three years, and put the king's chief minister, Thomas Wentworth, Earl of Strafford, on trial. When the trial did not reach a verdict, Parliament passed a bill to execute him instead. Charles had promised to protect him. In May 1641 he signed the bill.

THE ROAD TO WAR

The Long Parliament ended ship money and executed the king's chief minister. In October 1641 a rebellion broke out in Ireland, and the English argued over who should command an army to put it down. Neither side trusted the other with a sword.

The Irish rising began in Ulster, where native Irish rose against Protestant settlers who had taken their land. Reports in England, many of them exaggerated, told of thousands of Protestants murdered. Parliament refused to let the king command an army that might be turned against it, and the king refused to give up the command. In November a long list of complaints against the king, the Grand Remonstrance, passed the Commons by only eleven votes, and the narrow margin showed how many members were afraid of going further.

Then in January 1642 Charles made a serious mistake. He marched into the House of Commons with armed men to arrest five of its leaders. The five had been warned and were gone. "I see all the birds have flown," he said. No king had ever entered the Commons by force. Both sides now prepared for war, and in August Charles raised his royal banner at Nottingham.

Charles arrived on January 4 with several hundred armed men, who waited in the lobby while he walked in. He asked the Speaker, William Lenthall, where the five were, and Lenthall answered that he could see and speak only as the House directed him. The five, among them John Pym and John Hampden, had slipped away by river. A week later Charles left London. He would not come back until he was brought there as a prisoner.

CAVALIERS AND ROUNDHEADS

The king's supporters were called Cavaliers, and Parliament's were called Roundheads, after the short haircuts of London apprentices. The war was not simply rich against poor. Families divided and brothers fought on opposite sides. The king was strong in the north and west. Parliament held London, the great ports, and the navy.

The first major battle, at Edgehill in October 1642, ended in a draw. The king's best cavalry commander was his nephew, Prince Rupert, a dashing young officer whose charges swept away whatever they struck. Parliament had other advantages. London's merchants lent it money, and in 1643 it created a new tax, the excise, a duty on goods such as beer and tobacco that raised money steadily. It also made an alliance with the Scots, who sent an army into England in return for a promise to reform the English church. A war lasts as long as one side can pay its soldiers, and Parliament could pay more steadily than the king.

The early battles settled little. Then a gentleman farmer named Oliver Cromwell rose to prominence. He was a devout Puritan with no military training, but he built a cavalry force of disciplined, religious men who won at Marston Moor in 1644. In 1645 Parliament created the New Model Army, in which officers were promoted for ability rather than birth. It crushed the king at Naseby that June, and Charles gave himself up the next year.

Cromwell had seen the problem plainly. In a letter he wrote that he would rather have a plain captain who knew what he was fighting for than a gentleman who was nothing else. Parliament then passed a law requiring its members to give up their army commands, which cleared out some mediocre aristocratic officers, and Sir Thomas Fairfax took command of the New Model Army. Its soldiers were paid more regularly than most armies of the time, drilled hard, and many of them read the Bible daily. At Naseby, on June 14, 1645, they destroyed the king's main army and captured his private letters, which showed that he was seeking help from foreign troops. In May 1646 he surrendered to the Scots.

A WAR OF IDEAS

The war also stirred up ideas that frightened the men who had started it. In 1647 army officers and common soldiers met in the church at Putney to debate what kind of government England should have. A group called the Levellers wanted most men to have the vote. Colonel Thomas Rainsborough said that "the poorest he that is in England hath a life to live, as the greatest he." Cromwell's son-in-law, Henry Ireton, answered that those with no property had no lasting stake in the kingdom. If they could vote, they might vote to take property away from others.

The Levellers were led by John Lilburne, a London pamphleteer who had been whipped and jailed under Charles for his writings. They drew up a draft constitution, the Agreement of the People, which would have widened the vote and placed power in the people's hands. The argument at Putney was left unsettled. In November 1647 Charles escaped from custody, and soon afterward he made a secret agreement with the Scots, who promised to invade England on his behalf in return for church reform. Rainsborough was killed by royalists in 1648. In 1649 Cromwell crushed a Leveller mutiny in the army, and the movement was broken.

Neither side won the argument that day. But it showed that once the old order cracked, people began to ask who should rule and why. These questions would be debated for centuries.

THE TRIAL OF A KING

Even in defeat, Charles would not settle. He plotted in secret with the Scots, who invaded England again in 1648. The army was furious. In December soldiers kept out the members of Parliament who still wanted a deal with the king. The members left in the Commons put Charles on trial. He refused to recognize the court. He was found guilty of treason and beheaded outside his palace at Whitehall on January 30, 1649. England had become a republic.

The soldier who kept the members out was Colonel Thomas Pride, whose men stood at the door and turned the king's friends away. The remaining Commons, which came to be called the Rump, set up a special court with John Bradshaw as its president. Charles would not enter a plea. He said that no court had authority over a king and that he stood for the liberty of his people. On the morning of the execution he put on two shirts so that he would not shiver in the cold and seem afraid. He walked through his own Banqueting House and stepped out onto a scaffold in front of it, and witnesses said that the crowd groaned when the blow fell. Soon afterward a book said to be the king's own meditations, the Eikon Basilike, appeared and sold widely. It made him a martyr in the eyes of many.

THE REPUBLIC THAT FAILED

After the king's death the Commons abolished the monarchy and the House of Lords and declared England a commonwealth. Cromwell was sent to put down resistance in Ireland, and then to deal with the Scots. He defeated them at Dunbar in 1650. The next year the Scots crowned Charles's son as Charles II and marched into England, where Cromwell destroyed their army at Worcester. The young Charles escaped. He hid for a day in an oak tree at Boscobel while soldiers searched below, made his way to the south coast, and sailed for France. Had he been caught, the monarchy would have had no heir to call home.

The republic was not a golden age of liberty. Cromwell's troops killed most of the garrison and many civilians at Drogheda in Ireland. In 1653 he dissolved Parliament by force and took the title Lord Protector, which began what is called the Protectoratepruh-TEK-tuh-ritThe government of England under Oliver Cromwell from 1653 to 1659, in which he ruled as Lord Protector. See the glossary box.. He refused the crown, but he ruled with the army behind him. When he died in 1658, no one could hold the system together. In 1660 the English invited Charles's son home as Charles II.

Cromwell dissolved the Rump by marching in with soldiers and ordering the mace, the symbol of Parliament's authority, taken away. A written constitution, the Instrument of Government, made him Lord Protector. It limited his powers on paper, but the army's weight stood behind him. In 1655 he divided England into districts under army officers called major-generals, and many people resented soldiers who interfered with their lives. The Puritan government banned Christmas feasts, the theaters had been closed since the war began, and many people were tired of it all. His son Richard had no hold over the army and resigned within months. In 1660 General George Monck marched from Scotland to London, a new Parliament was called, and Charles II came home. The men who had signed the old king's death warrant were hunted down. Cromwell's own body was dug up and hanged at Tyburn.

Machiavelli had warned that republics born in civil war often end under the sword, and England seems to prove him right. Nothing was settled in 1660 either. How could a crown be limited without destroying it? The question had to be answered again, and the next lesson shows how.
