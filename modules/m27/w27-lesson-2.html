🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

Napoleon beat every army sent against him, yet he lost his empire. Today you will see how a war to strangle British trade dragged him into Spain and Russia, how he met his end at Waterloo, and how his wars helped bring about the independence of Haiti and Latin America.

HAITI AND LOUISIANA

Before he turned east, Napoleon tried to rebuild a French empire in the Americas. The sugar colony of Saint-Domingue, now Haiti, was the richest in the world, with half a million enslaved people, many times more than their owners. They had risen in 1791. Under Toussaint Louverture, a formerly enslaved man who became a brilliant general, they won their freedom, and France's revolutionary government had abolished slavery in 1794. Toussaint went on to defeat British and Spanish forces, and by 1801 he governed the whole island. His constitution named him governor for life, though he still called the colony French and professed loyalty to France.

To Napoleon the colony's sugar and coffee, which had made French port cities rich, were a prize he wanted back. He had also gotten Louisiana from Spain in a secret deal, and he meant to use it to supply the sugar islands. In 1802 he sent an army under his brother-in-law, General Leclerc, to take control of the island again, and he restored slavery in other French colonies. Toussaint was lured to a meeting, seized, and shipped to France, where he died in a cold mountain prison. Word that slavery was being restored elsewhere, and rumors that it would return to Saint-Domingue, sent the people back to arms.

Yellow fever, a disease the Europeans had no defense against, killed most of the French soldiers. No one in 1802 knew that mosquitoes carried it, and doctors blamed bad air. Leclerc himself died of it that autumn. The fighting went on under Toussaint's former officers, and in 1804 Jean-Jacques Dessalines declared the colony independent under the name Haiti. It was the second independent nation in the Americas and the first led by formerly enslaved people. Napoleon, now with little hope in the Americas and in need of money for a new war with Britain, sold Louisiana to the United States in 1803 for 15 million dollars. The sale roughly doubled the size of the United States. A disease had changed the map.

⚡ DECIDE FIRST
It is 1802. Saint-Domingue, the richest colony in the world, is ruled by the formerly enslaved people who won their freedom there. You advise Napoleon.
Decide first, and jot your answer in your notebook: would you send an army to take control again, and what could go wrong? Then open to compare.
Napoleon chose to retake the island by force, and he restored slavery in other French colonies. The expedition failed, for reasons he could not have controlled, and in 1804 Haiti declared its independence. Another path was to leave Saint-Domingue alone and keep France's attention and money in Europe. The choice he made cost him the island. A plan can fail in ways that no general can foresee, and a sound plan is not the same as a lucky one.
THE CONTINENTAL SYSTEM

After Trafalgar, Napoleon could not beat Britain at sea. So in 1806 he tried to beat it by trade. His Continental Systemkon-tih-NEN-tul SIS-tumNapoleon's plan to defeat Britain by closing the ports of Europe to British trade. See the glossary box., announced from Berlin after his victory at Jena, closed the ports of Europe to British goods, in the hope that Britain's merchants would go bankrupt. Britain answered by blockading French ports and forcing neutral ships to stop at British harbors first. But smuggling thrived, and the ban also hurt the countries Napoleon ruled, because Europeans still wanted the sugar, coffee, and cotton that now cost more. A ban is only as good as its coastline. To make it work, he had to control the whole coast of Europe. That meant invading country after country.

Portugal, an old ally of Britain, would not close its ports. French troops invaded in 1807, marching across Spain with its permission. The Portuguese royal family, with the help of the British navy, escaped across the Atlantic to Brazil just before the French reached Lisbon. The French troops did not go home from Spain, either. They stayed in its fortresses and towns, and Spain, nominally France's ally, was soon an occupied country.

THE SPANISH ULCER

In 1808 Napoleon tricked the Spanish royal family into meeting him in the French city of Bayonne. The king, Charles IV, and his son Ferdinand were quarreling bitterly, and each came hoping Napoleon would take his side. There Napoleon forced the king to give up his throne and put his own brother Joseph on it. On May 2 the people of Madrid rose up. French cavalry crushed the rising in the streets, and firing squads shot prisoners the next night. The painter Francisco de Goya later made both scenes famous.

Across Spain, ordinary people took to the hills and began a guerrillaguh-RIL-uhA Spanish word meaning "little war." It describes fighting by small bands of irregular troops who raid and ambush a larger army. See the glossary box. war, striking supply lines and small units. Priests and village leaders helped organize them, and local councils ruled in the name of the captive Ferdinand. That summer a French army surrendered at Bailén, a shock to a Europe that had thought the French could not be beaten. A British army under Arthur Wellesley, later the Duke of Wellington, landed in Portugal to help them. Napoleon himself came with a great army that winter and retook Madrid, but he left within weeks when Austria began arming again. The war went on without him.

Hundreds of thousands of French soldiers were tied down in Spain for years. Napoleon later called it "the Spanish ulcer." The French army was strong in open battle, but its best tool, the swift campaign against a single enemy army, was useless against a whole population. A column that held a town did not hold the road beyond it. Couriers needed escorts, foragers disappeared, and every garrison had to be fed from a countryside that hated it. The British, who could be supplied by sea through Portuguese ports, always had somewhere to fall back to.

In 1810 Napoleon divorced Joséphine, who had no children, and married Marie Louise, an Austrian princess and the daughter of the Austrian emperor. In 1811 she gave him a son.

RUSSIA, 1812

By 1812 Tsar Alexander was trading with Britain again. Napoleon decided to punish him. In June he crossed the Niemen River with about 600,000 men, drawn by conscriptionkun-SKRIP-shunA required call-up of citizens for military service, also called the draft. See the glossary box. from France and its allies and subjects. Only about half were French. The rest were Poles, Germans, Italians, and others who spoke a dozen languages. The Russian generals kept refusing the great battle Napoleon wanted, and they fell back and burned what they left, so the army ran short of food. Horses died on green fodder, fever and dysentery spread, and thousands dropped out of the ranks before the first great battle was fought.

At Borodino on September 7 the two sides fought a bloody battle that settled little. Napoleon entered Moscow a week later and found it nearly empty, since most of its people and its supplies had gone. Fires broke out across the city, and much of it burned. He waited for the tsar to ask for peace, but no message came. Napoleon waited more than a month while the days grew shorter.

In October he began the retreat. Cold, hunger, and Cossack raiders destroyed the army. At the Berezina River in late November, with Russian armies closing in, his engineers worked in icy water to build bridges under fire, and part of the army got across. Only a small fraction of the men who crossed the Niemen ever came home. Napoleon left the survivors in early December and raced to Paris to raise a new army. Had the tsar sued for peace, or had Napoleon turned back sooner, the story might have been different.

    LEIPZIG AND WATERLOO

    Now the other powers saw their chance. Prussia changed sides, and Austria followed in the summer of 1813. Napoleon raised a new army of young conscripts and still won battles that spring, which shows how great his skill remained. But in October 1813, at Leipzig, the "Battle of the Nations," Russia, Prussia, Austria, and Sweden defeated Napoleon. That autumn the allies offered peace that would have left him on the throne of a France that kept the Rhine and the Alps as its frontiers. Napoleon was slow to answer, and by the time he agreed, they had hardened their terms. In March 1814 their armies entered Paris. He had fought well against larger armies that winter, but his marshals told him the cause was lost, and he was forced to abdicateAB-dih-kaytTo give up the throne or another high office. See the glossary box.. He was sent to the small island of Elba, of which he was made the ruler. The Bourbons returned in the person of Louis XVIII, a brother of the executed king.

    In February 1815, while the powers were arguing at Vienna, Napoleon slipped away from Elba and landed in France. Near Grenoble a battalion barred the road with muskets leveled. Napoleon walked forward, opened his coat, and is reported to have said, "Here I am. Kill your emperor, if you wish." Soldiers sent to stop him joined him instead, and so did Marshal Ney, who had promised Louis to bring him back in an iron cage. For a hundred days he ruled again, and he gained approval of a new constitution in another plebiscitePLEB-ih-sytA vote in which all citizens say yes or no to a single proposal. See the glossary box.. The powers declared him an outlaw and raised their armies.

    On June 18, 1815, near the village of Waterloo in Belgium, he met Wellington's army. Two days earlier Napoleon had beaten the Prussians under Blücher and sent a marshal, Grouchy, with part of the army to chase them. Grouchy never caught the main Prussian army, and he was not on the field. Heavy rain had soaked the ground overnight, and Napoleon waited until late morning for it to firm enough for his guns. The battle was close. Fighting raged around two farms, Hougoumont and La Haye Sainte, that Wellington had turned into strongpoints in front of his line. French cavalry charged again and again against British squares that would not break, and Wellington's line held through the afternoon. Wellington later called it the nearest thing he ever saw. In the evening Napoleon sent in the Old Guard, his best troops, as a last attempt to break the line. They were met by volleys and fell back, and the cry went through the army that the Guard was retreating. At the same time the Prussian army of Blücher arrived on Napoleon's flank, and the French lines broke.

    Napoleon abdicated a second time and was exiled to Saint Helena, a remote island in the South Atlantic. British ships guarded the island, so there could be no second escape. He died there in 1821, after dictating his own account of his life, which helped to build a legend that outlived him.

    Wellington later called Waterloo the nearest thing he ever saw, and the Prussian army arrived that evening. How much did chance have to do with the result?
    THE COLONIES BREAK FREE

    Napoleon's seizure of Spain shook an empire that had stood for three hundred years. With the king a prisoner, Spanish Americans asked who was to rule them. In 1810 councils formed in cities such as Caracas and Buenos Aires and governed in the king's name. For generations the Crown had given the best offices in the colonies to men born in Spain. The creolesKREE-ohlIn Spanish America, a person of European descent who was born in the colonies rather than in Spain. See the glossary box. resented the Spanish-born officials who held those offices and now they wanted a say. Soon some wanted more than a say, and war broke out.

    Simón Bolívar, a wealthy Venezuelan who had read Locke and Rousseau and studied the Romans, became the leader in the north. His first republics fell, and he was twice driven into exile. In 1816 the president of Haiti gave him ships, weapons, and money on one condition: that he free the enslaved people in the lands he won. Haiti had shown that it could be done, and now it helped others to do it. Bolívar crossed the flooded plains and then the Andes in 1819 and won at Boyacá. In the south, José de San Martín led an army over the Andes from Argentina and freed Chile, then sailed to Peru. Spanish rule ended at the Battle of Ayacucho in 1824.

    Mexico gained independence in 1821. A priest, Miguel Hidalgo, had called his poor parishioners to rise in 1810, but that rising was crushed. Independence came later, and partly from conservative creoles who feared the liberal changes taking place in Spain. In Brazil the Portuguese prince Pedro, who had stayed behind when his father returned to Lisbon, declared independence in 1822. Bolívar dreamed of a united continent, but it split into many nations, and the large republic he had built fell apart in the last year of his life. He also learned how hard it is to build free government without the habits that sustain it.

    HEIR OR GRAVEDIGGER?

    Napoleon carried equality before the law, the end of feudal privilege, and his Code across Europe. In Germany and Italy, people who welcomed those reforms came to resent the foreign army that had brought them. He also silenced the press, ended elections, and fought wars that cost millions of lives. His rise was possible only because of the Revolution, but his rule undid its promise of liberty. Both parts of the story are true. Next week you will see how the winners at Vienna tried to rebuild Europe, and why they could not make the world go back.
