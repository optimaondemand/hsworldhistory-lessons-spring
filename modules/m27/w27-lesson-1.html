🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

Burke predicted that a revolution would end in the rule of a popular general. Today you will meet that general. You will see how a Corsican officer rose in ten years from obscurity to the crown of France, and why so many French people were glad to give it to him.

A CORSICAN IN FRANCE

Napoleon Bonaparte was born in 1769 on the island of Corsica, a year after France bought it from Genoa. His family belonged to the minor nobility, and at home they spoke Italian. When he won a place at a French military school at the age of nine, the other boys mocked his accent. He answered with hard work. He was especially good at mathematics, which suited the artillery, and he read widely, including Plutarch's Lives, until the ancient heroes became his models. At sixteen he held an officer's commission. In the old royal army, though, promotion went mostly to men of great family and good connections, and a young man from a poor island with no friends at court could expect to wait a very long time. When the Revolution came, the old rules that kept officers of humble birth from rising no longer held.

Corsica itself pulled at him. His family had once fought beside the island's patriot leader, Pasquale Paoli, and as a young officer Napoleon dreamed of serving Corsica more than France. He spent long leaves there and took a hand in its politics. Then the Revolution split the island. In 1793 Paoli broke with the government in Paris, and Napoleon, who had sided with the Republic, found that he and his family were now on the wrong side at home. The Bonapartes fled to the mainland with little more than they could carry. From then on his future lay in France.

His first chance came in 1793, when British ships held the port of Toulon, which French royalists had handed over to them. The army besieging the town had no plan that worked. Napoleon, a captain of artillery, saw that the harbor was ringed by hills, and that guns placed on one height above the roadstead would make it too dangerous for the fleet to stay. He pressed the plan on his superiors until they accepted it, and the British sailed away. At twenty-four he was a general.

His rise nearly ended the next summer. After Robespierre fell in July 1794, Napoleon was arrested for a short time because he had been friendly with Robespierre's younger brother. He was freed, but he spent the following year without a command, short of money, and looking for any post that would have him. Then, in October 1795, a royalist uprising threatened the Convention in Paris. The man in charge of the defense, Paul Barras, remembered Napoleon from Toulon and called him in the night before. Napoleon sent a young cavalry officer, Joachim Murat, galloping across the city to bring up cannon before the crowd could seize them. Napoleon fired cannon into the crowd and ended the rising in an afternoon. The Directory, grateful, gave him command of the army in Italy. Within months he had married Josephine de Beauharnais, a widow whose first husband had died on the guillotine and who moved in Barras's circle. Two days later he left for the front.

ITALY AND EGYPT

In 1796 he took over a ragged army whose soldiers were hungry, badly paid, and in some cases without shoes. He told them they would find glory and wealth if they followed him. The enemy armies, one from Austria and one from the kingdom of Piedmont, stood side by side but were poorly joined. He moved fast, struck at the place where they met, and defeated them one by one. His men marched light and lived on what they could take from the countryside instead of waiting for wagons, so they often arrived before the enemy thought they could. Piedmont was out of the war in weeks. Then came a series of victories at Lodi, Arcole, and Rivoli. At Lodi his men rushed a narrow bridge under cannon fire.

In 1797 he forced Austria to sign a peace at Campo Formio, which gave France the Austrian Netherlands, and he negotiated the terms himself. The Directory in Paris, which had expected only a sideshow in Italy, now found that its general was making treaties on his own and sending home gold and famous paintings and statues from Italian cities. He was already acting like a ruler. Newspapers he supported, some of them founded for his own army, made him famous in Paris.

Notice what kind of leader he was. He slept little, remembered the names of soldiers, and worked out the details of supply and marching himself. His men followed him because he won, and because he promised that a soldier could rise as far as his talent would carry him. Many of his marshals had been sergeants or sons of innkeepers. That promise tied the army to him more than it tied it to any government.

Paris, meanwhile, had a problem. A general this popular was a danger to a government this weak. When Napoleon proposed an expedition to Egypt, the Directors agreed, partly because it would put him far away. The plan was to seize a rich province and threaten Britain's road to India, striking at an enemy France could not reach across the Channel. He sailed in 1798 with an army and, unusually, with more than a hundred scholars, engineers, and artists.

He won on land at the Battle of the Pyramids, where he formed his infantry into hollow squares to meet the charge of the Egyptian cavalry. But the British admiral Horatio Nelson found the French fleet at anchor and destroyed it at the Nile, which stranded the army. Scholars who traveled with him studied Egypt's ruins, and soldiers found the Rosetta Stone, which later let scholars read hieroglyphs. Napoleon marched into Syria in 1799 but failed to take the fortress of Acre, and plague and disease thinned his ranks. News came from Europe that France was losing ground in Italy. In 1799 Napoleon left his army behind and slipped back to France, past British ships, and he landed in October to cheering crowds.

THE COUP OF BRUMAIRE

He found the Directory in trouble. It was unpopular, corrupt, and short of money, and it had lost battles in his absence. One of its members was Sieyès, the writer of the pamphlet about the Third Estate. He wanted to change the constitution and needed a general with an army. On November 9, 1799, they carried out a coup d'étatKOO day-TAHA French phrase meaning "a blow against the state." It is a sudden seizure of power by a small group, usually with the help of soldiers. See the glossary box.. The plan was to persuade the two legislative councils to move out of Paris to the palace of Saint-Cloud, away from the city's crowds, on the claim that a plot threatened them. Two of the five Directors, Sieyès and Roger Ducos, were in on it and resigned. The others were pushed aside, and Napoleon was given command of the troops in Paris.

The next day Napoleon went to the councils at Saint-Cloud to ask for their support, and nearly lost everything. He spoke first to the older council, and his speech rambled. Then he went into the hall of the Five Hundred, where armed men were not welcome. Deputies shouted "Outlaw!" at him, crowded around him, and shoved him, and he is said to have lost his composure. His brother Lucien, who was the president of the Five Hundred, ran out and told the soldiers that deputies had tried to stab the general. The troops, led by Murat, marched in with drums beating and cleared the hall. Some deputies fled through the windows. That evening a handful of those who remained, gathered by Lucien, voted to name three temporary consuls.

Think about how narrow this was. A few steadier nerves in the hall, or a few less loyal soldiers outside, and Napoleon might have been an exile or a prisoner. Instead he became First Consul. Sieyès had expected to write the constitution, but Napoleon cared little for his careful designs and cut out whatever limited the First Consul. The new constitution named him to rule for ten years, with two other consuls who could only advise him, and it gave him the power to appoint ministers, generals, and judges and to propose every law. A plebiscitePLEB-ih-sytA vote in which all citizens say yes or no to a single proposal. See the glossary box. approved it by a huge, though doubtful, margin. The count announced about three million yes votes and fewer than two thousand no, and the new government was already running before the voting ended.

ORDER AT HOME

Many French people welcomed him. They were tired of ten years of upheaval and wanted order, and Napoleon gave it to them. Austria had retaken much of Italy while he was away, so in the spring of 1800 he crossed the Alps with a new army. At Marengo, in June, the day nearly went wrong. The Austrians struck first, and by afternoon the French were falling back and Napoleon is said to have thought the battle lost. Then General Desaix, whom he had summoned, marched back toward the sound of the guns and arrived with fresh troops. Desaix was killed leading them forward, but the counterattack broke the Austrians. It was a great victory, and in 1802 he made peace with Britain at Amiens.

Now he could build. He founded the Bank of France to steady the currency, so that the government could borrow and pay its bills. He appointed a prefect to govern each department, a system that echoed the intendants of Louis XIV. These officials took orders from Paris, which is how a government in the capital could collect taxes and call up soldiers in every corner of France. He started public high schools called lycées, run by the state to train the officials and officers the country needed. And he made peace with the Church through a Concordatkon-KOR-datA formal agreement between a government and the pope about the Church's place in that country. See the glossary box. with Pope Pius VII in 1801. The Revolution had seized Church lands and forced priests to swear an oath to the state, and French Catholics had been divided. The Concordat recognized Catholicism as the faith of most French people, let the government nominate bishops, and paid the clergy from the treasury. But it kept the Church lands in the hands of those who had bought them.

His most lasting work was the Napoleonic Code of 1804, a single set of laws for the nation. In the old France the law had differed from province to province, and a family dispute could turn on where you happened to live. A commission of lawyers drew on Roman law and the older customs, and Napoleon sat in on many of its sessions himself. The Code kept the great gains of 1789: equality before the law, an end to feudal privilege, protection of property, and religious freedom. But it also gave husbands wide power over wives and children, and a married woman could not sign a contract without her husband's consent.

Careers were open to talent, and the new Legion of Honor, founded in 1802, rewarded merit rather than birth. Yet this was also a police state. Newspapers were censored, and soon only a handful of the Paris papers were left. A secret police under the minister Joseph Fouché watched for plots, and elections became formalities.

EMPEROR OF THE FRENCH

Royalist plots against his life gave his supporters an argument. In December 1800 a bomb exploded in a Paris street just after his carriage passed, on the way to the opera. If one bullet could end the regime, they said, France needed a government that would outlast one man. In 1802 another plebiscite made him consul for life. In March 1804, after another royalist plot was uncovered, French soldiers crossed the Rhine into the German state of Baden, seized a young Bourbon prince, the Duke of Enghien, and shot him after a hasty trial. The prince had taken no part in the plot. Within weeks the Senate asked Napoleon to take the title of emperor, so that the succession would be settled and no royalist could gain by killing him. In 1804 a third plebiscite approved a new title for him, Emperor of the French.

At his coronation in Notre-Dame, on December 2, 1804, Pope Pius VII was present, but Napoleon placed the crown on his own head. He wore a wreath of golden laurel leaves, like a Roman emperor, and then crowned Josephine himself. The republic of virtue was gone. It had been replaced by a regime that depended on the will and talent of one man, which is what Polybius would have expected to see. The Romans had seen their own republic end in the same way, with Caesar.

TRAFALGAR AND AUSTERLITZ

War with Britain returned in 1803. Napoleon gathered an army at Boulogne and planned to invade England but first needed control of the Channel. On October 21, 1805, Nelson attacked the combined French and Spanish fleet off Cape Trafalgar in two columns, cutting through the enemy line instead of sailing parallel to it. He destroyed the enemy fleet and died in the moment of victory, shot by a sharpshooter on a French ship. Britain was safe at sea for the rest of the war.

On land, however, Napoleon was unstoppable. Austria and Russia had joined Britain against him that year, and he swung his army from the Channel coast to the Danube in a matter of weeks. Raising armies by conscriptionkun-SKRIP-shunA required call-up of citizens for military service, also called the draft. See the glossary box. and living off the lands he conquered, he beat the Austrians and Russians at Austerlitz on December 2, 1805, perhaps his greatest victory. Austerlitz was won by a trap. He let the enemy believe he was weak, gave up the high ground at the center, and waited. The Allies came down to crush what looked like his exposed right flank. Then his troops stormed the thinned center and split their army in two. Austria made peace within weeks.

In 1806 he crushed Prussia at Jena, and within weeks he rode into Berlin. In 1807, at Tilsit, the Russian tsar Alexander I met him on a raft moored in the Niemen River, made peace, and agreed to cooperate with him.

By 1807 Napoleon was master of most of Europe. He made his brothers kings: Joseph in Naples, Louis in Holland, and Jérôme in Westphalia. The old Holy Roman Empire was dissolved in 1806. Was he the heir of the Revolution, spreading its laws and its equality? Or had he become its gravedigger? Next lesson you will see how that question was answered.
