🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

Burke predicted that a revolution would end in the rule of a popular general. Today you will meet that general. You will see how a Corsican officer rose in ten years from obscurity to the crown of France, and why so many French people were glad to give it to him.

A CORSICAN IN FRANCE

Napoleon Bonaparte was born in 1769 on the island of Corsica, a year after France bought it from Genoa. His family belonged to the minor nobility. He won a place in French military schools and became an artillery officer. He read widely, including Plutarch's Lives, and the ancient heroes became his models. When the Revolution came, the old rules that kept officers of humble birth from rising no longer held.

His first chance came in 1793, when British ships held the port of Toulon. Napoleon planned the artillery attack that drove them out, and at twenty-four he was a general. In October 1795 a royalist uprising threatened the Convention in Paris. Napoleon fired cannon into the crowd and ended the rising in an afternoon. The Directory, grateful, gave him command of the army in Italy.

ITALY AND EGYPT

In 1796 he took over a ragged army and told the soldiers they would find glory and wealth if they followed him. He moved fast, struck the divided enemy armies one by one, and won a series of victories at Lodi, Arcole, and Rivoli. In 1797 he forced Austria to sign a peace, and he negotiated the terms himself. He was already acting like a ruler. Newspapers he supported made him famous in Paris.

Notice what kind of leader he was. He slept little, remembered the names of soldiers, and worked out the details of supply and marching himself. His men followed him because he won, and because he promised that a soldier could rise as far as his talent would carry him. Many of his marshals had been sergeants or sons of innkeepers. That promise tied the army to him more than it tied it to any government.

In 1798 he led an army to Egypt, hoping to threaten Britain's road to India. He won on land at the Battle of the Pyramids, but the British admiral Horatio Nelson destroyed his fleet at the Nile, which stranded the army. Scholars who traveled with him studied Egypt's ruins, and soldiers found the Rosetta Stone, which later let scholars read hieroglyphs. In 1799 Napoleon left his army behind and slipped back to France.

THE COUP OF BRUMAIRE

He found the Directory in trouble. It was unpopular, corrupt, and short of money, and it had lost battles in his absence. One of its members was Sieyès, the writer of the pamphlet about the Third Estate. He wanted to change the constitution and needed a general with an army. On November 9, 1799, they carried out a coup d'étatKOO day-TAHA French phrase meaning "a blow against the state." It is a sudden seizure of power by a small group, usually with the help of soldiers. See the glossary box.. The next day Napoleon went to the councils at Saint-Cloud to ask for their support, and nearly lost everything. Deputies shouted "Outlaw!" at him, and he is said to have lost his composure. His brother Lucien, who was the president of one of the councils, ran out and told the soldiers that deputies had tried to stab the general. The troops cleared the hall.

Think about how narrow this was. A few steadier nerves in the hall, or a few less loyal soldiers outside, and Napoleon might have been an exile or a prisoner. Instead he became First Consul. A new constitution gave him almost all the power, and a plebiscitePLEB-ih-sytA vote in which all citizens say yes or no to a single proposal. See the glossary box. approved it by a huge, though doubtful, margin.

ORDER AT HOME

Many French people welcomed him. They were tired of ten years of upheaval and wanted order, and Napoleon gave it to them. He won a great victory at Marengo in 1800 and made peace with Britain in 1802. He founded the Bank of France to steady the currency. He appointed a prefect to govern each department, a system that echoed the intendants of Louis XIV. He started public high schools called lycées. And he made peace with the Church through a Concordatkon-KOR-datA formal agreement between a government and the pope about the Church's place in that country. See the glossary box. with Pope Pius VII in 1801. It recognized Catholicism as the faith of most French people but kept the Church lands in the hands of those who had bought them.

His most lasting work was the Napoleonic Code of 1804, a single set of laws for the nation. It kept the great gains of 1789: equality before the law, an end to feudal privilege, protection of property, and religious freedom. But it also gave husbands wide power over wives and children. Careers were open to talent, and the new Legion of Honor rewarded merit rather than birth. Yet this was also a police state. Newspapers were censored, a secret police watched for plots, and elections became formalities.

EMPEROR OF THE FRENCH

In 1802 another plebiscite made him consul for life. In 1804 a third approved a new title for him, Emperor of the French. At his coronation in Notre-Dame, on December 2, 1804, Pope Pius VII was present, but Napoleon placed the crown on his own head. The republic of virtue was gone. It had been replaced by a regime that depended on the will and talent of one man, which is what Polybius would have expected to see. The Romans had seen their own republic end in the same way, with Caesar.

TRAFALGAR AND AUSTERLITZ

War with Britain returned in 1803. Napoleon planned to invade England but first needed control of the Channel. On October 21, 1805, Nelson destroyed the combined French and Spanish fleet at Trafalgar and died in the moment of victory. Britain was safe at sea for the rest of the war. On land, however, Napoleon was unstoppable. Raising armies by conscriptionkun-SKRIP-shunA required call-up of citizens for military service, also called the draft. See the glossary box. and living off the lands he conquered, he beat the Austrians and Russians at Austerlitz on December 2, 1805, perhaps his greatest victory. In 1806 he crushed Prussia at Jena. In 1807, at Tilsit, the Russian tsar Alexander I made peace and agreed to cooperate with him.

By 1807 Napoleon was master of most of Europe. He made his brothers kings and the old Holy Roman Empire was dissolved. Was he the heir of the Revolution, spreading its laws and its equality? Or had he become its gravedigger? Next lesson you will see how that question was answered.
