🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

The phone in your pocket may have been designed in California, built from parts made on three continents, and assembled in Asia. Today you will see how such a world was made, who gained and who lost, and what Europe tried to build after its wars. Keep this week's discussion question in mind: is globalization uniting or dividing the world?

TRADE WITHOUT WALLS

The leaders who built the postwar order remembered the 1930s, when nations raised tariffsTAIR-iffA tax a government places on goods imported from other countries. See the glossary box. and trade collapsed. In 1947 twenty-three countries signed an agreement, called GATT, to lower tariffs step by step. In 1995 it became the World Trade Organization. The cost of moving goods also fell. In 1956 an American trucker named Malcom McLean sent a ship with fifty-eight truck bodies packed in its hold. His idea, the shipping container, let a crate be lifted from a ship to a train to a truck without being opened. Loading costs dropped sharply, and it became cheap to make goods far from where they were sold. Economists have long argued that trade lets each region make what it makes best, so that everyone can buy more for less. A shirt sewn in one country from cotton grown in another, and sold in a third, can cost a fraction of what it would cost if each country made everything for itself. Adam Smith had made this argument in 1776. The same logic also means that a worker whose job can be done more cheaply abroad may lose that job, a trade-off you will weigh in the discussion.

Political change added to the trend. When communism collapsed in Europe, a vast area opened to trade. In 1978 China, under Deng Xiaoping, began to allow markets, and it joined the World Trade Organization in 2001. Within a generation its economy grew from a poor farming land into the world's workshop. In 1991 India dropped many controls on its economy, and it, too, began to grow fast. These changes lifted hundreds of millions of people out of extreme poverty, which is among the greatest gains in human history.

A NEW KIND OF EUROPE

Europe's leaders drew a different lesson from the wars that had ravaged their continent. On May 9, 1950, the French foreign minister Robert Schuman, in a speech you will read, proposed that France and West Germany place their coal and steel production under a common authority open to other nations. Coal and steel were the stuff of weapons, so such a pact would make war between the two "not merely unthinkable, but materially impossible." Schuman and his adviser Jean Monnet had a plan, and Konrad Adenauer, the German chancellor, and others agreed. In 1951 six nations, France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg, formed the European Coal and Steel Community.

The six widened their cooperation in 1957 with a common market, and over the decades more nations joined. In 1992 the Maastricht Treaty created the European Union, and in 2002 most members adopted a common currency, the euro. Citizens could live and work in any member state. Yet the Union has also met resistance. Many voters feel that decisions are made by distant officials, and in 2016 Britain voted to leave. The Union is neither a nation nor a simple alliance, and Europeans still disagree about what it should become.

PEOPLE ON THE MOVE

People moved as freely as goods, and often more painfully. After the war, former colonies sent workers to Britain, France, and the Netherlands. West Germany invited guest workers from Turkey and southern Europe. Millions moved from poor countries to richer ones, seeking work and safety, and by 2020 about one person in thirty lived outside the country of their birth. Migration filled jobs and enriched cultures, but it also caused fierce arguments over borders, jobs, language, and identity. Some migrants fled war, among them millions from Syria after 2011, and the numbers strained many nations.

The movement of money, ideas, and fear also crossed borders. SCIENCE AND TECHNOLOGY

Science brought some of the sharpest changes. In the 1960s Norman Borlaug, an American scientist working in Mexico and India, helped develop wheat and rice that yielded far more grain. This Green Revolution is credited with saving a large number of people from famine. A worldwide campaign wiped out smallpox, which had killed hundreds of millions in the twentieth century, and the last natural case occurred in 1977. In 2003 scientists finished mapping the human genome, the full set of instructions in human cells. Average life expectancy around the world rose from about forty-six years in 1950 to more than seventy by the early 2020s.

Communication changed even faster. The first message passed between two computers in 1969. In 1989 a British scientist, Tim Berners-Lee, proposed the World Wide Web, and by 1991 it was running. Within a generation, billions of people were online, able to share information in seconds. In 2020 a new virus, COVID-19, spread around the world in weeks, aided by air travel. Scientists then designed effective vaccines in less than a year, which was only possible because researchers in many nations shared data.

UNITING OR DIVIDING?

The internet itself carries both effects. It lets a student in a small town read the same books as one in a great city, and it lets a hidden network plan an attack or spread a lie as quickly as a doctor can share a cure. Tools do not decide how they are used. People do.

The gains were real, and so were the costs. Factory towns in older industrial nations lost jobs when production moved abroad, and some communities have never recovered. Competition from abroad pushed wages down in some trades, and some countries, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, gained little. Others worry that local customs and languages are being drowned by a global culture. Meanwhile, new powers, led by China, challenge the position the United States has held since 1945.

    Neither a rosy view nor a gloomy one fits the record. For centuries, from the Roman roads to the Atlantic trade you studied earlier, people have tied the world together, and the pattern has often brought wealth along with conflict. History offers no law that says what comes next. Nations and their leaders will keep making choices, and those choices will depend on the character of citizens as much as on markets and machines. You have now followed the story from the fall of Rome to the present. In your discussion, weigh what you have learned and decide what you think.
