🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

Just as the Cold War was ending, a different kind of struggle was taking shape in the Middle East and Central Asia. Today you will see how a revolution in Iran, a war in Afghanistan, and a network of violent extremists led to the attacks of September 11, 2001.

IRAN AND THE SHAH

Iran, an ancient country of Persian culture, was ruled in the twentieth century by the Pahlavi family. Its oil was developed by a British company, and many Iranians resented that foreigners took the profits. In 1951 the prime minister, Mohammad Mossadegh, nationalized the oil industry. Britain and the United States, fearing that Iran might drift toward the Soviet side, helped organize a coup in 1953 that removed him and restored the young Shah, Mohammad Reza Pahlavi, to full power.

The Shah ruled as a modernizer. In the 1960s his "White Revolution" gave land to peasants, gave women the vote, and built schools and factories. But he ruled as an autocrat, banned real opposition, and relied on a secret police called SAVAK. Oil money flowed in, and the gap between the wealthy and the poor grew. Many religious Iranians were angered by his Western ways, and many bazaar merchants and students resented his power. His alliance with America made the United States a target of their anger.

Foreign Language Vocabulary

Two foreign-language terms appear in the next section. Here is how to say them and what they mean.

ayatollah
eye-uh-TOH-luh
A Persian title, from the Arabic, meaning "sign of God." It is given to the most respected senior scholars of Shia Islam.
mujahideen
moo-jah-heh-DEEN
An Arabic word meaning "those who struggle." In Afghanistan in the 1980s it named the fighters who resisted the Soviet army.
THE REVOLUTION OF 1979

Most Iranians belong to the Shia branch of Islam, which differs from the Sunni branch, to which most Muslims elsewhere belong, over who should lead the community after Muhammad. Shia clergy have long held great moral authority. The Shah's chief critic was an ayatollaheye-uh-TOH-luhA Persian title, from the Arabic, meaning "sign of God." It is given to the most respected senior scholars of Shia Islam. See the glossary box., Ruhollah Khomeini, who denounced him for corruption and for submission to the West. Khomeini was exiled in 1964 and spent years in Iraq and then France, where his sermons were smuggled home on cassette tapes.

In 1978 protests began, and when soldiers fired on crowds the protests grew. Strikes, including those of oil workers, paralyzed the country. The Shah, secretly ill with cancer, hesitated to use full force, and some of his generals lost heart. He left Iran in January 1979. Khomeini returned in triumph in February, and a referendum soon created an Islamic Republic. Khomeini taught that, in the absence of the hidden Imam, the senior scholars should rule. The result was a theocracythee-OK-ruh-seeA government in which religious leaders hold the highest political power and rule in the name of God. See the glossary box., with an elected president and parliament but a Supreme Leader above them. Many who had joined the revolution, including liberals and the left, found themselves jailed or silenced. The Shah's fall was not inevitable. A different response in 1978 might have kept him on the throne.

HOSTAGES AND WAR

In October 1979 President Jimmy Carter allowed the exiled Shah to enter the United States for medical treatment. Many Iranians feared a repeat of 1953. On November 4, students stormed the American embassy in Tehran and seized more than sixty Americans. Khomeini backed them. Fifty-two of the hostages were held for 444 days, released only on the day Ronald Reagan took office in January 1981. An American rescue attempt failed in the desert. The crisis humiliated the United States and helped to defeat Carter's bid for re-election.

In September 1980 Iraq's dictator, Saddam Hussein, invaded Iran, expecting a quick victory over a country in turmoil. Instead the war lasted eight years and killed hundreds of thousands. Iran used waves of young volunteers, and Iraq used poison gas, against Iran and against its own Kurdish citizens. Both sides were exhausted by the end in 1988, with the borders where they started. Khomeini died in 1989, and his system outlived him.

AFGHANISTAN AND AL-QAEDA

In December 1979 Soviet troops invaded Afghanistan to prop up a communist government. Many Afghans, a deeply religious and tribal people, rose against it. These fighters, the mujahideenmoo-jah-heh-DEENAn Arabic word meaning "those who struggle." In Afghanistan in the 1980s it named the fighters who resisted the Soviet army. See the glossary box., received weapons and money from the United States, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan, and many volunteers came from other Muslim lands. A wealthy young Saudi named Osama bin Laden helped to fund and organize some of them. The Soviets withdrew in 1989 after nearly ten years, a defeat that helped bring on the collapse of the Soviet Union.

When the Soviets left, the world lost interest, and Afghanistan fell into civil war. A movement of religious students, the Taliban, took the capital, Kabul, in 1996, and imposed a harsh rule, forbidding girls to attend school and barring women from most work. Bin Laden, who had turned against the United States, formed a network called al-Qaeda, meaning "the base." He had objected to American troops in Saudi Arabia, home to Islam's holiest cities, after the Gulf War of 1991, and he issued a declaration of war on Americans in 1996. The Taliban gave him a base.

Al-Qaeda practiced terrorismTER-er-iz-umThe use of violence against civilians to spread fear and force a government or a people to give in to a political or religious demand. See the glossary box.. In 1993 a bomb exploded beneath the World Trade Center in New York, killing six. In August 1998 truck bombs destroyed the American embassies in Kenya and Tanzania, killing more than two hundred people, most of them Africans. In October 2000 a small boat packed with explosives hit the destroyer USS Cole in Yemen and killed seventeen sailors. Most Muslims, including most religious scholars, rejected such violence, and most victims of the extremists were themselves Muslims.

SEPTEMBER 11, 2001

On the morning of September 11, 2001, nineteen men, most of them from Saudi Arabia, boarded four airliners in the northeastern United States. Armed with box cutters, they took over the planes. At 8:46 one flew into the north tower of the World Trade Center, and at 9:03 another struck the south tower. At 9:37 a third hit the Pentagon, near Washington. Passengers on the fourth, Flight 93, learned by telephone what had happened to the others. They fought the hijackers, and the plane crashed in a field in Pennsylvania, short of its target, which was probably the Capitol or the White House. Both towers collapsed within two hours.

Nearly three thousand people from more than ninety countries died, the deadliest attack on American soil. Firefighters and police climbed the stairs as others fled, and 343 firefighters were among the dead. Air travel in the United States halted for days. It was a day of horror, and also of courage, as many ordinary people helped strangers.
