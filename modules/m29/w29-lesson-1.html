🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

After twenty-three years of war, the kings of Europe sat down to rebuild. Today you will see how they tried to make a lasting peace, why it worked for a generation, and how two new ideas, liberty and nationhood, finally broke through in the revolutions of 1848.

THE CONGRESS OF VIENNA

From September 1814 to June 1815, diplomats gathered in Vienna to settle Europe after Napoleon. The host was Prince Klemens von Metternich, the foreign minister of Austria. Britain sent Lord Castlereagh, Russia sent Tsar Alexander I himself, and Prussia sent its own ministers. France, which had just lost the war, sent Talleyrand, a veteran of every regime since the old monarchy. He was so skillful that France was soon sitting at the table as an equal.

The negotiators wanted two things above all. The first was a balance of powerBAL-uns uv POW-erAn arrangement in which no single nation grows strong enough to dominate all the others. See the glossary box., so that no country, whether France or any other, could again dominate the continent. The second was legitimacyluh-JIT-uh-muh-seeThe right to rule, based on law and custom, such as a king's claim through his family line. See the glossary box.: the return of the old ruling families, such as the Bourbons in France, whose claim to rule came from law and long custom and not from a general's sword. To guard against French attack, the diplomats strengthened the countries around it. They joined Belgium to the Netherlands and gave Prussia lands on the Rhine. They also created a German confederationkun-fed-er-AY-shunA loose union of states that keep most of their own powers and share only a few tasks. See the glossary box. of thirty-nine states, with Austria presiding. Tsar Alexander took most of Poland as a kingdom under his own crown, and Britain kept Malta, the Cape Colony, and Ceylon, which secured the sea lanes it needed. Austria regained Lombardy and Venetia in northern Italy.

The settlement was almost interrupted. In March 1815 Napoleon escaped from Elba and returned to France, and for a hundred days the war began again. It ended at Waterloo on June 18, 1815. Even so, the peace with France stayed fairly mild. The French kept their borders of 1792 and paid an indemnity. The statesmen wanted a France that was content, not one that dreamed of revenge.

A PEACE THAT LASTED

Judge the Congress by the first test you have learned to apply to any regime: did it keep the peace? On that measure it did well. No war between the great powers engulfed all of Europe for almost forty years, until the Crimean War of 1854. The great powers agreed to meet when trouble arose, which became known as the Concert of Europe. Alexander also proposed a Holy Alliance of Christian monarchs, though the others thought it mostly a pious gesture. The diplomats showed prudence, which is the habit of weighing consequences before acting, and moderation toward the defeated. A harsher peace might have made France a permanent enemy.

TWO NEW FORCES

Yet the settlement ignored two powerful ideas that the Revolution and Napoleon had scattered across Europe. The first was liberalismLIB-er-ul-iz-umIn the 1800s, the belief in written constitutions, elected parliaments, free speech, and protection of property. See the glossary box.. Liberals wanted written constitutions, elected parliaments, free speech, and the protection of private property. Most were not democrats. They tended to be merchants, lawyers, and educated men who wanted a voice for people like themselves.

The second was nationalismNASH-un-ul-iz-umThe belief that people who share a language, history, and homeland should form their own nation and state. See the glossary box.. Napoleon's armies had marched through Germany and Italy, and the people they conquered began to think of themselves as Germans or Italians, not as subjects of this prince or that bishop. Poets, scholars, and students sang and wrote about a shared language and history. But nationalism was dangerous to Austria. The Austrian emperor ruled Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Poles, Italians, and many others. If each people demanded its own state, his empire could fall to pieces.

Look at a map of Europe in 1815 and you will see the problem. The German-speaking lands were split among thirty-nine states, and the Italian peninsula among seven. The Poles had no state at all, and the Czechs, Hungarians, and Serbs lived under foreign emperors or sultans. Each of those peoples had its own poets and its own dream.

METTERNICH'S SYSTEM

Metternich saw this clearly, and he set out to hold the line. In 1819 a student murdered the conservative writer August von Kotzebue. Metternich used the event to push the Carlsbad Decrees through the German Confederation. They put universities under watch, banned student societies, and censored the press. Spies and police were common. Revolts that broke out in Spain and Naples in 1820 were put down by foreign troops.

But the pressure kept building. The Greeks rose against the Ottoman Empire in 1821 and, after years of war and the intervention of Britain, France, and Russia, won their independence by 1830. The poet Lord Byron died in 1824 helping them. In 1830 a revolution in Paris drove out Charles X and replaced him with a more liberal king, Louis-Philippe, and Belgium broke away from the Dutch. Britain, meanwhile, passed the Reform Act of 1832, which widened the vote, and repealed the Corn Laws in 1846, which had kept bread dear. Britain was changing through laws and elections. Much of the continent was not.

THE CUSTOMS UNION

In 1834 Prussia led most of the German states into a ZollvereinTSOL-fair-eyenA German word meaning "customs union." It was an agreement among German states to drop tariffs on goods crossing their borders. See the glossary box.. Goods now crossed dozens of borders without tolls. Merchants prospered, railways spread, and the German states drew closer together in everyday life. Notice who was left out: Austria. Without anyone intending it, a path was opening toward a Germany shaped around Prussia.

1848: THE SPRINGTIME OF PEOPLES

Then in 1845 and 1846 harvests failed, food grew dear, and workers lost their jobs. In February 1848 Paris rose again, and King Louis-Philippe fled. The news raced across Europe by newspaper and railway. In March crowds rose in Vienna, and Metternich, who had governed for nearly forty years, resigned and escaped to England. Revolts followed in Berlin, in Hungary under Lajos Kossuth, and in cities across Italy. Liberals demanded constitutions, nationalists demanded nations, and some workers demanded socialismSOH-shuh-liz-umThe idea that the main tools and factories should be owned by the whole community or by the workers, not by private owners. See the glossary box..

At Frankfurt an elected assembly met to write a constitution for all Germany. After long debate it offered the crown of a united Germany to King Frederick William IV of Prussia. He refused. He would not take a crown from an assembly of commoners, and he would not break with the other German princes. The assembly had no army of its own, and it was scattered in 1849.

The revolutions failed because the revolutionaries disagreed among themselves. Liberals feared the workers, nationalists quarreled with other nationalists, and the old armies stayed loyal to their rulers. Austria, with help from Russia, crushed the Hungarians. In France the voters chose Louis-Napoleon Bonaparte, a nephew of the emperor, as president in December 1848, and he made himself emperor four years later. A later German leader would draw a lesson from all this. The great questions of the day, he said, would not be decided by speeches and votes. You will meet him in the next lesson.
