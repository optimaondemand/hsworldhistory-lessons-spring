🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

After twenty-three years of war, the kings of Europe sat down to rebuild. Today you will see how they tried to make a lasting peace, why it worked for a generation, and how two new ideas, liberty and nationhood, finally broke through in the revolutions of 1848.

THE CONGRESS OF VIENNA

From September 1814 to June 1815, diplomats gathered in Vienna to settle Europe after Napoleon. The host was Prince Klemens von Metternich, the foreign minister of Austria. He came from a noble family of the Rhineland, had served as Austria's ambassador to Napoleon in Paris, and had helped arrange Napoleon's marriage to the emperor's daughter. Britain sent Lord Castlereagh, Russia sent Tsar Alexander I himself, and Prussia sent its own ministers. Alexander had ridden into Paris at the head of his army the year before, and he saw himself as the liberator of Europe. France, which had just lost the war, sent Talleyrand, a veteran of every regime since the old monarchy. He was so skillful that France was soon sitting at the table as an equal. The whole Congress never met in one room. The real work went on in small committees and private talks, while the city filled with balls and dinners that kept the crowned heads busy.

The negotiators wanted two things above all. The first was a balance of powerBAL-uns uv POW-erAn arrangement in which no single nation grows strong enough to dominate all the others. See the glossary box., so that no country, whether France or any other, could again dominate the continent. The second was legitimacyluh-JIT-uh-muh-seeThe right to rule, based on law and custom, such as a king's claim through his family line. See the glossary box.: the return of the old ruling families, such as the Bourbons in France, whose claim to rule came from law and long custom and not from a general's sword. To guard against French attack, the diplomats strengthened the countries around it. They joined Belgium to the Netherlands, enlarged the kingdom of Piedmont in northern Italy, and gave Prussia lands on the Rhine. They also created a German confederationkun-fed-er-AY-shunA loose union of states that keep most of their own powers and share only a few tasks. See the glossary box. of thirty-nine states, with Austria presiding. Tsar Alexander took most of Poland as a kingdom under his own crown, and Britain kept Malta, the Cape Colony, and Ceylon, which secured the sea lanes it needed. Austria regained Lombardy and Venetia in northern Italy.

The Congress nearly broke up over two prizes. Alexander wanted Poland, and Prussia wanted the German kingdom of Saxony, whose king had stayed loyal to Napoleon too long. Britain and Austria feared a Russia and a Prussia grown so large. Talleyrand saw his opening. He argued that the settlement should rest on legitimacy, and that the king of Saxony, whatever his faults, had a right to his throne. In January 1815 France joined Britain and Austria in a secret treaty to stand against Russia and Prussia if it came to that. War was close. The quarrel ended in a compromise. Russia received most of Poland, and Prussia received only part of Saxony, along with its lands on the Rhine.

The settlement was almost interrupted. In March 1815 Napoleon escaped from Elba and returned to France, and for a hundred days the war began again. It ended at Waterloo on June 18, 1815. Even so, the peace with France stayed fairly mild. The French kept their borders of 1792 and paid an indemnity. The statesmen wanted a France that was content, not one that dreamed of revenge. The Congress also condemned the slave trade as contrary to humanity, though it set no date for ending it.

Napoleon's second fall was final. After Waterloo he surrendered to the British, who sent him to Saint Helena, a speck of rock in the South Atlantic about twelve hundred miles from the nearest mainland. He died there in 1821. The Allies had learned what it cost to let him escape once, and they did not mean to let him escape twice.

A PEACE THAT LASTED

Judge the Congress by the first test you have learned to apply to any regime: did it keep the peace? On that measure it did well. No war between the great powers engulfed all of Europe for almost forty years, until the Crimean War of 1854. The great powers agreed to meet when trouble arose, which became known as the Concert of Europe. In November 1815 Britain, Austria, Prussia, and Russia renewed their alliance and promised to meet from time to time and deal with problems before they became wars. Alexander also proposed a Holy Alliance of Christian monarchs, though the others thought it mostly a pious gesture. Castlereagh privately called it "a piece of sublime mysticism and nonsense," and Britain never signed. The diplomats showed prudence, which is the habit of weighing consequences before acting, and moderation toward the defeated. A harsher peace might have made France a permanent enemy. But ask who had a say in all this. Belgians, Poles, Italians, and Norwegians were handed from one ruler to another, and no one asked them.

The system proved flexible at first. In 1818 the powers met again at Aix-la-Chapelle, agreed to withdraw their armies of occupation from France, and admitted France to their councils as a partner. A defeated enemy had been turned into a member of the club in only three years, which is much faster than most peace settlements manage.

TWO NEW FORCES

Yet the settlement ignored two powerful ideas that the Revolution and Napoleon had scattered across Europe. The first was liberalismLIB-er-ul-iz-umIn the 1800s, the belief in written constitutions, elected parliaments, free speech, and protection of property. See the glossary box.. Liberals wanted written constitutions, elected parliaments, free speech, and the protection of private property. Most were not democrats. They tended to be merchants, lawyers, and educated men who wanted a voice for people like themselves. Even the restored Louis XVIII had to grant France a written Charter in 1814, with two chambers of parliament, which showed how far the old order had to bend.

The second was nationalismNASH-un-ul-iz-umThe belief that people who share a language, history, and homeland should form their own nation and state. See the glossary box.. Napoleon's armies had marched through Germany and Italy, and the people they conquered began to think of themselves as Germans or Italians, not as subjects of this prince or that bishop. Poets, scholars, and students sang and wrote about a shared language and history. The philosopher Johann Gottfried Herder had argued that every people has its own spirit, shown most clearly in its language. The brothers Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm collected German folk tales and began a dictionary of the German language. In 1817 students gathered at the Wartburg castle in Germany to celebrate their country and burned the books of writers they disliked. But nationalism was dangerous to Austria. The Austrian emperor ruled Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Poles, Italians, and many others, and the Germans were only a minority of his subjects. If each people demanded its own state, his empire could fall to pieces.

Music carried the cause as well. Giuseppe Verdi wrote operas about oppressed peoples, and Italian audiences heard the chorus of enslaved Hebrews in his Nabucco, in 1842, as a song about themselves. In 1859 crowds chalked "Viva Verdi" on walls as a cheer the police could not easily forbid. The letters also stood for Vittorio Emanuele, Re d'Italia, the king of Piedmont whom they wished to see as king of all Italy.

Look at a map of Europe in 1815 and you will see the problem. The German-speaking lands were split among thirty-nine states, and the Italian peninsula among seven. The Poles had no state at all, and the Czechs, Hungarians, and Serbs lived under foreign emperors or sultans. Each of those peoples had its own poets and its own dream. In 1831 the Italian exile Giuseppe Mazzini founded a society called Young Italy to work for a single Italian nation.

METTERNICH'S SYSTEM

Metternich saw this clearly, and he set out to hold the line. In 1819 a student named Karl Sand murdered the conservative writer August von Kotzebue. Metternich used the event to push the Carlsbad Decrees through the German Confederation. They put universities under watch, banned student societies, and censored the press. Spies and police were common. Revolts that broke out in Spain and Naples in 1820 were put down by foreign troops. Austrian soldiers marched into Naples, and in 1823 a French army crossed the Pyrenees into Spain. Britain refused to take part.

But the pressure kept building. The Greeks rose against the Ottoman Empire in 1821 and, after years of war and the intervention of Britain, France, and Russia, won their independence by 1830. The poet Lord Byron died in 1824 helping them. In 1827 a British, French, and Russian fleet destroyed the Ottoman and Egyptian fleet at Navarino. In 1830 King Charles X tried to muzzle the press and dissolve the legislature, and Paris answered with barricades in three days. A revolution drove out Charles X and replaced him with a more liberal king, Louis-Philippe, and Belgium broke away from the Dutch. The Poles rose against Russian rule that year too, and were crushed. Britain, meanwhile, passed the Reform Act of 1832, which widened the vote, and repealed the Corn Laws in 1846, which had kept bread dear. The prime minister, Robert Peel, did so while the failure of the Irish potato crop was starving Ireland. Britain was changing through laws and elections. Much of the continent was not.

THE CUSTOMS UNION

Before 1834 a merchant who sent cloth across Germany might pay a toll at nearly every frontier, and the frontiers were many. In 1834 Prussia led most of the German states into a ZollvereinTSOL-fair-eyenA German word meaning "customs union." It was an agreement among German states to drop tariffs on goods crossing their borders. See the glossary box.. Goods now crossed dozens of borders without tolls. Merchants prospered, railways spread, and the German states drew closer together in everyday life. The first German railway had opened in 1835, and the economist Friedrich List argued that railways and a single market would make a Germany that existed in trade before it existed on any map. Notice who was left out: Austria. Without anyone intending it, a path was opening toward a Germany shaped around Prussia.

The union began with eighteen states and about twenty-three million people. The tariff money collected along its outer edges was shared among the members by population, so even small states gained, and in the years that followed the members moved toward common weights, measures, and coins. A merchant in Leipzig could now plan his business as if Germany were one country, long before any government declared it so.

1848: THE SPRINGTIME OF PEOPLES

Then in 1845 and 1846 harvests failed, a potato blight spread, food grew dear, and workers lost their jobs. In France political meetings were banned, so opponents of the government held reform banquets instead. When the government forbade one in February 1848, crowds built barricades in the streets of Paris, and King Louis-Philippe fled. The news raced across Europe by newspaper and railway. In March crowds rose in Vienna, and Metternich, who had governed for nearly forty years, resigned and escaped to England. Revolts followed in Berlin, in Hungary under Lajos Kossuth, and in cities across Italy. In Milan the people fought the Austrian garrison in the streets for five days. Harvests failed in 1845 and 1846, food grew dear, and workers lost their jobs. Then Paris rose in February 1848, and the news raced across Europe.

At Frankfurt an elected assembly met to write a constitution for all Germany. Many of its members were professors and lawyers. They argued for months over whether Austria, with its many non-German peoples, should be part of the new Germany. A Germany without Austria would be led by Prussia. After long debate it offered the crown of a united Germany to King Frederick William IV of Prussia. He refused. He would not take a crown from an assembly of commoners, and he would not break with the other German princes. He is said to have called it a crown from the gutter. The assembly had no army of its own, and it was scattered in 1849.

The revolutions failed because the revolutionaries disagreed among themselves. Liberals feared the workers, nationalists quarreled with other nationalists, and the old armies stayed loyal to their rulers. In France the new republic opened workshops to give jobs to the unemployed, and then closed them in June. The workers of Paris rose again, General Cavaignac crushed them in four days of street fighting, and thousands died. After that, middle-class liberals everywhere were slower to side with the poor. In Austria the government ended the old feudal dues that peasants owed their landlords, and many peasants lost interest in the revolution once they had what they wanted. In Italy the king of Piedmont went to war with Austria and lost. Austria, with help from the Russian army of Tsar Nicholas I, crushed the Hungarians in 1849. The sickly Austrian emperor gave up his throne for his eighteen-year-old nephew, Franz Joseph, who would reign for sixty-eight years. In France the voters chose Louis-Napoleon Bonaparte, a nephew of the emperor, as president in December 1848, and he made himself emperor four years later. A later German leader would draw a lesson from all this. The great questions of the day, he said, would not be decided by speeches and votes. You will meet him in the next lesson.

Britain had its own scare. On April 10, 1848, the Chartists planned a great march to Parliament with another petition. The government swore in tens of thousands of ordinary citizens as special constables, and the Duke of Wellington, now seventy-eight, quietly arranged for troops. Rain fell, the crowd on Kennington Common was smaller than feared, and the leaders agreed to send the petition by cab. A country with a Parliament that had been widening the vote could let off steam in ways that much of the continent could not.
