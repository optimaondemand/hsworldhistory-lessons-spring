🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

In 1850 there was no Italy and no Germany, only a patchwork of kingdoms, duchies, and cities. By 1871 both were nations. Today you will meet three men who helped to bring this about, and ask how much of it was their doing and how much would have happened anyway.

ITALY: A GEOGRAPHIC EXPRESSION

After 1815 Metternich called Italy a mere geographic expression. Austria held Lombardy and Venetia in the north, the pope ruled Rome and the center, a Bourbon king held the south, and the little kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia, ruled by the House of Savoy, sat in the northwest. The movement to unite them was called the risorgimentoree-sor-jee-MEN-tohAn Italian word meaning "resurgence" or "rising again." It names the movement to unite Italy in the 1800s. See the glossary box.. It had poets, conspirators, and dreamers. Giuseppe Mazzini, an exile and a passionate republican, wanted an Italian republic and founded the group Young Italy. His risings failed again and again, and in 1849 a short-lived republic in Rome, defended by volunteers under Giuseppe Garibaldi, was crushed by French troops, who put the pope back in power and stayed to guard him. That same year Piedmont's king lost a war against Austria and gave up his throne to his son, Victor Emmanuel II. The new king kept the written constitution, while the other Italian rulers took theirs away. That made Piedmont the one state in Italy where liberals and nationalists could hope for something.

After the Roman Republic fell, Garibaldi led a few thousand men out of the city and tried to reach Venice, which was still holding out against Austria. Pursued by four armies, he lost most of his men, and his wife Anita, who had ridden beside him, died on the march. He escaped into exile and for a time worked in a candle factory on Staten Island, in New York. Italy's most famous fighter had been beaten, and he did not give up.

The man who finally succeeded was a practical statesman. Count Camillo di Cavour became prime minister of Piedmont in 1852. He was not a soldier or a romantic. He was a landowner who had experimented with fertilizers and irrigation, and as a minister he built railways, cut tariffs, and strengthened the army. He knew that Piedmont alone could not defeat Austria, so he looked for an ally. He sent about fifteen thousand troops to join Britain and France in the Crimean War, which won him a place at the peace table, where he raised the Italian question before the great powers. In 1858 he met Napoleon III of France in secret and made an agreement: in return for Nice and Savoy, France would help to drive Austria from northern Italy.

⚡ DECIDE FIRST
It is 1858. You are Count Cavour, and little Piedmont cannot defeat Austria alone.
Decide first, and jot your answer in your notebook: where would you look for help, and what might it cost you? Then open to compare.
Cavour looked for an ally and found one in Napoleon III. The price was Nice and Savoy, and the bargain carried a risk: an ally has aims of his own and may change them. Another path was to wait for Italians to rise on their own, as Mazzini hoped, but his risings failed again and again. Each path had its own risks, and neither was certain to work.

Their meeting took place at the spa town of Plombières in July 1858, and the bargain had a second part. Napoleon's cousin, Prince Napoleon, was to marry Victor Emmanuel's fifteen-year-old daughter, Clotilde. The wedding took place in January 1859. A king was giving his daughter to a prince of a family he did not much trust, in exchange for the one thing Piedmont could not make for itself, a French army.

WAR, VOLUNTEERS, AND A KINGDOM

War came in 1859, after Cavour had maneuvered Austria into declaring it, so that Piedmont would look like the victim. The French and Piedmontese won the bloody battles of Magenta and Solferino. A Swiss traveler, Henri Dunant, saw the wounded lying untended after Solferino, and the experience led him to found the Red Cross. But Napoleon III grew alarmed at the cost and at the nationalist forces he had set loose. Without warning he signed a peace with Austria, leaving Venetia in Austrian hands. Cavour was furious and resigned. Even so, Piedmont received Lombardy, and the people of Tuscany and other central states voted to join it in 1860.

Dunant wrote a book, A Memory of Solferino, in 1862, describing what he had seen and asking that trained volunteers be ready to care for the wounded of any army. In 1863 a small committee met in Geneva, and in 1864 twelve governments signed the first Geneva Convention, promising to protect wounded soldiers and the people who tended them. A battle in northern Italy had led to a rule that still governs how armies treat the wounded.

Then came the second figure. Giuseppe Garibaldi was a seaman and a famous guerrilla fighter. He had been born in Nice, and he never forgave Cavour for giving it to France. In May 1860 he sailed from Genoa with about a thousand volunteers in red shirts and landed in Sicily. Outnumbered, they defeated the Bourbon troops at Calatafimi, Sicilian peasants began to join them, and in the summer they crossed to the mainland and marched into Naples. Cavour did not trust Garibaldi, who was a republican, but he also could not stop him. Fearing that Garibaldi would march on Rome and bring the French army down on Italy, Cavour sent a Piedmontese army through the Papal States to meet him. Garibaldi, to his credit, put the good of Italy above his own ambitions. He met King Victor Emmanuel II of Piedmont, greeted him as king of Italy, and handed him the whole south. Voters in the south approved union with Piedmont, and Garibaldi went home to his farm on a small island.

Luck did a great deal of the work at the start. The thousand sailed in two old steamships, and they came ashore at Marsala while two British warships lay in the harbor. The Bourbon captains hesitated to fire for fear of hitting the British, and by the time they opened fire the volunteers were already on land. Had the Bourbon navy been waiting in force, Garibaldi's expedition might have ended on the beach.

In March 1861 the Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed. Its king kept the name Victor Emmanuel II, as if Italy were simply Piedmont enlarged. Cavour died that June, only a few months later. The new kingdom was not an easy one to govern. The north was prosperous and the south poor, and many southerners felt ruled from far away. A Piedmontese statesman, Massimo d'Azeglio, is often quoted as saying that now that Italy was made, Italians had to be made. The pope refused to recognize the new state for decades. Italy added Venice in 1866 as an ally of Prussia, and Rome in 1870, when the French garrison went home to fight Germany.

    PRUSSIA AND ITS MINISTER

    Germany's road was different. After 1848, Austria and Prussia were rivals in the German confederationkun-fed-er-AY-shunA loose union of states that keep most of their own powers and share only a few tasks. See the glossary box.. In 1850 Austria had forced Prussia to back down in a dispute over leading the German states, and Prussia's leaders did not forget the humiliation. Prussia had the stronger economy, helped by the zollvereinTSOL-fair-eyenA German word meaning "customs union." It was an agreement among German states to drop tariffs on goods crossing their borders. See the glossary box. and its coal and iron, and the better army. In 1862 King William I wanted to enlarge that army, but the elected parliament refused to pay for it. The king, close to giving up the throne, called on Otto von Bismarck.

    The army that the king wanted to enlarge was largely the work of his minister of war, Albrecht von Roon. Roon wanted three years of service for every recruit and a larger number of men called up each year, and he wanted a reserve of trained men who could be called back in a crisis. The parliament was happy to pay for defense but would not pay for an army that the king alone commanded. The quarrel was about money, but it was also about who ruled Prussia, the crown or the parliament.

    Bismarck came from the Junkers, the landed nobility of eastern Prussia. He had been Prussia's envoy to the German confederation at Frankfurt, where he saw for himself how weak Austria's hold was, and then ambassador in Saint Petersburg and Paris. He was a clever diplomat and a believer in realpolitikray-AHL-poh-lee-teekA German word for politics based on power and practical results rather than on ideals. See the glossary box., politics based on power and results. He told the parliament that the great questions would be settled not by speeches and majority votes but by iron and blood, and that the mistake of 1848 had been to think otherwise. He then ruled for years without a legal budget. Notice what kind of leader he was. He was no romantic nationalist. He wanted a strong Prussia, and he used German nationalism as a tool when it served that purpose.

    THREE WARS

    His first war came in 1864. Prussia and Austria together defeated Denmark and took the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein. The question of who should rule those duchies was so tangled that the British prime minister, Lord Palmerston, is said to have joked that only three men had ever understood it, and one was dead, one was mad, and he himself had forgotten it. Bismarck then quarreled with Austria over how to govern them, and prepared for war with his partner. He made sure Russia stayed neutral and gained the friendship of Italy. In 1866 the Prussian army, moved by railroad and armed with the breech-loading needle gun, crushed the Austrians at Königgrätz in a matter of weeks. Its chief of staff, Helmuth von Moltke, planned the campaign, and he sent his armies to the front along several railway lines at once. A soldier with a needle gun could reload lying down and fire several times as fast as an Austrian with a muzzle-loader. More than four hundred thousand men fought at Königgrätz, one of the largest battles Europe had yet seen. For hours the outcome hung in doubt, until the Crown Prince's army, which had marched late, struck the Austrian flank.

    ⚡ THE WAR OF 1866
    The Prussian army crushed the Austrians in a matter of weeks in 1866. How much of that speed came from the way the army was moved and armed?

    Here Bismarck showed his judgment. The king and the generals wanted to march on Vienna and take land. Bismarck argued against it, so fiercely that he was close to resigning, until the Crown Prince helped to bring the old king around. He took no Austrian territory, because he wanted Austria as a friend in the future. Austria was excluded from Germany, and the German states north of the Main River joined a North German Confederation under Prussian leadership. Prussia swallowed several of Austria's allies outright, including the kingdom of Hanover. The lower house of the new confederation was elected by all adult men. Bismarck was betting that ordinary voters would stand by the king more than the liberal middle class did.

    Defeat at Königgrätz had another result. In 1867 the Austrian emperor made a bargain with the Hungarians, and his empire became a dual monarchy, Austria-Hungary, in which Hungary governed its own affairs under the same ruler. The compromise saved the empire for another half century. But it left the Czechs, Slovaks, Serbs, Croats, and other peoples under the rule of the two favored nations, and the problem Metternich had feared in 1815 did not go away.

    The last step was France. In 1870 a quarrel arose over a Hohenzollern prince's candidacy for the Spanish throne. The prince withdrew, but the French government demanded a promise that he would never run again. Bismarck received a telegram from the king's vacation spot at Ems describing a polite meeting with the French ambassador, in which the king had refused, and he edited it to sound sharper. He told his colleagues it would work on the French like a red rag on a bull. French opinion was inflamed, and Napoleon III declared war in July. The southern German states joined Prussia, and the German armies won victory after victory. On September 1, 1870, Napoleon III was captured at Sedan. Two days later the empire fell in Paris, and the new republic fought on. Paris held out through a long siege in which its people ate their cats, rats, and even the animals of the zoo. On January 18, 1871, in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, the palace of the French kings, King William was proclaimed German Emperor. Bismarck became the first chancellorCHAN-suh-lerThe chief minister of a government in some countries, such as Germany. See the glossary box. of the new empire. France paid a large indemnity and lost Alsace and part of Lorraine, a loss it would not forget.

    At Sedan a French army of more than a hundred thousand men, with the emperor himself among them, was surrounded on low ground and shelled from the hills above. Napoleon III, sick and in pain, finally sent out a white flag. The surrender gave Germany a great victory. It did not end the war, because Paris refused to quit.

    Compare the cost with the great wars of the past. The fighting that made Germany lasted only a few weeks in 1866 and about ten months in 1870 and 1871. Yet the result was a new great power in the middle of the continent. The new Germany had some forty-one million people, more than France, and its coal and steel output would soon pass Britain's. The British statesman Benjamin Disraeli told the House of Commons that the war was a greater political event than the French Revolution, because it had overturned the order that Europe had known since 1815.

    WHAT MADE THE NATIONS?

    Was it the leaders, or was it deeper forces? In both countries, the answer is a mixture. NationalismNASH-un-ul-iz-umThe belief that people who share a language, history, and homeland should form their own nation and state. See the glossary box., railways, and trade made unity attractive and likely. But the actual shape of the new states depended on particular decisions: Napoleon III's secret bargain, Garibaldi's gamble, King William's choice of Bismarck, and Bismarck's restraint after Königgrätz. A Germany that included Austria was entirely possible. So was an Italy of two or three kingdoms. If Frederick William IV had accepted the crown at Frankfurt in 1849, a very different Germany might have been born.
