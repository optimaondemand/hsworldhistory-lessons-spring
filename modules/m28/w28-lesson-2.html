🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

The new machines made goods cheap, and they also made new problems. Today you will walk into a mill town, meet three thinkers who tried to explain what was happening, and ask which of them saw it most clearly.

LIFE IN THE MILL TOWNS

Imagine waking before dawn to a factory bell. In many towns a man called a knocker-up walked the streets tapping on bedroom windows with a long pole to rouse the workers. In the early cotton mills a normal day ran twelve to fourteen hours, six days a week, and a worker who came late could be fined. The air was thick with cotton dust and the machines were loud, hot, and unguarded. A tired worker could lose a hand or be pulled into the gears. Owners hired women and children because they cost less than men and could reach in among the machines. Some children came from workhouses and were sent to the mills as apprentices at seven or eight years old. Their jobs were small and dangerous. They crawled under the moving spinning machines to pick up loose cotton and tied broken threads, while grown men ran the machines.

Outside the mill, the towns grew faster than anyone could plan. Families crowded into cheap rows of brick houses, and the poorest lived in damp cellars. Coal smoke blackened the sky. Many streets had no drains or clean water, and outbreaks of cholera and typhus swept through them. In 1842 a government report by Edwin Chadwick showed that laborers in the new towns died on average far younger than laborers in the countryside. Historians still argue about how much worse this was than the poverty of the countryside. Most agree that wages for ordinary workers began to rise noticeably only after about 1830 or 1840. Others point out that many people moved to the towns of their own choosing, because the wages there were better than the wages at home.

Disease followed the water. Cholera first reached Britain in 1831 and came back in later waves, killing tens of thousands. Doctors argued over whether it spread through foul air or through water. In 1854 a London physician, John Snow, marked the homes of the dead on a map of Soho and found them clustered around one public water pump on Broad Street. When the pump's handle was removed, the outbreak there faded. It took years for his explanation to win acceptance, and longer still for towns to build sewers and bring in clean water.

Some workers resisted the machines directly. Between 1811 and 1812, bands of skilled stocking weavers in the English Midlands, known as Luddites, broke the frames that were taking their work. They took their name from a legendary leader, "Ned Ludd," whom no one could find. They chose their targets, too, and mostly struck the owners who cut wages or turned out cheap, shoddy goods. Parliament answered by making frame-breaking a crime punishable by death, and thousands of soldiers were sent to the region. Lord Byron spoke against the law in the House of Lords, but it passed, and more than a dozen men were hanged at York in 1813. Workers had long been barred from forming unions by the Combination Acts of 1799 and 1800. Later, they turned to peaceful means. Parliament repealed the laws against workers' associations in 1824, and trade unions slowly took shape.

Breaking the machines was a direct answer, and some workers chose it. Parliament replied by making frame-breaking a crime punishable by death. A different path was open: working together peacefully. Parliament repealed the laws against workers' associations in 1824, and trade unions slowly took shape. Nobody in 1811 could know how either path would turn out, and each had its own costs.
REFORM BY LAW

Many Britons were moved by what they heard about life under the factory systemFAK-tuh-ree SIS-tumA way of making goods in which many workers come to one building and work with power-driven machines under a manager. See the glossary box.. An early law of 1802 had limited the hours of the children sent from workhouses, but no one was paid to enforce it, and it was seldom obeyed. In 1832 a committee led by the member of Parliament Michael Sadler questioned mill workers about their hours and treatment. Witnesses told the committee that overseers beat children who fell asleep at their work, and its report shocked the public. Mill owners protested that the witnesses had been chosen to prove a point, and a later royal commission painted a more mixed picture. But the Factory Act of 1833 passed. It barred children under nine from textile mills, limited the hours of older children, and, most important, created paid inspectors to see that the law was kept. There were only four of them at first, for the whole country. The Ten Hours Act of 1847 limited the working day for women and young people. Lord Ashley, later Earl of Shaftesbury, spent decades pressing for these laws, motivated by his Christian faith.

Other changes followed. The Reform Act of 1832 gave the vote to more middle-class men and gave seats in Parliament to new cities such as Manchester, which had never had a member of its own. It nearly failed. The House of Lords voted the bill down in 1831, riots broke out in Bristol and other cities, and in 1832 the Lords gave way only after the king agreed to create enough new peers to pass it. Workers, who still had no vote, drew up the People's Charter in 1838, which demanded the vote for all men and a secret ballot. They presented petitions with millions of names, and Parliament turned them down. But over the following decades most of the Charter's demands, such as the secret ballot, became law. In 1842 a royal commission published drawings of women and children hauling coal carts through low tunnels underground, and the public was shocked. Parliament barred women and boys under ten from working in the mines. Many of the men who led the unions and reform societies had learned to speak in public and keep accounts in Methodist chapels. Reform in Britain came from Parliament, from churches, and from owners and workers who argued in public, and it came without a revolution.

Not every reform was gentle. The same Parliament that passed the Factory Act reformed poor relief in 1834. Under the new Poor Law, an able-bodied man who asked for help had to enter a workhouse, where families were separated and the food was meant to be plainer than what a laborer could earn outside. The aim was to push men toward work and to cut the cost of relief. Many of the poor came to dread the workhouse, and Charles Dickens's novel Oliver Twist, published in 1838, turned that dread into a story the public could not forget.

ADAM SMITH: THE FREE MARKET

The first thinker is the Scottish professor Adam Smith, who published The Wealth of Nations in 1776, before most of these machines existed. He had first made his name with a book about moral feeling, The Theory of Moral Sentiments, which argued that people are moved by sympathy for one another. In his second book he described a pin factory. One man alone might make a few pins a day. But when the job was split into about eighteen small steps, ten workers could make tens of thousands. One drew out the wire, another straightened it, a third cut it, and a fourth sharpened the point. A man who spends his life on one or two simple tasks, he warned, "generally becomes as stupid and ignorant as it is possible for a human creature to become." For that reason he wanted government to help pay for schools for the children of the poor.

Smith argued that a free marketFREE MAR-kitAn economy in which buyers and sellers make their own choices about prices and trades, with little control by the government. See the glossary box. would turn each person's pursuit of his own interest into a benefit for others. The baker bakes bread to earn a living, not out of kindness, yet the town is fed. He said people were guided as if by "an invisible hand." Smith did not think government had no job. He thought it should defend the country, keep justice, and build roads and bridges. He also wrote that a society could hardly be happy if most of its members were poor, and he favored good wages. Later defenders of the factory system would lean on his name, and he would have warned them against owners who combined to keep prices high. In the same book he wrote, "People of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment and diversion, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public, or in some contrivance to raise prices." The market, in his view, worked well only where owners were not allowed to join together against their customers.

⚡ THE ROLE OF GOVERNMENT
Adam Smith praised the market. How much of a role did he give government?

Smith was no flatterer of employers when it came to wages, either. He wrote that masters were always and everywhere in a sort of quiet but steady agreement not to raise wages above their usual rate, and that when workers combined, the law came down on them far harder than it did on masters who did the same. A reader in the 1820s could set that remark beside the Combination Acts and draw a conclusion Smith himself might have shared.

ROBERT OWEN: THE MODEL MILL

The second thinker was not a professor. Robert Owen, a Welshman who rose from shop boy to mill manager, took over the cotton mills at New Lanark, in Scotland, in 1800. The mills had belonged to his father-in-law, David Dale. Owen cut the working day, refused to hire young children, built a school, opened a shop that sold decent goods at fair prices, and cleaned up the village. He even hung a small four-colored block over each worker's machine, turned to a color that showed how well that worker had behaved that day, so that managers did not have to scold. Visitors came from across Europe to see the result. The mills still made a profit. Owen pressed Parliament for a law to protect children in the mills, and an act passed in 1819, though it was weakly enforced. Owen argued that people are shaped by their surroundings and that good surroundings would make good workers. If surroundings alone make a person's character, though, there is little room left for choice and virtue. Ask yourself whether that is true.

By about 1816 Owen had opened a building he called the New Institution for the Formation of Character, with a school for the youngest children, who were taught through play, song, and dancing rather than the rod. The village held around two thousand people, and the mill's working day had been cut to about ten and a half hours. Grand Duke Nicholas of Russia, later Tsar Nicholas I, was among the visitors who came to see it.

Later Owen grew bolder. He believed a whole society could be built on shared property and cooperation, and he started a community at New Harmony, Indiana, in 1825. It broke up within a few years. His followers were among the first to be called socialists, and the word socialismSOH-shuh-liz-umThe idea that the main tools and factories should be owned by the whole community or by the workers, not by private owners. KARL MARX: THE CLASH OF CLASSES

The third thinker was Karl Marx, a German philosopher born in 1818. Exiled for his writings, he settled in London, where he spent his days reading in the British Museum and lived much of his life in poverty. His friend Friedrich Engels, whose family owned a cotton mill in Manchester, had written a harsh account of the workers' lives there, and Engels's income from the mill helped to support Marx. Together they published The Communist Manifesto in 1848, for a small group of exiled workers, and few people read it at the time. Its famous opening claim was that the history of all society was the history of class struggle. In the English translation of 1888 the sentence reads, "The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles."     The heart of Capital was an argument about wages. A worker, Marx said, creates more value in a day than he is paid for, and the owner keeps the difference as profit. The more machines the owner installs, the more he can squeeze from each worker, and the wider the gap between owners and workers grows. Many economists later argued that this theory misjudged how wages and prices are set, and wages did rise over the century instead of falling. Still, the question he asked was hard to dismiss. Why did the gains of one side so often look like the losses of the other?

    Marx wrote with real force, and he drew attention to real hardships. But judge his argument as you would any other. He claimed that economic conditions drive nearly everything else, and that ideas, religion, and law are only reflections of them. Yet the factory laws you just read about came partly from ideas, since they were driven by Christian conscience and public argument. He also predicted that workers in the most industrial country would rebel first. Britain's workers did not. Their wages rose, their unions grew, and Parliament widened the vote. The revolutions that later took Marx's name broke out in a farming country, Russia, which you will meet in the coming weeks.

    WHO WAS RIGHT?

    Smith explained why markets create wealth. Owen showed that a mill could be run with decency and still pay. Marx showed how sharply the new order divided owners from workers. Each caught something true, and none of them told the whole story. In this week's discussion you will weigh a question that still divides people: who paid the price for the factory system, and who gained? Think about the primary sources you will read as well. They come from people who lived through the change and wrote down what they saw.
