🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

The new machines made goods cheap, and they also made new problems. Today you will walk into a mill town, meet three thinkers who tried to explain what was happening, and ask which of them saw it most clearly.

LIFE IN THE MILL TOWNS

Imagine waking before dawn to a factory bell. In the early cotton mills a normal day ran twelve to fourteen hours, six days a week. The air was thick with cotton dust and the machines were loud, hot, and unguarded. A tired worker could lose a hand or be pulled into the gears. Owners hired women and children because they cost less than men and could reach in among the machines. Some children came from workhouses and were sent to the mills as apprentices at seven or eight years old.

Outside the mill, the towns grew faster than anyone could plan. Families crowded into cheap rows of brick houses. Coal smoke blackened the sky. Many streets had no drains or clean water, and outbreaks of cholera and typhus swept through them. Historians still argue about how much worse this was than the poverty of the countryside. Most agree that wages for ordinary workers began to rise noticeably only after about 1830 or 1840. Others point out that many people moved to the towns of their own choosing, because the wages there were better than the wages at home.

Some workers resisted the machines directly. Between 1811 and 1812, bands of skilled stocking weavers in the English Midlands, known as Luddites, broke the frames that were taking their work. Parliament answered by making frame-breaking a crime punishable by death. Later, workers turned to peaceful means. Parliament repealed the laws against workers' associations in 1824, and trade unions slowly took shape.

REFORM BY LAW

Many Britons were moved by what they heard about life under the factory systemFAK-tuh-ree SIS-tumA way of making goods in which many workers come to one building and work with power-driven machines under a manager. See the glossary box.. In 1832 a committee led by the member of Parliament Michael Sadler questioned mill workers about their hours and treatment, and its report shocked the public. Mill owners protested that the witnesses had been chosen to prove a point, and a later royal commission painted a more mixed picture. But the Factory Act of 1833 passed. It barred children under nine from textile mills, limited the hours of older children, and, most important, created paid inspectors to see that the law was kept. The Ten Hours Act of 1847 limited the working day for women and young people. Lord Ashley, later Earl of Shaftesbury, spent decades pressing for these laws, motivated by his Christian faith. Reform in Britain came from Parliament, from churches, and from owners and workers who argued in public, and it came without a revolution.

ADAM SMITH: THE FREE MARKET

The first thinker is the Scottish professor Adam Smith, who published The Wealth of Nations in 1776, before most of these machines existed. Smith described a pin factory. One man alone might make a few pins a day. But when the job was split into about eighteen small steps, ten workers could make tens of thousands. He also wrote that a society could hardly be happy if most of its members were poor, and he favored good wages. Later defenders of the factory system would lean on his name, and he would have warned them against owners who combined to keep prices high.

ROBERT OWEN: THE MODEL MILL

The second thinker was not a professor. Robert Owen, a Welshman who rose from shop boy to mill manager, took over the cotton mills at New Lanark, in Scotland, in 1800. He cut the working day, refused to hire young children, built a school, opened a shop that sold decent goods at fair prices, and cleaned up the village. Visitors came from across Europe to see the result. The mills still made a profit. Owen argued that people are shaped by their surroundings and that good surroundings would make good workers.

Later Owen grew bolder. He believed a whole society could be built on shared property and cooperation, and he started a community at New Harmony, Indiana, in 1825. It broke up within a few years. KARL MARX: THE CLASH OF CLASSES

The third thinker was Karl Marx, a German philosopher born in 1818. Exiled for his writings, he settled in London. His friend Friedrich Engels, whose family owned a cotton mill in Manchester, had written a harsh account of the workers' lives there. Together they published The Communist Manifesto in 1848. Its famous opening claim was that the history of all society was the history of class struggle. Marx wrote with real force, and he drew attention to real hardships. But judge his argument as you would any other. He claimed that economic conditions drive nearly everything else, and that ideas, religion, and law are only reflections of them. Yet the factory laws you just read about came partly from ideas, since they were driven by Christian conscience and public argument. He also predicted that workers in the most industrial country would rebel first. Britain's workers did not. Their wages rose, their unions grew, and Parliament widened the vote. The revolutions that later took Marx's name broke out in a farming country, Russia, which you will meet in the coming weeks.

WHO WAS RIGHT?

Smith explained why markets create wealth. Owen showed that a mill could be run with decency and still pay. Marx showed how sharply the new order divided owners from workers. Each caught something true, and none of them told the whole story. In this week's discussion you will weigh a question that still divides people: who paid the price for the factory system, and who gained? Think about the primary sources you will read as well. They come from people who lived through the change and wrote down what they saw.
