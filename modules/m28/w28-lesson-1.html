🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

In 1750 Britain was a farming country with a few busy ports. A century later it was the workshop of the world. Today you will see how coal, iron, and cotton changed the way people made things, and why that change began on one rainy island and not somewhere else.

AN ISLAND WITH COAL

For most of history, people made goods by hand, in homes and small shops, with muscle, wood, wind, and running water. In the English countryside much of the cloth was made under what was called the putting-out system. A merchant delivered wool to a cottage, the family spun and wove it by their own fireside, and the merchant came back to collect the cloth and pay by the piece. Because every family worked at its own pace and the merchant could not watch them, the cloth was uneven in quality and often arrived late. By the 1700s Britain was running short of wood, but it had plenty of coal. Coal lay close to the surface and close to the sea, and ships carried it from Newcastle to London in great numbers, where it warmed the fireplaces of the city. It was cheap. Wages, meanwhile, were fairly high for a European country.

Coal was already at work long before any engine. It heated homes, breweries, glassworks, and brickworks, so Britain had miners, shipowners, and merchants who knew how to dig it and move it. There was a catch. The shallow seams near the surface were the first to be worked out, and the miners had to follow the coal deeper, where water seeped into the shafts faster than buckets and horse-driven pumps could carry it out. A country that digs deeper for fuel has a reason to want a better pump.

Think about what that combination meant for a business owner. When workers are costly and fuel is cheap, a machine that saves labor and burns coal is a good bargain. Picture a mill owner deciding whether to buy a new machine. If the machine cost a great deal but did the work of ten workers who each earned a good wage, it paid for itself quickly. In a country where labor was cheap and fuel was dear, the same machine might never have been worth building. Nobody planned an industrial revolution. Thousands of people simply answered the prices and chances in front of them, and the answers added up to something new.

WHY BRITAIN?

Coal alone was not enough, since other countries had coal too. Britain also had a settled government. After 1688 Parliament and the courts protected property, so an investor could expect to keep what he earned. The law protected ideas as well. An inventor could patent a new machine and hold the sole right to sell it for fourteen years, which made the long labor of invention worth the risk. There were no tolls or tariffs between English towns, and rivers, the coast, and new canals made it cheap to move heavy goods. In France, by contrast, goods paid duties each time they crossed from one province to another until the Revolution swept the barriers away. The Bridgewater Canal, opened in 1761, ran from the Duke of Bridgewater's coal mines at Worsley to Manchester, and it cut the price of coal in Manchester almost in half. A single horse on a towpath could draw a barge carrying many times the load it could pull in a wagon.

Britain also had a large navy and an overseas empire, which gave its merchants markets and supplies of raw material. And it had a great many skilled craftsmen: clockmakers, mill builders, instrument makers, and blacksmiths who liked to tinker and improve. Many of the inventors in this lesson were not scholars. Thomas Newcomen was an ironmonger. James Hargreaves was a weaver, and Richard Arkwright began as a barber and wig-maker. They were practical men who knew a problem because they worked beside it. Many of them also swapped ideas in clubs. In Birmingham, Matthew Boulton and James Watt belonged to the Lunar Society, which met on the night of the full moon so that members could find their way home. Its members included the chemist Joseph Priestley and the potter Josiah Wedgwood.

Religion played a part too. Many of the early ironmasters, bankers, and manufacturers were Dissenters, Protestants outside the Church of England, such as the Quaker family of Abraham Darby. The law kept Dissenters out of the universities and out of public office. Shut out of those careers, they put their energy into trade, schooling, and invention, and they trusted one another with money because they knew each other from the meeting house.

None of this made the outcome certain. A different civil war, a ruined government, or a few inventors who gave up might have sent the island in another direction. Industrialization began in Britain because many separate things happened to be true there at once, and because particular people seized the chance.

THE STEAM ENGINE

Coal mines had a constant problem: water seeped in and flooded the shafts. In 1712 Thomas Newcomen built an engine that used steam to pump water out. It swallowed huge amounts of coal, but that hardly mattered at a coal mine, where fuel was nearly free. Anywhere else, coal had to be hauled in by cart, and the engine cost too much to run. So it stayed at the mines, and for half a century nobody did much better.

Newcomen's engine worked by a simple trick. Steam was let into a cylinder under a piston, and then a spray of cold water turned the steam back into water. The steam took up far more room than the water, so its disappearance left a partial vacuum, and the weight of the air outside pushed the piston down. A great wooden beam, rocking like a seesaw, carried that push to the pump rods in the shaft. The engine did not push with steam at all. It pulled with the air, and then it had to be heated all over again for the next stroke.

James Watt, a Scottish instrument maker, was asked to repair a small model of a Newcomen engine. He saw how much heat was wasted by heating and cooling the same cylinder over and over. By his own later account, the idea of a separate chamber for the steam to cool in came to him on a walk in 1765. In 1769 he patented a separate condenser that fixed the problem and used far less coal. Watt was no businessman, and he nearly gave up. Matthew Boulton, a Birmingham manufacturer, provided the money and the workshop, and in 1775 the two became partners. Parliament extended Watt's patent that year, which gave the partners time to earn back what they had spent. They did not simply sell an engine. They charged the owner about a third of the value of the coal it saved over a Newcomen engine, a price that made sense to a mine owner who counted his coal. The engines also needed cylinders bored round and true, and the ironmaster John Wilkinson had a machine that could do it. Within a few years their engines could turn a wheel, not just pump a shaft. For the first time, a factory did not have to sit beside a fast river. It could stand anywhere a cart of coal could reach.

THE COTTON REVOLUTION

The first great change came in textilesTEK-stylCloth, or the fibers such as wool and cotton from which cloth is made. See the glossary box.. Wool was Britain's old trade. Cotton was a newcomer, first imported from India and later grown in the West Indies and the American South by enslaved workers. A spinner at a wheel made one thread at a time, and weavers often stood idle waiting for yarn. John Kay's flying shuttle of 1733 had made weaving faster, and so made the shortage of yarn worse. Inventors attacked that bottleneck. James Hargreaves built the spinning jenny around 1764, which let one worker spin many threads at once. Spinners who feared for their living broke into his house and smashed his machines. Richard Arkwright's water frame of 1769 made strong thread by water power, and in 1771 he opened a large mill at Cromford that ran day and night with shifts of workers. A water frame needed a fast stream, so the early mills stood in river valleys, and Arkwright built houses for his workers beside his mill. The courts later canceled his patents, and rivals copied his methods freely, so mills of his kind spread quickly.

The jenny was simple enough that a family could build one at home. It had a row of spindles turned by one wheel, and the first machines spun eight threads at once, where a spinner at a wheel made one. Later models ran many times that number. The thread from the jenny was weak, though, and it was good only for the crosswise threads of the cloth. Arkwright's frame made the strong threads, and a damp climate helped, because the cotton fibers snapped less often in the moist air of Lancashire than in dry air.

Samuel Crompton's mule, in 1779, made thread that was both fine and strong, though he was too poor to patent it and others made the money. Edmund Cartwright's power loom, patented in 1785, mechanized the weaving as well. It spread slowly, and for decades handloom weavers outnumbered the machines.

The price of cotton cloth fell, and British cloth began to sell everywhere, even in India, which had once sold fine cotton to Britain. Raw cotton poured in, and after Eli Whitney's gin of 1793 made American short-staple cotton cheap to clean, the American South became Britain's main supplier. The gin made enslaved labor on cotton land more profitable than before, and slavery in the American South grew along with the British mills. Lancashire became the center of the trade, and the town of Manchester grew from about 70,000 people in 1800 to more than 300,000 by 1850.

IRON AND RAILS

Machines needed iron, and iron needed fuel. Smelters had burned charcoal, which meant cutting forests. In 1709 Abraham Darby at Coalbrookdale learned to smelt iron with coke, a fuel baked from coal. In the 1780s Henry Cort's puddling process made tough wrought iron in large amounts. Workers stirred the molten iron with long rods in a furnace until the carbon burned out, leaving metal that could be hammered and rolled instead of cracking. Iron went into engines, tools, ships, and the world's first iron bridge, built over the Severn in 1779 by Darby's grandson. Britain's output of pig iron rose from under 20,000 tons a year in 1740 to more than two million tons by 1850.

Then came the railway. Watt had distrusted steam at high pressure, which seemed likely to burst a boiler. When his patent ran out in 1800, younger engineers tried it anyway, and in 1804 Richard Trevithick ran a steam locomotive on rails in Wales. George Stephenson made the idea work as a business. Several builders entered a trial at Rainhill in 1829, and his locomotive, the Rocket, was the only one to finish the course, reaching about thirty miles an hour. Its boiler carried hot gases through many small tubes running through the water, which raised steam quickly. In 1830 the Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened, and carried passengers and cotton at speeds no horse could match. On opening day a member of Parliament, William Huskisson, stepped onto the track to greet the Duke of Wellington, was struck by the Rocket, and died that night. A trip that once took a day by coach took a couple of hours. Railways even changed how towns told time. Each town had kept its own local clock, but a timetable needed one time for the whole line, and by the 1840s railway time was spreading across Britain. Within twenty years, more than six thousand miles of track crossed Britain.

Railways also drew crowds of investors. In the 1840s so many companies were formed to build lines that the years around 1845 were called the Railway Mania. Shares rose, and ordinary families put their savings into them. Many of the lines were never built, and many investors lost money when prices fell. But the lines that were built left Britain with a network that carried coal, cotton, and mail across the island, and the failures had paid for much of it.

THE FACTORY

These changes produced the factory systemFAK-tuh-ree SIS-tumA way of making goods in which many workers come to one building and work with power-driven machines under a manager. See the glossary box.. In the old way, work went to the worker's cottage. Now the worker went to the machine. The machine set the pace, a bell marked the hours, and an overseer watched the floor. Workdays of twelve hours or more were common. Another idea shaped the new mills: breaking a job into small steps, so that each worker did one task over and over and became fast at it. A mill was a workshop run on that idea on a grand scale. In the next lesson you will meet the writer who made the idea famous. Owners who raised the money to build the mills and buy the engines were called capitalists, and they took the profit and the risk.

By 1850 Britain made about half the world's iron and a large share of its cloth. Its population had also grown fast, and the towns around the mills and mines drew people from every corner of the island and from Ireland. By 1851 more than half of all Britons lived in towns, the first large country where that was true. Ordinary people could buy cheap shirts and cheap pots that their grandparents could not have afforded. But a new kind of life had begun, in which hundreds of families lived in crowded towns and depended on a wage. How did that new life feel to those who lived it?
