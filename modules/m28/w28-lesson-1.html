🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

In 1750 Britain was a farming country with a few busy ports. A century later it was the workshop of the world. Today you will see how coal, iron, and cotton changed the way people made things, and why that change began on one rainy island and not somewhere else.

AN ISLAND WITH COAL

For most of history, people made goods by hand, in homes and small shops, with muscle, wood, wind, and running water. By the 1700s Britain was running short of wood, but it had plenty of coal. Coal lay close to the surface and close to the sea, and ships carried it from Newcastle to London in great numbers. It was cheap. Wages, meanwhile, were fairly high for a European country.

Think about what that combination meant for a business owner. When workers are costly and fuel is cheap, a machine that saves labor and burns coal is a good bargain. Nobody planned an industrial revolution. Thousands of people simply answered the prices and chances in front of them, and the answers added up to something new.

WHY BRITAIN?

Coal alone was not enough, since other countries had coal too. Britain also had a settled government. After 1688 Parliament and the courts protected property, so an investor could expect to keep what he earned. There were no tolls or tariffs between English towns, and rivers, the coast, and new canals made it cheap to move heavy goods. The Bridgewater Canal, opened in 1761, cut the price of coal in Manchester almost in half.

Britain also had a large navy and an overseas empire, which gave its merchants markets and supplies of raw material. None of this made the outcome certain. A different civil war, a ruined government, or a few inventors who gave up might have sent the island in another direction. Industrialization began in Britain because many separate things happened to be true there at once, and because particular people seized the chance.

THE STEAM ENGINE

Coal mines had a constant problem: water seeped in and flooded the shafts. In 1712 Thomas Newcomen built an engine that used steam to pump water out. It swallowed huge amounts of coal, but that hardly mattered at a coal mine, where fuel was nearly free.

James Watt, a Scottish instrument maker, was asked to repair a small model of a Newcomen engine. He saw how much heat was wasted by heating and cooling the same cylinder over and over. In 1769 he patented a separate condenser that fixed the problem and used far less coal. Watt was no businessman, and he nearly gave up. Matthew Boulton, a Birmingham manufacturer, provided the money and the workshop, and in 1775 the two became partners. Within a few years their engines could turn a wheel, not just pump a shaft. For the first time, a factory did not have to sit beside a fast river. It could stand anywhere a cart of coal could reach.

THE COTTON REVOLUTION

The first great change came in textilesTEK-stylCloth, or the fibers such as wool and cotton from which cloth is made. See the glossary box.. Wool was Britain's old trade. Cotton was a newcomer, first imported from India and later grown in the West Indies and the American South. A spinner at a wheel made one thread at a time, and weavers often stood idle waiting for yarn. Inventors attacked that bottleneck. James Hargreaves built the spinning jenny around 1764, which let one worker spin many threads at once. Richard Arkwright's water frame of 1769 made strong thread by water power, and in 1771 he opened a large mill at Cromford. Samuel Crompton's mule, in 1779, made thread that was both fine and strong. Edmund Cartwright's power loom, patented in 1785, mechanized the weaving as well.

The price of cotton cloth fell, and British cloth began to sell everywhere, even in India, which had once sold fine cotton to Britain. Raw cotton poured in, and after Eli Whitney's gin of 1793 made American short-staple cotton cheap to clean, the American South became Britain's main supplier. Lancashire became the center of the trade, and the town of Manchester grew from about 70,000 people in 1800 to more than 300,000 by 1850.

IRON AND RAILS

Machines needed iron, and iron needed fuel. Smelters had burned charcoal, which meant cutting forests. In 1709 Abraham Darby at Coalbrookdale learned to smelt iron with coke, a fuel baked from coal. In the 1780s Henry Cort's puddling process made tough wrought iron in large amounts. Iron went into engines, tools, ships, and the world's first iron bridge, built over the Severn in 1779.

Then came the railway. George Stephenson's locomotive, the Rocket, won a famous trial at Rainhill in 1829. In 1830 the Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened, and carried passengers and cotton at speeds no horse could match. A trip that once took a day by coach took a couple of hours. Within twenty years, thousands of miles of track crossed Britain.

THE FACTORY

These changes produced the factory systemFAK-tuh-ree SIS-tumA way of making goods in which many workers come to one building and work with power-driven machines under a manager. See the glossary box.. In the old way, work went to the worker's cottage. Now the worker went to the machine. The machine set the pace, a bell marked the hours, and an overseer watched the floor. Owners who raised the money to build the mills and buy the engines were called capitalists, and they took the profit and the risk.

By 1850 Britain made about half the world's iron and a large share of its cloth. Its population had also grown fast, and the towns around the mills and mines drew people from every corner of the island and from Ireland. Ordinary people could buy cheap shirts and cheap pots that their grandparents could not have afforded. But a new kind of life had begun, in which hundreds of families lived in crowded towns and depended on a wage. How did that new life feel to those who lived it?
