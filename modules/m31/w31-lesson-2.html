🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

The soldiers of 1914 expected a short, bold war. They got four years of mud, wire, and machine guns. Today you will follow the war from the trenches of France to the revolution in Russia and the armistice of 1918, and see how it broke four empires.

THE WAR OF MOVEMENT ENDS

The German armies swept through Belgium in August 1914 and came within about thirty miles of Paris. Then the French and British turned them back at the Marne in September, helped by Parisian taxis that carried soldiers to the front. Both sides tried to outflank the other until they reached the sea, and then they dug in. By winter a line of trenches ran almost four hundred miles from the English Channel to Switzerland.

In the east the war moved more freely. At Tannenberg in August 1914, the Germans under Paul von Hindenburg and Erich Ludendorff nearly destroyed a Russian army. Russia's mobilizationmoh-buh-lih-ZAY-shunThe calling up of soldiers and the preparing of an army and its supplies for war. See the glossary box. had been faster than the Germans expected, and it forced them to send troops east. Even after Tannenberg, its vast size and weak railways kept it in the war, and the Austrians suffered heavy defeats at its hands. The Ottoman Empire entered on the German side in late 1914, widening the war to the Middle East.

LIFE AND DEATH IN THE TRENCHES

Imagine a ditch some six feet deep, half full of water, with barbed wire in front of it. Behind it are rows of other trenches, and in front is no man's landNOH manz LANDThe ground between two enemy trench lines, open to fire from both sides. See the glossary box., torn by shells. Machine guns could fire hundreds of rounds a minute, and artillery pounded the lines for days. An attack meant climbing out of the trench and crossing open ground. Defenders with a machine gun and a few men could stop hundreds of attackers. Soldiers lived with rats, lice, mud, and the nearby dead. Some men came to suffer from what was then called shell shock, a breakdown caused by constant bombardment. On Christmas Eve 1914, in a few places, German and British soldiers sang carols across the lines and met in no man's land, but the generals soon forbade it.

At home, governments took control of daily life. Food and fuel were rationed, factories turned out shells instead of goods, and censors and propaganda posters shaped opinion. Women took jobs in factories, offices, and hospitals as men left for the front, and in 1916 Britain began to draft its young men. This was a war that demanded the whole strength of a nation.

Neither side could find a way through. The generals tried longer bombardments, poison gas, which the Germans first used at Ypres in April 1915, and eventually tanks and aircraft. Still, they tended to fall back on attritionuh-TRISH-unA strategy of wearing down an enemy by steady losses until he can no longer fight. See the glossary box., the plan of wearing down the enemy until he broke. In February 1916 the Germans attacked the fortress of Verdun, expecting to bleed France white. The French held, under General Philippe Pétain, and the battle dragged on for ten months. In July 1916 the British attacked along the Somme. On the first day alone, nearly sixty thousand British soldiers were killed or wounded. By November the combined losses on the Somme were over a million, and the line had moved only a few miles.

A WIDENING WAR

Elsewhere the war spread. In 1915 a British and Anzac attempt to knock the Ottomans out of the war at Gallipoli failed after eight months. Its chief sponsor, Winston Churchill, then head of the British navy, lost his post, while a young Ottoman officer named Mustafa Kemal won lasting fame defending the heights. Italy joined the Allies that year, and Bulgaria joined the Central Powers. In 1915 the Ottoman government deported the Armenians of Anatolia, and hundreds of thousands died of massacre, hunger, and exposure. Britain's fleet blockaded Germany, and Germany answered with submarines that sank ships without warning. In May 1915 a U-boat sank the liner Lusitania, and 128 Americans were among the dead. Under American protest, Germany drew back.

1917: THE TURNING POINT

Two events in 1917 changed the war. In January, Germany gambled that its U-boatsOO-bohtFrom the German word Unterseeboot, meaning "undersea boat." It is the name for a German submarine. A secret German telegram, the Zimmermann Telegram, proposing an alliance with Mexico against the United States, was intercepted by the British and published. On April 6, 1917, Congress declared war at the request of President Woodrow Wilson, who said the world must be made safe for democracy.

The second event was in Russia. Defeat, hunger, and the failures of the tsar's government brought the monarchy down in March 1917, and Nicholas II gave up the throne. A weak Provisional Government stayed in the war. In November the BolsheviksBOHL-shuh-vikFrom the Russian word for "majority." In March 1918 they signed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, giving Germany a huge slice of Russian territory. Freed from the east, Germany moved its armies west, and for a few months it seemed it might win.

THE END OF THE WAR

Ludendorff launched a great offensive in the spring of 1918, and German troops came close to Paris again. But they were exhausted, and American soldiers were arriving by the hundreds of thousands, and by the autumn more than a million were in France. From July the Allies, now under the French Marshal Foch, counterattacked with tanks, aircraft, and fresh troops. Germany's allies fell away: Bulgaria, the Ottomans, and Austria-Hungary all asked for terms. At home the Germans were hungry and discouraged. The British blockade had cut off food and fuel, and in the winter of 1916 and 1917 many Germans had lived on turnips. Strikes and protests were growing. Sailors mutinied at Kiel, revolution spread, and on November 9 the Kaiser gave up his throne and fled to the Netherlands. At eleven o'clock in the morning on November 11, 1918, an armisticeAR-mih-stisAn agreement by both sides in a war to stop fighting, usually before a final peace treaty is made. See the glossary box. ended the fighting.

About nine million soldiers had died, and many millions more were wounded, along with huge numbers of civilians. Four old empires, the Russian, the German, the Austro-Hungarian, and the Ottoman, had collapsed. The Europe that Metternich had built at Vienna in 1815 was gone. In the poem you will read this week, Wilfred Owen, who fought in the trenches, describes what he saw. Think about what the war did to the faith so many had put in progress, and what a peace would have to accomplish after so much loss. That is the subject of the next lesson.
