🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

In the summer of 1914, one murder in a Balkan town set off a war that spread across the world within weeks. Today you will see how Europe came to the edge, who made the choices that pushed it over, and where it might have stepped back.

BISMARCK'S EUROPE

After 1871 Bismarck declared that Germany was satisfied. His aim now was peace, because a new war might unite the others against it. He kept France isolated, since France could not forgive the loss of Alsace and Lorraine. He tied Germany to Austria-Hungary and, in 1882, to Italy in the Triple Alliance. And he kept up a secret treaty with Russia, so that Germany would not have to fight on two fronts.

That system depended on the skill of one man. In 1890 the young KaiserKY-zerThe German word for emperor. It comes from the Latin name Caesar. See the glossary box. Wilhelm II, who was impatient and eager for glory, dismissed Bismarck. The treaty with Russia was allowed to lapse. Russia and France, who had little in common but fear of Germany, signed an alliance in 1894. Germany now had enemies on both sides.

THE ARMS RACE

Britain had long stayed out of European alliances, relying on its navy. But Germany now wanted a navy too. From 1898 Admiral Tirpitz pushed laws through the German parliament to build a battle fleet. To Britain, which lived on imported food and trade, a strong German fleet seemed a threat to its survival. In 1906 Britain launched HMS Dreadnought, a battleship so powerful that it made older ships obsolete, and both nations raced to build more dreadnoughtsDRED-nawtA modern battleship with heavy guns of one large size, first built by Britain in 1906. Europe was now divided into two armed camps: Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy on one side, and France, Russia, and Britain on the other. Italy would later change its mind. Crises came and went, as in Morocco in 1905 and 1911, and each one left both sides more suspicious.

TIMETABLES FOR WAR

The general staffs of every great power drew up war plans, and the plans made war more likely. Germany's Schlieffen Plan assumed a war against both France and Russia. It called for knocking out France in about six weeks by marching through neutral Belgium, then turning east to meet the slower Russians. Russia would take weeks to bring its army to the border by mobilizationmoh-buh-lih-ZAY-shunThe calling up of soldiers and the preparing of an army and its supplies for war. See the glossary box., and so the Germans could not wait. France had its own plan to attack in the east. All the plans depended on speed, and each country's mobilization looked like an act of war to its neighbors.

Few expected a long war. Most people thought the fighting would be over by Christmas. Some trusted that trade between nations was too valuable to risk, as the writer Norman Angell argued in 1910. Many young men, raised on stories of glory, were eager. Newspapers in every capital fanned the sense of rivalry, and Kaiser Wilhelm spoke of Germany's right to a place in the sun.

By 1914 every great power on the Continent had universal conscription. Young men served for a time and then stayed in the reserve, so that millions could be called up within days. Germany could put several million trained men into the field, and Russia even more. Large reserves made armies powerful, but they also made delay dangerous, since the first side to mobilize gained an advantage and the other side felt it could not afford to wait.

THE BALKAN POWDER KEG

The spark would come from the Balkans, where the Ottoman Empire was losing ground. Serbia, a small kingdom that dreamed of uniting all the South Slavs, was growing stronger and was backed by Russia. Austria-Hungary, which ruled millions of Slavs, feared that Serbia would pull its empire apart. In 1908 Austria annexed Bosnia, which angered Serbia. The Balkan Wars of 1912 and 1913 almost doubled Serbia's size. Austria's leaders wanted to crush Serbia, but they feared Russia.

SARAJEVO, JUNE 28, 1914

On June 28, 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austrian throne, visited Sarajevo, the capital of Bosnia, with his wife Sophie. A band of young conspirators, helped by Serbian officers in a secret society called the Black Hand, waited along the route. One threw a bomb that bounced off the car. The archduke went on to the town hall. Afterward, he decided to visit the wounded in the hospital, but the driver took a wrong turn and stopped to back up in front of a delicatessen. There stood a nineteen-year-old, Gavrilo Princip. He stepped forward and shot the archduke and his wife. Both died within minutes. Ironically, Franz Ferdinand had often argued against war with Serbia, because he feared that a conflict would destroy the empire he was to inherit.

Think about how narrow this was. The first attempt failed, and the second chance came only by an error. Had the driver turned right, the heir to the throne might have lived, and the summer might have ended quietly.

THE JULY CRISIS

Austria's leaders saw their chance to deal with Serbia. On July 5 and 6 they asked Germany for support, and Kaiser Wilhelm promised it. This promise is often called the blank check. On July 23 Austria sent Serbia an ultimatumul-tuh-MAY-tumA final demand, backed by a threat of force if it is not met by a fixed time. See the glossary box. with demands designed to be hard to accept. Serbia agreed to nearly all of them, but Austria declared war on July 28.

Then the alliances took over. Russia began to mobilize to protect Serbia. Tsar Nicholas II and his cousin Wilhelm exchanged anxious telegrams, which you will read this week, but their generals were already moving. On August 1 Germany declared war on Russia, and on August 3 on France. To carry out its plan, Germany invaded Belgium on August 4. Britain had promised in 1839 to defend Belgium's neutrality, and the next day it declared war on Germany.

Within six weeks of the shooting, Europe was at war. At almost every step, someone could have chosen differently: Germany by restraining Austria, Austria by accepting Serbia's reply, Russia by waiting, Britain by speaking more clearly at the start. In this week's discussion you will weigh which of those choices mattered most.
