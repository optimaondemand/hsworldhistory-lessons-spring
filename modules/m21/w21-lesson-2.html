🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

Last lesson ended with planters who needed workers and were willing to buy them. Today you will follow the trade that supplied them: how it worked, who took part, what the voyage was like, and how a small group of people finally turned the world against it.

A NEW SCALE

Slavery was not new. It existed in ancient Greece and Rome, across Africa, and throughout the Mediterranean and the Islamic world. What the sugar colonies created was different in scale and in kind. In the Atlantic system, slavery was passed down from mother to child and tied to African ancestry. It was backed by law, and it was organized to produce goods for a market. Millions of people were bought to work on plantationsplan-TAY-shunA large farm that grows one cash crop, such as sugar, for sale far away. See the glossary box., usually for life.

AFRICA AND THE FIRST SHIPS

The Portuguese began buying captives on the West African coast in the 1440s, and in 1482 they built a fort at Elmina, in what is now Ghana, to protect the trade. Africans were not passive in what followed. Powerful states such as Asante and Dahomey sold captives to European ships, often prisoners from their own wars. The Europeans mostly stayed near the coast, partly because tropical diseases killed them and partly because African rulers did not allow them inland. Merchants paid in cloth, iron, guns, and shells. The guns made the wars that produced more captives.

Not every ruler agreed. Afonso I, the Christian king of Kongo, wrote to the king of Portugal in 1526. He complained that Portuguese merchants were kidnapping his people, even members of noble families, and emptying his kingdom. The king of Portugal never stopped the trade. Afonso's letter shows that the trade was criticized from the beginning, including by African rulers.

THE TRIANGLE AND ITS NUMBERS

Historians often picture the trade as a triangle. Ships left European ports such as Bristol, Liverpool, Nantes, and Lisbon loaded with trade goods. They sailed to Africa and exchanged those goods for captives. They then crossed the Atlantic to sell the captives in the Americas, and loaded sugar, tobacco, and other crops for the voyage home. In practice, many voyages did not follow that shape. Ships sailed directly between Brazil and Angola, for example.

The numbers are hard to take in. Between the 1500s and the 1860s, about twelve and a half million Africans were loaded onto ships. More than ten million survived the voyage. Most went to Brazil and the Caribbean, where sugar was king. A much smaller share, roughly four hundred thousand, went to the lands that became the United States. The sugar islands were so deadly that planters bought new workers rather than expect the old ones to live long enough to raise families.

THE MIDDLE PASSAGE

The crossing from Africa to the Americas was the middle leg of the triangle, and the name Middle PassageMID-ul PASS-ijThe sea voyage that carried enslaved Africans across the Atlantic to the Americas. See the glossary box. has stuck. It took from five to ten weeks. Captives were chained in the hold, packed so closely that they could hardly turn over. The air was foul and the heat was terrible. Dysentery and smallpox spread quickly. On average about one person in eight died on the way, and sailors died at similar rates. Captains had a financial reason to keep captives alive, but they also packed their ships tightly in order to carry more.

Enslaved people fought back. There were revolts on perhaps one voyage in ten, and some captives refused to eat. Others jumped overboard. In 1781 the captain of a ship called the Zong had about 130 sick captives thrown into the sea so that his owners could claim the loss from their insurers. A court heard the case as a dispute about money. When the story came out in England, many people were horrified. At the end of the voyage, captives were sold at auction in the port, and families were broken up. LIFE ON THE PLANTATION

On a sugar plantation, work began at dawn and ended long after dark. During harvest, workers cut cane and tended the mill for sixteen or eighteen hours a day, since the juice would spoil if it sat. Overseers, called drivers, enforced the pace with whips. Some enslaved people were also skilled craftsmen, cooks, or household servants, but nearly all lived under constant threat of punishment. On the sugar islands deaths outnumbered births, so planters kept buying new workers.

Governments wrote laws to control the system. Barbados passed a slave code in 1661 that later served as a model for other English colonies. In 1685 the king of France issued the Code Noir for his Caribbean colonies. It ordered that enslaved people be baptized and limited some punishments, but it also treated them as property that could be bought, sold, and inherited. Still, the enslaved held on to their languages, music, and religious practices. Some escaped to the mountains and formed communities of their own. In Jamaica these free communities, known as Maroons, fought the British to a standstill and forced them to sign a treaty in 1739.

THE LONG FIGHT TO END IT

Opposition came slowly. The Quakers, a Christian group, began condemning slavery in the 1600s. In the 1700s a growing number of Britons joined them. These abolitionistsab-uh-LISH-un-istA person who works to end slavery. See the glossary box. included Thomas Clarkson, who gathered evidence, William Wilberforce, who fought for a ban in Parliament, and Olaudah Equiano, a formerly enslaved African who wrote about his experience. They printed pamphlets, organized boycotts of sugar, and petitioned Parliament by the thousands. In 1789 Equiano published his life story, and it sold widely and persuaded many readers that the people in the holds of the ships were human beings with families, memories, and hopes like their own. Planters and shipowners fought back, claiming that abolition would ruin Britain's economy. Their persistence was an example of courage that went against the interests of powerful men.

Enslaved people also freed themselves. In 1791 the enslaved people of Saint-Domingue rose against their masters. After years of war, they won, and in 1804 they declared the independent nation of Haiti. In 1807 Britain banned its slave trade, and the United States did the same in 1808. Britain abolished slavery itself in its empire in 1833. Brazil was the last to act, in 1888. Remember what you saw in this module. Sugar made fortunes, and the demand for labor drew millions of Africans across the Atlantic into bondage. Ending the trade took the work of many people over many decades.
