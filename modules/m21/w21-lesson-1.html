🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

After 1492 the two halves of the world traded plants, animals, and diseases that had never met before. Today you will see how that exchange remade the world's diet and population, and how one sweet crop pulled the Atlantic into a new economy.

TWO WORLDS MEET

For many thousands of years, the Americas had developed apart from Europe, Africa, and Asia. They had their own crops, their own animals, and their own diseases. When Columbus crossed the ocean in 1492, he opened a door that would never close again. In 1972 the historian Alfred Crosby gave the result a name: the Columbian Exchangekuh-LUM-bee-un eks-CHAYNJThe movement of plants, animals, people, and diseases between the Americas and the rest of the world after 1492. See the glossary box.. It was not planned by any government. It happened because ships, sailors, and passengers carried living things with them, and some of those living things changed the world.

WHAT CROSSED THE OCEAN

Europeans brought horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, wheat, and grapes to the Americas. Horses changed life on the Great Plains, where peoples learned to ride and hunt on horseback. Cattle and pigs spread across the continent, because the land had room and no natural enemies for them.

In the other direction went crops that fed the world. Maize and potatoes, which grew well in poor soil, spread across Europe, Africa, and Asia. The potato in particular was a cheap source of calories, and it helped feed growing populations across northern Europe. Cassava, a starchy root, became a staple in parts of Africa. Tomatoes, beans, peppers, and cacao came as well, and so did tobacco, which Europeans quickly learned to smoke and to sell.

The most deadly passengers were invisible. Europeans carried smallpox, measles, and influenza. People in the Americas had never met these diseases, and they had no resistance. In the Caribbean the Taíno people, who had greeted Columbus, nearly vanished within two generations. On the mainland the population fell by a huge share over the next century, though historians still argue about the exact numbers. The Spaniards did not understand what was happening, and they did not intend it. Spanish labor demands added to the toll. By the 1500s, whole islands stood empty of the people who had once farmed them.

A NEW WORLD DIET

These changes did not only reach the people who first met them. The potato took a long time to win acceptance in Europe, since many people distrusted a food that grew underground. But it grew well in cold, wet climates, and a small plot could feed a family. In places such as Ireland and northern Germany, populations rose. In China, farmers planted maize and sweet potatoes on hillsides where rice would not grow, and these crops helped the population increase in the 1600s and 1700s.

Other American plants changed habits more than diets. Tobacco, which King James I of England disliked, became a huge business anyway. Chocolate, a drink the Aztecs had prized, became a fashion in Spain and then across Europe. Many of these goods were soon grown far from where they began. Cacao, for example, was planted in Africa and Asia. The world's farms were slowly becoming one.

THE ROAD OF SUGAR

One of the plants that crossed the ocean had a long history already. Sugarcane began in the Pacific and was farmed in India. Arab traders carried it around the Mediterranean, and the Crusaders, whom you met earlier, brought a taste for it home. Sugar was rare and expensive, a luxury for the rich. In the 1400s the Portuguese planted cane on islands in the Atlantic, such as Madeira. On his second voyage in 1493 Columbus brought cane cuttings to the island of Hispaniola.

Sugar was hard to grow and harder to make. It needs heat, rain, and a great deal of land. The cane must be cut at exactly the right time and crushed within a day, or the juice spoils. The juice is then boiled in huge pots over fires that burn day and night. Mills and boiling houses cost a fortune. WHY SUGAR WON

Europeans soon wanted all the sugar they could get. It sweetened the tea and coffee and chocolate that were becoming popular drinks, and it turned from a luxury into an everyday food. Planters, merchants, and governments all saw profit in this, and profit shaped what each of them did. Nobody in this chain needed a grand theory. They needed workers, and plenty of them. Look at how different this is from the Spanish conquests you read about earlier. There, the prize was gold and silver that could be taken. Here, the prize was a crop that had to be planted, cut, crushed, and boiled again and again, year after year. A farm like that runs on labor more than on any other resource.

That was the problem. Native populations had collapsed in the Caribbean. Indentured servants were too few and died too fast, and as English conditions improved, fewer people were willing to volunteer. The planters needed a labor supply that would not run out, and they were willing to buy it. In the next lesson you will see where it came from, and the terrible journey that brought it across the sea.
